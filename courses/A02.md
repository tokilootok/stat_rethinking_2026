## 1. Main thesis

The central argument is:

> **Bayesian inference is a disciplined way to count, compare, update, and propagate possible explanations of data.**

The garden of forking data represents every sequence of observations that could arise under each possible conjecture. Once observations are known, paths inconsistent with those observations are pruned. The paths that remain determine the relative plausibility of the conjectures.

In compressed mathematical form:

$$ \text{posterior plausibility} \propto \text{likelihood} \times \text{prior plausibility} $$

The lecture then extends this logic from inference about a parameter to prediction of new observations:

$$ \text{posterior} \rightarrow \text{sampling distribution} \rightarrow \text{posterior predictive distribution} $$

A02 therefore develops four connected ideas:

1. Bayesian updating is counting possible paths.
2. A probability model compresses this counting.
3. Posterior samples make uncertainty easy to summarize.
4. Prediction requires propagating both parameter uncertainty and observation uncertainty.

## 3. The garden of forking data

The metaphor comes from Jorge Luis Borges' story *The Garden of Forking Paths*.

In an ordinary narrative, the story follows one path. At each decision point, one event occurs and the alternatives disappear from the narrative.

A generative probability model instead retains all paths that could have occurred.

Suppose a model can generate a sequence of binary observations:

$$ D = (d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_N) $$

with each $d_i$ equal to water or land, success or failure, purchase or no purchase.

Before observing the data, the model contains many possible sequences. After observing the actual sequence, only paths consistent with it remain relevant.

The key inferential question becomes:

> Under each conjecture, how many or how much total probability of paths lead to the observed data?

The book begins with a finite bag-of-marbles example. Different conjectures specify different blue-and-white compositions of a four-marble bag. Each composition creates a different garden of possible observation sequences. Observed colors eliminate some conjectures and give different surviving path counts to others.

This finite example is important because it shows that Bayesian updating does not begin as an abstract formula. It begins as logical bookkeeping.

### 3.1 Likelihood as surviving paths

For conjecture $h$, the likelihood is:

$$ p(D \mid h) $$

It measures how readily conjecture $h$ produces the observed data.

A conjecture that cannot produce the observed sequence has:

$$ p(D \mid h)=0 $$

and is eliminated inside the model.

A conjecture with many compatible paths receives more support than one with fewer compatible paths.

### 3.2 Prior information as additional paths

The observed data are not always the only relevant information.

Some conjectures may have been more plausible before the current data because of:

- earlier observations;
- known physical constraints;
- a process model for how parameter values arise;
- previous experiments;
- or deliberate regularization.

This information is encoded by the prior:

$$ p(h) $$

The combined weight is:

$$ p(D \mid h)p(h) $$

Multiplication is compressed counting. The number of ways a conjecture can be present is combined with the number of ways it can produce the observations.

### 3.3 Normalization

The products do not generally sum to one. They are converted into probabilities by dividing by the sum across all conjectures:

$$ p(h \mid D) = \frac{p(D \mid h)p(h)} {\sum_{h'}p(D \mid h')p(h')} $$

For a continuous parameter $p$, the denominator becomes an integral:

$$ p(p \mid D) = \frac{p(D \mid p)p(p)} {\int p(D \mid p)p(p)\,dp} $$

The denominator does not change the relative ranking of parameter values. It standardizes the posterior so that its total probability is one.

## 4. Bayes' theorem as conditioning

The conventional expression is:

$$ p(\theta \mid D,M) = \frac{ p(D \mid \theta,M)p(\theta \mid M) }{ p(D \mid M) } $$

where:

- $M$ denotes the complete model;
- $\theta$ denotes an unknown parameter or collection of parameters;
- $D$ denotes the observations;
- $p(\theta \mid M)$ is the prior;
- $p(D \mid \theta,M)$ is the likelihood;
- $p(\theta \mid D,M)$ is the posterior;
- $p(D \mid M)$ is the average probability of the data under the model.

The denominator is:

$$ p(D \mid M) = \int p(D \mid \theta,M) p(\theta \mid M) \,d\theta $$

For a discrete set of conjectures, the integral is replaced by a sum.

### 4.1 Posterior as conditional plausibility

The posterior does not state what is unconditionally true.

It states what is plausible conditional on:

- the likelihood;
- the prior;
- the observed data;
- the assumed sampling process;
- and all other elements of model $M$.

Therefore:

$$ p(\theta \mid D) $$

is shorthand for:

$$ p(\theta \mid D,M) $$

This suppressed conditioning is easy to forget and is responsible for many overconfident interpretations.

### 4.2 Likelihood is not posterior probability

The likelihood:

$$ p(D \mid \theta) $$

is not the same object as:

$$ p(\theta \mid D) $$

The first asks how compatible the observations are with a parameter value.

The second asks how plausible the parameter value is after combining likelihood and prior information.

Reversing the conditioning reverses the question.

## 5. Building the globe-tossing model

The globe-tossing example asks:

> What proportion of the Earth's surface is water?

Let:

- $p$ be the proportion of the surface covered by water;
- $N$ be the number of tosses;
- $W$ be the number of water observations;
- $L=N-W$ be the number of land observations.

The generative model is:

$$ W \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(N,p) $$

A complete elementary Bayesian model adds a prior:

$$ W \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(N,p) $$

$$ p \sim \operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta) $$

The observed A02 example contains:

$$ W=6 $$

$$ L=3 $$

$$ N=9 $$

### 5.1 Why a binomial distribution?

The binomial model is appropriate inside the small world when:

1. there are $N$ trials;
2. each trial has two possible recorded outcomes;
3. the probability of water is constant at $p$;
4. trials are conditionally independent given $p$;
5. only the number of water observations matters.

The probability of observing exactly $W$ water outcomes in $N$ tosses is:

$$ p(W \mid N,p) = {N \choose W} p^W(1-p)^{N-W} $$

The combinatorial term:

$$ {N \choose W} $$

counts how many distinct orderings contain $W$ water outcomes and $N-W$ land outcomes.

This is another appearance of the garden. The binomial likelihood compresses the enumeration of all compatible binary sequences.

### 5.2 Sufficiency of the count under the model

For a fixed $p$ and conditionally independent tosses, the order of water and land observations does not affect the likelihood.

For example, the sequences:

- `W L W W W L W L W`
- `W W W W W W L L L`

have the same likelihood under the model because both contain six water and three land observations.

This is a model-dependent statement. If order carries information because the process changes through time, the simple binomial model is no longer adequate.

## 6. Prior distributions

The prior assigns plausibility to possible values of $p$ before conditioning on the current observations.

A beta prior has density:

$$ p(p) = \frac{1}{B(\alpha,\beta)} p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\beta-1} $$

for:

$$ 0 \leq p \leq 1 $$

The parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ can be understood as controlling the prior concentration toward water and land.

### 6.1 Uniform prior

The official sequential-updating script begins with:

$$ \alpha=1 $$

$$ \beta=1 $$

so:

$$ p \sim \operatorname{Beta}(1,1) $$

This density is uniform over the interval from zero to one.

A uniform prior does not mean the analysis has no assumptions. It assumes that equal-length intervals of $p$ receive equal prior probability.

### 6.2 Informative and regularizing priors

Different priors encode different initial gardens of forking data.

#### Definitions

- **Informative Prior**: An informative prior encodes explicit, substantive domain knowledge, previous empirical studies, or physical constraints. It concentrates prior probability mass in specific plausible parameter regions (e.g., $p \approx 0.67$), carrying substantial weight that requires strong sample evidence to override.
- **Regularizing Prior (or Weakly Informative Prior)**: A regularizing prior is designed to prevent overfitting and curb extreme parameter estimates driven by sample noise or small sample sizes. Functioning similarly to L1/L2 regularization in Machine Learning (e.g., Lasso/Ridge), it gently shrinks estimates toward conservative baselines (e.g., away from boundary values $0$ or $1$) without dominating the likelihood as sample size $N$ grows.

#### Examples

A mild **regularizing prior** such as:

$$ p \sim \operatorname{Beta}(2,2) $$

places zero probability mass at the absolute boundaries ($p=0$ and $p=1$) and gently pulls estimates toward the center ($0.5$). This prevents overreacting to small-sample fluctuations (for example, inferring $p=1.0$ after observing 3 consecutive water tosses).

A strongly **informative prior** such as:

$$ p \sim \operatorname{Beta}(20,10) $$

expresses substantial prior scientific evidence concentrated near:

$$ \mathbb{E}[p] = \frac{20}{20+10} = \frac{2}{3} $$

representing the equivalent weight of 20 prior water and 10 prior land observations.

Prior choice should be evaluated through its joint implications via prior predictive simulation, rather than relying solely on subjective labels such as “informative” or “noninformative.”

## 7. Beta-binomial updating

The beta distribution is conjugate to the binomial likelihood.

If:

$$ p \sim \operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta) $$

and:

$$ W \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(N,p) $$

then:

$$ p \mid W,N \sim \operatorname{Beta} \left( \alpha+W, \beta+N-W \right) $$

Using $L=N-W$:

$$ p \mid W,L \sim \operatorname{Beta} \left( \alpha+W, \beta+L \right) $$

For the A02 example with a uniform prior:

$$ p \sim \operatorname{Beta}(1,1) $$

and six water plus three land observations:

$$ p \mid W=6,L=3 \sim \operatorname{Beta}(7,4) $$

### 7.1 Posterior mean

For a beta distribution:

$$ \mathbb{E}[p \mid D] = \frac{\alpha+W} {\alpha+\beta+N} $$

Therefore:

$$ \mathbb{E}[p \mid D] = \frac{7}{11} \approx 0.636 $$

### 7.2 Posterior mode

When both posterior shape parameters are greater than one, the mode is:

$$ p_{\mathrm{mode}} = \frac{\alpha+W-1} {\alpha+\beta+N-2} $$

For the example:

$$ p_{\mathrm{mode}} = \frac{6}{9} \approx 0.667 $$

The posterior mean and posterior mode are different summaries. Neither is automatically the correct decision.

## 8. Sequential updating

The official globe-tossing script begins with:

$$ a=1 $$

$$ b=1 $$

After each water observation:

$$ a \leftarrow a+1 $$

After each land observation:

$$ b \leftarrow b+1 $$

The posterior after each toss is:

$$ p \mid D_t \sim \operatorname{Beta}(a_t,b_t) $$

where $D_t$ denotes the observations available after toss $t$.

This animation demonstrates three facts.

### 8.1 The posterior after one observation becomes the prior for the next

Bayesian updating can be performed sequentially:

$$ p(\theta \mid D_1,D_2) \propto p(D_2 \mid \theta) p(\theta \mid D_1) $$

The posterior after $D_1$ becomes the prior before observing $D_2$.

### 8.2 Order does not matter under conditional independence

If observations are conditionally independent given $p$:

$$ p(D \mid p) = \prod_{i=1}^{N}p(d_i \mid p) $$

Multiplication is commutative, so the final posterior depends on the counts, not their order.

### 8.3 Early observations move the posterior more strongly

When little information has accumulated, one observation represents a large fraction of the available evidence.

As the sample grows, a single additional observation has a smaller effect on the posterior.

This does not mean later observations are ignored. It means they are combined with a larger existing information base.

## 9. Infinite possibilities and approximation

The marble example has a finite set of conjectures. The globe parameter $p$ can take infinitely many values between zero and one.

The inferential target is therefore a continuous posterior density:

$$ p(p \mid W,N) $$

The book introduces several engines for approximating posterior distributions:

1. grid approximation;
2. quadratic approximation;
3. Markov chain Monte Carlo.

A02 focuses mainly on the logic of the posterior and on direct simulation. The book develops grid and quadratic approximation more explicitly.

---

## 10. Grid approximation

Grid approximation replaces the continuum of possible parameter values with a finite set:

$$ p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_K $$

For each grid value:

1. evaluate the prior;
2. evaluate the likelihood;
3. multiply them;
4. normalize the resulting weights.

Formally:

$$ q_k = p(W \mid N,p_k)p(p_k) $$

and:

$$ \widetilde{q}_k = \frac{q_k}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}q_j} $$

The normalized values $\widetilde{q}_k$ approximate the posterior probabilities on the grid.

A basic R implementation is:

```r
p_grid <- seq(0, 1, length.out = 1000)
prior <- rep(1, 1000)
likelihood <- dbinom(6, size = 9, prob = p_grid)
posterior <- likelihood * prior
posterior <- posterior / sum(posterior)
```

### 10.1 Why grid approximation matters pedagogically

Grid approximation is inefficient in high dimensions, but it makes every operation visible.

It shows explicitly that the posterior is obtained by:

$$ \text{prior} \times \text{likelihood} \rightarrow \text{unnormalized posterior} \rightarrow \text{normalized posterior} $$

### 10.2 Why grid approximation does not scale

If each of $d$ parameters is represented by $K$ grid points, the total number of combinations is:

$$ K^d $$

With $K=100$ and $d=5$:

$$ 100^5 = 10^{10} $$

This curse of dimensionality makes grid approximation impractical for most realistic models.

## 11. Quadratic approximation

Chapter 2 also introduces quadratic approximation.

The basic strategy is:

1. locate the posterior mode;
2. estimate the curvature around that mode;
3. approximate the posterior with a Gaussian distribution.

Symbolically:

$$ p(\theta \mid D) \approx \operatorname{Normal} \left( \widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{mode}}, \widehat{\Sigma} \right) $$

The approximation works best when the posterior is approximately symmetric and bell-shaped.

It can perform poorly when the posterior is:

- strongly skewed;
- bounded near zero or one;
- multimodal;
- weakly identified;
- or based on little information.

For the nine-toss globe example, the posterior is not perfectly Gaussian. The book shows that the approximation improves as the amount of data increases while the observed proportion remains similar.

### 11.1 The fitting engine is part of practical model behavior

Two analysts can write the same likelihood and priors but obtain different practical results if their numerical approximations fail differently.

Therefore, the complete analytical machine includes:

- the likelihood;
- the priors;
- the data;
- the parameterization;
- and the computational engine.

## 12. Sampling from the posterior

Chapter 3 replaces direct manipulation of posterior densities with samples.

Suppose we have grid values $p_k$ and normalized posterior probabilities $\widetilde{q}_k$. We can draw:

$$ p^{(s)} \sim p(p \mid D) $$

for:

$$ s=1,2,\ldots,S $$

Using grid approximation in R:

```r
samples <- sample(
  p_grid,
  prob = posterior,
  size = 10000,
  replace = TRUE
)
```

Values with high posterior probability appear frequently in the sample. Values with low posterior probability appear rarely.

The empirical distribution of the samples approximates the posterior distribution.

### 12.1 Why posterior samples are useful

Once samples are available, many posterior questions become simple operations.

For example:

$$ \Pr(p<0.5 \mid D) \approx \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^{S} \mathbb{I} \left[p^{(s)}<0.5\right] $$

The posterior mean is approximated by:

$$ \mathbb{E}[p \mid D] \approx \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^{S}p^{(s)} $$

A posterior quantile is obtained from the corresponding empirical quantile of the samples.

Posterior sampling therefore turns integration into averaging.

## 13. Summarizing posterior uncertainty

A posterior distribution is the main inferential object. Any summary removes information.

Useful summaries answer specific questions.

### 13.1 Posterior probability of an interval

The posterior probability that $p$ lies between $a$ and $b$ is:

$$ \Pr(a < p < b \mid D) $$

Using samples:

$$ \Pr(a < p < b \mid D) \approx \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^{S} \mathbb{I} \left[a<p^{(s)}<b\right] $$

This is a direct probability statement about the parameter inside the model.

### 13.2 Percentile intervals

A central interval with posterior mass $q$ removes equal posterior mass from both tails.

For a central 89 percent interval, the lower and upper quantiles are approximately:

$$ 0.055 $$

and:

$$ 0.945 $$

The interval is a summary of posterior mass. It is not a repeated-sampling confidence interval.

### 13.3 Highest posterior density intervals

A highest posterior density interval contains the specified posterior mass while favoring regions of greater posterior density.

For a unimodal posterior, it is often shorter than the equal-tail percentile interval.

However, the result can be sensitive to parameterization, and a single interval can hide multimodality.

The book's broader lesson is not that one interval is universally best. It is that intervals should be chosen to answer a substantive question.

### 13.4 Point estimates require a loss function

A posterior does not automatically nominate one best number.

A decision $d$ can be evaluated with a loss function:

$$ L(d,p) $$

The expected posterior loss is:

$$ \mathbb{E} \left[ L(d,p) \mid D \right] = \int L(d,p) p(p \mid D) \,dp $$

The optimal decision minimizes expected loss:

$$ d^* = \arg\min_d \mathbb{E} \left[ L(d,p) \mid D \right] $$

Under absolute-error loss:

$$ L(d,p)=|d-p| $$

the optimal point is the posterior median.

Under squared-error loss:

$$ L(d,p)=(d-p)^2 $$

the optimal point is the posterior mean.

The correct point estimate therefore depends on the consequences of error, not only on the shape of the posterior.

## 14. From posterior inference to prediction

Inference about $p$ is not the same as prediction of future observations.

The posterior answers questions about the parameter:

$$ p(p \mid D) $$

The posterior predictive distribution answers questions about new data:

$$ p(\widetilde{W} \mid D) $$

where $\widetilde{W}$ denotes a future count of water observations.

The posterior predictive distribution is:

$$ p(\widetilde{W} \mid D) = \int p(\widetilde{W} \mid p) p(p \mid D) \,dp $$

This expression averages the sampling distribution over posterior uncertainty in $p$.

### 14.1 Two distinct sources of predictive uncertainty

Prediction contains at least two sources of uncertainty.

#### Parameter uncertainty

We do not know the exact value of $p$.

This uncertainty is represented by:

$$ p(p \mid D) $$

#### Observation uncertainty

Even if $p$ were known exactly, future binary outcomes would remain random unless:

$$ p=0 $$

or:

$$ p=1 $$

For a known $p$:

$$ \widetilde{W} \sim \operatorname{Binomial} (\widetilde{N},p) $$

The posterior predictive distribution propagates both types of uncertainty.

## 15. The official predictive simulation

The A02 predictive script uses:

$$ W=6 $$

$$ L=3 $$

and a uniform beta prior, producing:

$$ p \mid D \sim \operatorname{Beta}(7,4) $$

For each simulation $s$:

1. draw a possible value of $p$ from the posterior;
2. draw a possible future count from a binomial distribution conditional on that $p$.

Formally:

$$ p^{(s)} \sim \operatorname{Beta}(7,4) $$

$$ \widetilde{W}^{(s)} \sim \operatorname{Binomial} (9,p^{(s)}) $$

Repeated draws approximate:

$$ p(\widetilde{W} \mid D) $$

A compact R version is:

```r
p_sim <- rbeta(10000, 7, 4)
W_sim <- rbinom(10000, size = 9, prob = p_sim)
```

The official animation separates the process into three panels:

1. the posterior distribution of $p$;
2. the conditional binomial distribution for one selected value of $p$;
3. the accumulated posterior predictive distribution.

This makes visible the integral:

$$ p(\widetilde{W} \mid D) = \int p(\widetilde{W} \mid p) p(p \mid D) \,dp $$

## 16. Prior predictive and posterior predictive simulation

The same simulation structure can be run before seeing data.

### 16.1 Prior predictive distribution

The prior predictive distribution is:

$$ p(\widetilde{W}) = \int p(\widetilde{W} \mid p) p(p) \,dp $$

Simulation proceeds as:

$$ p^{(s)} \sim p(p) $$

$$ \widetilde{W}^{(s)} \sim p(\widetilde{W} \mid p^{(s)}) $$

Prior predictive simulation asks:

> What observations does the model consider plausible before seeing the current data?

It is a way to inspect the joint implications of the likelihood and prior.

### 16.2 Posterior predictive distribution

The posterior predictive distribution is:

$$ p(\widetilde{W} \mid D) = \int p(\widetilde{W} \mid p) p(p \mid D) \,dp $$

It asks:

> What observations does the fitted model consider plausible after conditioning on the data?

### 16.3 Prediction is not validation by itself

A posterior predictive distribution can reveal discrepancies between model expectations and observations, but it cannot prove that the model is correct.

A flexible but scientifically misleading model may predict existing observations well.

Predictive checking must therefore be combined with:

- causal criticism;
- measurement criticism;
- sensitivity analysis;
- external validation;
- and alternative generative models.

## 17. What the garden teaches about model checking

The garden metaphor is not only about posterior calculation.

Every model defines a set of possible data paths. A model check asks whether the observed data resemble the paths generated by the model in scientifically relevant ways.

### 17.1 Notation and component definitions

To formalize posterior predictive checking, we define five key mathematical elements:

- **$D$ — Observed data:**  
  The actual, empirical dataset collected from the real world (for example, the observed sequence of $N$ globe tosses, such as $W=6$ and $L=3$). In model checking, $D$ is fixed and serves as the reference benchmark.

- **$\theta$ — Parameter vector:**  
  The vector of unobserved parameters (or scalar parameter, e.g., the true proportion of water $p$) that governs the data-generating likelihood $p(D \mid \theta)$.

- **$\theta^{(s)}$ — Posterior parameter draw:**  
  The $s$-th parameter value sampled from the posterior distribution $p(\theta \mid D)$, for Monte Carlo index $s = 1, 2, \ldots, S$. Each $\theta^{(s)}$ represents a single plausible state of nature under posterior uncertainty.

- **$D_{\mathrm{rep}}^{(s)}$ — Replicated dataset:**  
  A synthetic dataset simulated from the likelihood conditional on the $s$-th posterior draw:
$$ D_{\mathrm{rep}}^{(s)} \sim p(D_{\mathrm{rep}} \mid \theta^{(s)}) $$
  It possesses the exact same sample size, structure, and experimental conditions as the original observed dataset $D$.

- **$T(D)$ — Diagnostic test statistic / feature function:**  
  A scalar- or vector-valued summary function $T(\cdot)$ chosen by the analyst to measure a specific feature or discrepancy of data (e.g., total success count, maximum run length, proportion of switches, or sample variance).
  - $T(D)$ denotes the diagnostic statistic evaluated on the actual observed data $D$.
  - $T\left(D_{\mathrm{rep}}^{(s)}\right)$ denotes the diagnostic statistic evaluated on the $s$-th replicated dataset $D_{\mathrm{rep}}^{(s)}$.

### 17.2 Generic posterior predictive checking procedure

A generic posterior predictive check proceeds in four steps:

1. **Draw parameter values** from the posterior distribution:
$$ \theta^{(s)} \sim p(\theta \mid D), \quad s=1,\ldots,S $$
2. **Simulate replicated data** under each drawn parameter value:
$$ D_{\mathrm{rep}}^{(s)} \sim p(D_{\mathrm{rep}} \mid \theta^{(s)}) $$
3. **Compute the diagnostic feature** $T(\cdot)$ on each replicate to form an empirical distribution $\{T\left(D_{\mathrm{rep}}^{(s)}\right)\}_{s=1}^S$.
4. **Compare the observed statistic** $T(D)$ with the distribution of replicated statistics $T\left(D_{\mathrm{rep}}^{(s)}\right)$.

The choice of $T$ should target model weaknesses that matter scientifically.

For globe tossing, possible checks include:

- the total number of water observations;
- long runs of water or land;
- changes in water frequency through time;
- dependence on the previous toss;
- dependence on globe orientation;
- or geographic clustering.

The simple binomial model uses only the total count. Sequence-based checks can reveal structure that the likelihood discards.

## 18. Small-world guarantees and large-world limits

Bayesian conditioning provides a coherent posterior inside the model.

If the likelihood, prior, and observations are specified, probability theory determines the posterior.

This is a small-world guarantee.

It does not establish that:

- tosses are independent;
- the water probability is constant;
- the physical tossing procedure samples the globe uniformly;
- water and land are measured without error;
- or the globe is an adequate representation of the target planet.

Those are large-world questions.

The posterior can therefore be exactly right for the wrong model.

### 18.1 A concrete sampling problem

The official updating script samples latitude uniformly between approximately minus 70 and 70 degrees and longitude uniformly between minus 180 and 180 degrees.

Uniform latitude and longitude do not produce uniform sampling over spherical surface area because equal latitude intervals cover different areas near the equator and the poles.

This illustrates a valuable lesson: even simulation code that looks like a random globe toss can encode a nonuniform sampling process.

The inferential answer is always conditional on the actual data-generating and sampling procedure, not merely on the intended verbal description.

## 19. Common errors exposed by A02

### Error 1: Interpreting the likelihood as a probability of the parameter

$$ p(D \mid \theta) $$

is not:

$$ p(\theta \mid D) $$

### Error 2: Treating a uniform prior as assumption-free

A uniform prior is a modeling choice and is not invariant to reparameterization.

### Error 3: Reporting only the posterior mode

The mode discards uncertainty and may not minimize the relevant decision loss.

### Error 4: Confusing parameter uncertainty with observation uncertainty

Knowing $p$ exactly would not make future tosses deterministic.

### Error 5: Predicting with a single parameter estimate

Using only:

$$ \widehat{p} $$

in a binomial sampling distribution ignores posterior uncertainty in $p$ and can produce overconfident predictions.

### Error 6: Treating a credible interval as a confidence interval

A posterior interval is conditional on the model and data. Its interpretation is not based on hypothetical repeated confidence procedures.

### Error 7: Assuming the count contains all relevant information in reality

The count is sufficient only under the binomial model. Temporal order or spatial dependence may matter in the large world.

### Error 8: Assuming a good posterior predictive fit proves the causal model

Prediction can be compatible with multiple causal processes.

### Error 9: Forgetting normalization

Likelihood multiplied by prior gives relative posterior weights. Those weights must be normalized to define a probability distribution.

### Error 10: Confusing Monte Carlo error with posterior uncertainty

Using more posterior draws reduces simulation error in numerical summaries. It does not reduce the underlying uncertainty represented by the posterior.

## 20. Application to CRM conversion rates

Suppose a CRM team sends an offer to $N$ customers and observes $Y$ purchases.

A simple model is:

$$ Y \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(N,p) $$

where $p$ is the purchase probability for customers generated under the same process.

With:

$$ p \sim \operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta) $$

the posterior is:

$$ p \mid Y,N \sim \operatorname{Beta} \left( \alpha+Y, \beta+N-Y \right) $$

### 20.1 Posterior question

A useful posterior question is:

$$ \Pr(p>p_0 \mid D) $$

where $p_0$ is the minimum conversion rate required for campaign profitability.

### 20.2 Posterior predictive question

Suppose the next campaign will target $N_{\mathrm{new}}$ comparable customers.

The predictive quantity is:

$$ Y_{\mathrm{new}} \sim \operatorname{Binomial} (N_{\mathrm{new}},p) $$

averaged over:

$$ p(p \mid D) $$

Therefore:

$$ p(Y_{\mathrm{new}} \mid D) = \int p(Y_{\mathrm{new}} \mid p) p(p \mid D) \,dp $$

This distribution is more useful for campaign planning than a single estimated conversion rate because it provides probabilities of operational outcomes such as:

$$ \Pr \left( Y_{\mathrm{new}} \geq y_{\mathrm{break-even}} \mid D \right) $$

### 20.3 Why the elementary binomial model may fail in CRM

CRM customers are usually heterogeneous.

Purchase probability can vary with:

- customer tenure;
- price sensitivity;
- product affinity;
- channel;
- season;
- campaign fatigue;
- previous purchases;
- and treatment assignment policy.

The elementary model assumes one common $p$. It may therefore underestimate variation across campaigns or customer segments.

A richer generative model might use customer-specific probabilities:

$$ Y_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_i) $$

with:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_i) = \alpha + \beta_A A_i + \beta_X X_i + u_i $$

A02 supplies the fundamental update-and-predict logic. Later lectures provide the regression, causal, and multilevel structures needed for realistic CRM data.

### Question 1: In what sense is multiplication in Bayes' theorem compressed counting?

**Answer:**
Bayesian updating originates from counting possible paths through the garden of forking data. Each candidate parameter value $\theta$ represents a conjecture about the underlying state of nature.
- The **prior** $p(\theta)$ counts (or weights) how many ways that conjecture itself could have arisen from prior information or assumptions.
- The **likelihood** $p(D \mid \theta)$ counts how many paths of observation are consistent with data $D$ given conjecture $\theta$.

Multiplying prior paths by likelihood paths computes the total number of surviving paths consistent with both prior assumptions and current observations:
$$ \text{Total Surviving Paths}(\theta) = p(D \mid \theta) \times p(\theta) $$
Multiplication compresses the step-by-step tree enumeration into a product. Normalization then divides each conjecture's path count by the total sum of all surviving paths across all conjectures, converting raw path counts into standardized posterior probabilities.

### Question 2: Why is $p(D \mid \theta)$ not the same as $p(\theta \mid D)$?

**Answer:**
- $p(D \mid \theta)$ is the **likelihood**: the probability density of observing data $D$ assuming parameter value $\theta$ is fixed.
- $p(\theta \mid D)$ is the **posterior**: the probability density of parameter value $\theta$ given observed data $D$.

Confusing these two is known as the *transposed conditional fallacy* (or prosecutor's fallacy). For instance, $\Pr(\text{Positive Medical Test} \mid \text{Rare Disease})$ may be $99\%$, but $\Pr(\text{Rare Disease} \mid \text{Positive Medical Test})$ might be less than $5\%$ if the disease is extremely rare. Bayes' theorem links the two by scaling the likelihood by the prior ratio:
$$ p(\theta \mid D) = p(D \mid \theta) \cdot \frac{p(\theta)}{p(D)} $$

### Question 3: What assumptions justify the binomial likelihood in the globe-tossing example?

**Answer:**
1. **Binary outcomes:** Each toss results in exactly one of two mutually exclusive categories (Water $W$ or Land $L$).
2. **Constant probability ($p$):** Every location on the globe has the exact same probability $p$ of being sampled (uniform surface sampling, constant true water proportion).
3. **Independent trials:** Conditional on $p$, the result of any toss carries no information about the result of any other toss (no temporal dependence, no memory, no spatial clustering bias).

---

### Question 4: Why does the order of water and land observations not affect the posterior under this model?

**Answer:**
Under the assumption of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Bernoulli trials, the probability of observing any specific sequence of $W$ waters and $L$ lands (where $N = W + L$) is:
$$ \Pr(\text{sequence} \mid p) = p^W (1-p)^L $$
Because real-number multiplication is commutative, the sequence order does not affect the likelihood product $p^W (1-p)^L$. The binomial coefficient $\binom{N}{W}$ counts the number of such permutations, but since it is a constant scaling factor across all candidate $p$ values, it cancels out upon normalization. Thus, the total count $W$ is a **sufficient statistic** for $p$.

### Question 5: Starting from $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$, what posterior follows from six water and three land observations?

**Answer:**
Using Beta-Binomial conjugacy:
- Prior: $p \sim \operatorname{Beta}(\alpha_0, \beta_0) = \operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$ (Uniform prior).
- Data: $W = 6$, $L = 3$ ($N = 9$).
- Posterior: 
$$ \alpha_{\mathrm{post}} = \alpha_0 + W = 1 + 6 = 7 $$
$$ \beta_{\mathrm{post}} = \beta_0 + L = 1 + 3 = 4 $$
  So the posterior distribution is $\operatorname{Beta}(7,4)$.

### Question 6: Why are the posterior mean and posterior mode different in the globe example?

**Answer:**
The posterior distribution $\operatorname{Beta}(7,4)$ is asymmetric (negatively skewed toward higher values of $p$).
- **Mode:** The point of maximum probability density ($\operatorname{Mode} = \frac{7-1}{7+4-2} = \frac{6}{9} \approx 0.667$).
- **Mean:** The center of mass / expected value ($\mathbb{E}[p \mid D] = \frac{7}{7+4} = \frac{7}{11} \approx 0.636$).
- **Median:** The 50th percentile ($\approx 0.652$).

For any skewed probability distribution, mean, median, and mode do not coincide because the long tail pulls the mean away from the peak density.

### Question 7: How does sequential updating relate to batch updating?

**Answer:**
Sequential updating and batch updating yield the **exact same final posterior distribution** whenever observations are conditionally independent given the parameters.
- **Batch updating:** Condition on all data $D = (d_1, \dots, d_N)$ simultaneously using prior $p(\theta)$.
- **Sequential updating:** Condition on $d_1$ to obtain posterior $p(\theta \mid d_1)$, then use $p(\theta \mid d_1)$ as the prior to condition on $d_2$, and so on.

Mathematically:
$$ p(\theta \mid d_1, d_2) \propto p(d_2 \mid \theta) \left[ p(d_1 \mid \theta) p(\theta) \right] = p(d_1, d_2 \mid \theta) p(\theta) $$

### Question 8: Why does grid approximation fail in high-dimensional models?

**Answer:**
Grid approximation suffers from the **curse of dimensionality**. If we evaluate $K$ grid points per parameter dimension, a model with $M$ parameters requires $K^M$ total grid evaluations.
- $M = 1$ parameter, $K = 100 \implies 100^1 = 100$ points.
- $M = 5$ parameters, $K = 100 \implies 100^5 = 10^{10}$ points (10 billion).
- $M = 10$ parameters, $K = 100 \implies 100^{10} = 10^{20}$ points (uncomputable).

This exponential explosion renders grid search completely infeasible for multi-parameter models, necessitating MCMC sampling algorithms (like HMC/NUTS) or Laplace/quadratic approximations.

### Question 9: What information is lost when the posterior is reduced to one point estimate?

**Answer:**
Reducing a posterior distribution $p(\theta \mid D)$ to a single point estimate (such as the mean, median, or MAP mode) discards:
1. **Uncertainty & Spread:** The variance, standard deviation, and plausible range of parameter values.
2. **Skewness & Asymmetry:** Information regarding tail risks and asymmetric plausibility.
3. **Multimodality:** Secondary peaks representing alternative competing hypotheses.
4. **Covariance:** Joint dependence between parameters in multi-parameter models.

This loss of information causes downstream decision-making and forecasting to become overconfident and fragile.

### Question 10: Why does choosing a point estimate require a loss function?

**Answer:**
A posterior distribution describes how plausible different parameter values are; it does not automatically select one best decision number. Choosing a single point estimate $d$ requires defining a **loss function** $L(d, \theta)$ that specifies the economic, physical, or operational cost of choosing decision $d$ when the true parameter value is $\theta$.

The optimal decision $d^*$ minimizes the **expected posterior loss**:
$$ d^* = \arg\min_d \mathbb{E}[L(d, \theta) \mid D] = \arg\min_d \int L(d, \theta) p(\theta \mid D) \, d\theta $$
- **Absolute loss ($L = |d - \theta|$):** Optimal decision is the **posterior median**.
- **Squared loss ($L = (d - \theta)^2$):** Optimal decision is the **posterior mean**.
- **0-1 loss ($L = 0$ if $d=\theta$ else $1$):** Optimal decision is the **posterior mode**.

### Question 11: What are the two main sources of uncertainty in posterior prediction?

**Answer:**
1. **Parameter uncertainty:** We do not know the exact parameter value $\theta$; this uncertainty is quantified by the posterior distribution $p(\theta \mid D)$.
2. **Observation uncertainty (Sampling variation):** Even if $\theta$ were known with $100\%$ certainty, future observation outcomes $\widetilde{Y}$ remain inherently random, governed by the data-generating process $p(\widetilde{Y} \mid \theta)$.

The posterior predictive distribution integrates over both sources of uncertainty:
$$ p(\widetilde{Y} \mid D) = \int p(\widetilde{Y} \mid \theta) \, p(\theta \mid D) \, d\theta $$

### Question 12: How does a prior predictive distribution differ from a posterior predictive distribution?

**Answer:**
- **Prior Predictive Distribution:** Simulates future data $\widetilde{Y}$ before observing current data $D$, propagating uncertainty from the **prior** $p(\theta)$:
$$ p(\widetilde{Y}) = \int p(\widetilde{Y} \mid \theta) \, p(\theta) \, d\theta $$
  It answers: *"What observations does the model deem plausible based purely on domain knowledge and initial assumptions?"*
- **Posterior Predictive Distribution:** Simulates future data $\widetilde{Y}$ after observing current data $D$, propagating uncertainty from the **posterior** $p(\theta \mid D)$:
$$ p(\widetilde{Y} \mid D) = \int p(\widetilde{Y} \mid \theta) \, p(\theta \mid D) \, d\theta $$
  It answers: *"What observations does the fitted model expect to see in future trials?"*

### Question 13: Why can a model predict observed data well while remaining causally wrong?

**Answer:**
Predictive accuracy relies on **correlation and statistical association**, whereas causal inference requires modeling the **true data-generating physical mechanism**.
A statistically flexible model can capture surface correlations (or confounding paths) to accurately predict $Y$ from $X$ under passive observation. However, if $X$ does not causally produce $Y$ (e.g., $X$ and $Y$ are co-confounded by an unobserved variable $Z$), intervening on $X$ ($\operatorname{do}(X=x)$) will fail to change $Y$. A model can be predictively accurate in the small world of passive data while being completely wrong under active intervention.

### Question 14: What sequence-based pattern could reveal failure of a binomial model even when the total count appears plausible?

**Answer:**
The binomial model assumes exchangeable, independent trials with a constant probability $p$. It compresses the data into a single summary count $W$.
Sequence-based patterns that violate these assumptions include:
1. **Autocorrelation / Persistence:** Runs of identical outcomes (e.g., long sequences of $W$ followed by long sequences of $L$), indicating state dependence or spatial clustering.
2. **Alternating patterns:** $W, L, W, L, W, L$, indicating negative autocorrelation or repulsive dynamics.
3. **Temporal Trend / Drift:** Water frequency increasing or decreasing systematically over the course of the experiment.

Diagnostic test statistics $T(D)$ measuring run length or lag-1 autocorrelation will easily flag these failures even if the total count $W$ matches binomial expectations.

### Question 15: Why is a posterior predictive CRM conversion distribution more useful than a single conversion-rate estimate?

**Answer:**
A single point estimate of conversion rate (e.g., $\widehat{p} = 4\%$) produces a single point forecast of revenue, obscuring operational risk.
In contrast, a full posterior predictive distribution of conversions $p(\widetilde{Y}_{\mathrm{CRM}} \mid D)$ for a campaign of size $N_{\mathrm{new}}$ provides:
1. **Risk of Financial Loss:** $\Pr(\text{Revenue} < \text{Campaign Cost})$.
2. **Break-even Probabilities:** $\Pr(\widetilde{Y}_{\mathrm{CRM}} \ge Y_{\mathrm{target}})$.
3. **Capacity & Resource Planning:** Tail quantiles (e.g., 95th percentile) for server infrastructure or customer support demand.

This allows decision-makers to optimize strategies using expected utility rather than naive point forecasts.

### Question 16: How does $p(Y_{\mathrm{new}} \mid D)$ differ from $p(Y \mid \operatorname{do}(A=a),D)$?

**Answer:**
- $p(Y_{\mathrm{new}} \mid D)$ is the **observational posterior predictive distribution**: It forecasts future outcomes $Y_{\mathrm{new}}$ under passive observation of the ongoing system.
- $p(Y \mid \operatorname{do}(A=a),D)$ is the **interventional distribution**: It predicts the outcome $Y$ when treatment variable $A$ is actively set to value $a$ by external action, severing all incoming causal arrows into $A$ in the Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG).

Unless there is no confounding between $A$ and $Y$, the observational prediction $p(Y_{\mathrm{new}} \mid D)$ will not equal the interventional prediction $p(Y \mid \operatorname{do}(A=a),D)$.

### 1. Mathematical Construction of Posterior Distribution

#### Model Specification
- **Parameter:** $p \in [0,1]$, the proportion of Earth's surface covered by water.
- **Prior:** Uniform distribution over $[0,1]$, equivalent to $p \sim \operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$.
- **Likelihood:** $W \mid p, N=14 \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(14, p)$.

#### Conjugate Analytical Derivation
By Bayes' theorem and Beta-Binomial conjugacy:
$$ p(p \mid W=3, L=11) \propto p(W=3 \mid p, N=14) \cdot p(p) $$
$$ p(p \mid D) \propto \left[ \binom{14}{3} p^3 (1-p)^{11} \right] \cdot \left[ 1 \right] \propto p^{4-1} (1-p)^{12-1} $$

Thus, the exact analytical posterior distribution is:
$$ p \mid D \sim \operatorname{Beta}(\alpha_{\mathrm{post}}, \beta_{\mathrm{post}}) = \operatorname{Beta}(4, 12) $$

#### Key Posterior Summaries
- **Posterior Mean:**
$$ \mathbb{E}[p \mid D] = \frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{post}}}{\alpha_{\mathrm{post}} + \beta_{\mathrm{post}}} = \frac{4}{4 + 12} = \frac{4}{16} = 0.2500 $$
- **Posterior Mode (MAP):**
$$ \operatorname{Mode}(p \mid D) = \frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{post}} - 1}{\alpha_{\mathrm{post}} + \beta_{\mathrm{post}} - 2} = \frac{3}{14} \approx 0.2143 $$
- **Posterior Variance:**
$$ \operatorname{Var}(p \mid D) = \frac{\alpha \beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)} = \frac{4 \times 12}{16^2 \times 17} = \frac{48}{4352} \approx 0.01103 \implies \text{SD} \approx 0.1050 $$
