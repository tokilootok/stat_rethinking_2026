## 1. Main thesis

The central argument is:

> **Linear regression is a powerful descriptive approximation, not a literal theory of how the world works.**

McElreath compares linear regression with Ptolemaic geocentric astronomy. The geocentric model is wrong as a physical account of the solar system, yet suitably calibrated epicycles can predict apparent planetary motion rather well.

Linear regression has the same dual character:

- it can summarize patterns;
- interpolate and predict;
- approximate complicated relationships;
- but its parameters do not automatically reveal causal mechanisms.

The workflow is:

$$
\text{generative assumptions}
\rightarrow
\text{Gaussian observation model}
\rightarrow
\text{linear model for the mean}
\rightarrow
\text{priors}
\rightarrow
\text{prior predictive simulation}
\rightarrow
\text{posterior approximation}
\rightarrow
\text{prediction and criticism}
$$

A03 moves from the one-parameter globe model to regression with several jointly uncertain parameters.

## 3. Why “geocentric models”?

The official Ptolemaic script animates:

1. an Earth-centered deferent-and-epicycle model;
2. a Copernican Earth–Mars model that generates apparent retrograde motion.

The comparison separates description from explanation.

A geocentric model can reproduce the apparent path of Mars. A heliocentric model gives a more useful account of why that path appears.

The statistical lesson is:

$$
\text{predictive success}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{causal truth}
$$

A regression model may be excellent for interpolation or forecasting within a narrow domain while remaining poor for intervention, extrapolation, or mechanistic explanation.

This does not make regression useless. It defines how its output should be interpreted.

## 4. Least squares and the Gaussian likelihood

Suppose:

$$
y_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma)
$$

with:

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha+\beta x_i
$$

The likelihood is:

$$
p(y \mid \alpha,\beta,\sigma)
=
\prod_{i=1}^{N}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}
\exp
\left[
-
\frac{(y_i-\alpha-\beta x_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}
\right]
$$

Its log is:

$$
\log p(y \mid \alpha,\beta,\sigma)
=
-N\log \sigma
-
\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
(y_i-\alpha-\beta x_i)^2
+
\text{constant}
$$

For fixed $\sigma$, maximizing the likelihood is equivalent to minimizing:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
(y_i-\alpha-\beta x_i)^2
$$

This is the ordinary least-squares objective.

Bayesian regression adds priors and produces a joint posterior:

$$
p(\alpha,\beta,\sigma \mid y,x)
\propto
p(y \mid \alpha,\beta,\sigma,x)
\,p(\alpha,\beta,\sigma)
$$

The result is a distribution over plausible lines and residual scales, not merely one best-fitting line.

## 5. Why Gaussian distributions are common

Section 4.1 and `03_gaussian_generative_sim.r` motivate the Gaussian distribution through aggregation.

### 5.1 Football-field random walk

Many people begin at the center of a field. At each step, a coin toss moves each person left or right.

For person $j$ after $T$ steps:

$$
X_j
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}S_{jt}
$$

where:

$$
S_{jt}
\in
\{-1,+1\}
$$

Central positions can arise through many sequences. Extreme positions require unusual sequences dominated by movement in one direction. The resulting distribution becomes approximately Gaussian.

### 5.2 Accumulated growth

The script also represents growth as accumulated increments:

$$
X
=
X_0
+
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta_t
$$

When many small influences accumulate and no single one dominates, the aggregate often approaches a Gaussian form after centering and scaling.

This explains how adult height or weight can be approximately Gaussian without any biological mechanism literally sampling from a normal distribution.

### 5.3 Gaussian is not universal

Some processes produce skewness, heavy tails, bounds, multiple modes, or discrete outcomes. Gaussian tails are thin and can underestimate extreme events.

The Gaussian should be used because its implications are reasonable for the task, not because every continuous outcome is inherently normal.

## 6. The Gaussian observation model

A Gaussian variable is written:

$$
Y
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma)
$$

with density:

$$
p(y \mid \mu,\sigma)
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}
\exp
\left[
-
\frac{(y-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}
\right]
$$

The parameters have distinct roles:

$$
\mathbb{E}[Y]
=
\mu
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var}(Y)
=
\sigma^2
$$

When predictors enter the model, the relevant claim is conditional:

$$
Y_i
\mid
X_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma)
$$

A raw outcome histogram need not be perfectly Gaussian. The model concerns residual variation around the conditional expectation.

---

## 7. Gaussian model without a predictor

Chapter 4 first models adult height with one mean and standard deviation:

$$
h_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu,\sigma)
$$

with priors such as:

$$
\mu
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(178,20)
$$

$$
\sigma
\sim
\operatorname{Uniform}(0,50)
$$

The posterior is:

$$
p(\mu,\sigma \mid h)
\propto
\left[
\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(h_i \mid \mu,\sigma)
\right]
p(\mu)p(\sigma)
$$

This is already a multivariate posterior. Even if the priors factorize, the data may induce posterior dependence:

$$
p(\mu,\sigma \mid h)
\neq
p(\mu \mid h)p(\sigma \mid h)
$$

Regression adds further parameters and richer posterior geometry.

---

## 8. Linear prediction

The generic regression model is:

$$
y_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma)
$$

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha+\beta x_i
$$

The first line is the observation model. The second is the model for the expected outcome.

In the uncentered form:

- $\alpha$ is the expected outcome when $x=0$;
- $\beta$ is the expected change in the outcome associated with one unit of $x$.

Formally:

$$
\beta
=
\frac{\partial \mu}{\partial x}
$$

This is an associational interpretation unless causal assumptions justify an interventional one.

---

## 9. Centering the predictor

The Howell models use:

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha
+
\beta(x_i-\overline{x})
$$

When:

$$
x_i=\overline{x}
$$

then:

$$
\mu_i=\alpha
$$

Centering therefore makes the intercept the expected outcome at an average predictor value.

### 9.1 Interpretive advantage

Without centering, the intercept may refer to adult weight at zero centimeters or height at zero kilograms. Such values lie outside the meaningful domain.

### 9.2 Computational advantage

Without centering, the intercept and slope may compensate strongly for each other. Centering often reduces posterior covariance and improves numerical approximation.

### 9.3 Predictions remain equivalent

Centered and uncentered models span the same family of lines. They differ in parameter interpretation and posterior geometry, not in their possible predictions.

## 10. The official 2026 Howell weight model

Let:

- $W_i$ be weight in kilograms;
- $H_i$ be height in centimeters;
- $\overline{H}$ be mean adult height.

The official fitted model is:

$$
W_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma)
$$

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha
+
\beta(H_i-\overline{H})
$$

with priors:

$$
\alpha
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(60,10)
$$

$$
\beta
\sim
\operatorname{LogNormal}(0,1)
$$

$$
\sigma
\sim
\operatorname{Uniform}(0,10)
$$

Interpretation:

- $\alpha$ is expected weight at average adult height;
- $\beta$ is expected weight change per additional centimeter;
- $\sigma$ is residual variation among adults of the same height.

A log-normal slope prior implies:

$$
\beta>0
$$

This encodes prior knowledge that the expected adult weight–height relationship should not slope downward over the modeled range. The restriction must come from substantive knowledge, not from inspecting the sample trend.

## 11. Prior predictive simulation

Coefficient priors are difficult to judge in isolation. Their implications for lines and observations are more informative.

For draw $s$:

$$
\alpha^{(s)}
\sim
p(\alpha)
$$

$$
\beta^{(s)}
\sim
p(\beta)
$$

$$
\sigma^{(s)}
\sim
p(\sigma)
$$

The expected line is:

$$
\mu^{(s)}(H)
=
\alpha^{(s)}
+
\beta^{(s)}(H-\overline{H})
$$

A prior predictive observation is:

$$
W^{(s)}
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\mu^{(s)}(H),
\sigma^{(s)}
\right)
$$

The official scripts first generate implausible lines from broad priors and then use more defensible priors.

Prior predictive checks ask whether the model permits or favors:

- negative adult weights;
- impossible weight changes over a modest height range;
- downward relationships judged implausible beforehand;
- or unrealistic residual variation.

The key lesson is:

$$
\text{prior over parameters}
\rightarrow
\text{prior over functions and data}
$$

A modest-looking slope prior can imply extreme outcomes when multiplied by a wide predictor range.

## 12. Generative simulation before fitting

The official Howell script simulates data from a known regression:

$$
H_i
\sim
\operatorname{Uniform}(130,170)
$$

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha
+
\beta(H_i-\overline{H})
$$

$$
W_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma)
$$

Because the simulation chooses $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\sigma$, their true values are known.

The fitting procedure can then be tested:

1. choose or draw true parameters;
2. generate heights;
3. generate weights;
4. fit the model with `quap`;
5. compare posterior summaries with the known values;
6. repeat across many simulations.

This tests parameter recovery in the model's small world.

A stronger robustness exercise would simulate from alternatives such as:

- heavy-tailed residuals;
- nonlinear relationships;
- heteroskedasticity;
- measurement error;
- subgroup mixtures;
- or selection bias.

## 13. Quadratic approximation

For the regression model:

$$
\theta
=
(\alpha,\beta,\sigma)
$$

Quadratic approximation:

1. finds the posterior mode $\widehat{\theta}$;
2. evaluates local curvature;
3. replaces the posterior near the mode with a multivariate Gaussian.

The approximation is:

$$
p(\theta \mid D)
\approx
\operatorname{MVNormal}
\left(
\widehat{\theta},
\widehat{\Sigma}
\right)
$$

with:

$$
\widehat{\Sigma}
\approx
\left[
-
\nabla^2
\log p(\theta \mid D)
\bigg|_{\theta=\widehat{\theta}}
\right]^{-1}
$$

Near a smooth maximum:

$$
\log p(\theta \mid D)
\approx
\log p(\widehat{\theta} \mid D)
-
\frac{1}{2}
(\theta-\widehat{\theta})^{\mathsf T}
\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}
(\theta-\widehat{\theta})
$$

Exponentiating this quadratic form gives a multivariate Gaussian approximation.

### 13.1 When it works

Quadratic approximation is often adequate when the posterior is:

- unimodal;
- reasonably symmetric;
- well identified;
- and away from hard boundaries.

### 13.2 When it can fail

It can be poor for posteriors that are:

- skewed;
- multimodal;
- truncated;
- funnel-shaped;
- weakly identified;
- or strongly nonlinear.

The fitting engine is part of the practical model and must be checked.

---

## 14. Posterior geometry and covariance

Each pair $(\alpha,\beta)$ defines one line.

Before data, the prior produces a broad cloud of parameter pairs and a broad family of lines. After data, the posterior concentrates.

Under quadratic approximation, equal-density contours are ellipses. A tilted ellipse indicates posterior covariance.

If the intercept increases, the slope may need to decrease to preserve similar predictions over the observed range.

Predictions should therefore use joint posterior draws:

$$
(\alpha^{(s)},\beta^{(s)},\sigma^{(s)})
\sim
p(\alpha,\beta,\sigma \mid D)
$$

Combining unrelated marginal endpoints does not preserve posterior dependence.

---

## 15. Sequential learning of a line

The official scripts animate the movement from prior to posterior as observations are added.

At $n=0$, lines come from the prior. With each observation, the posterior becomes:

$$
p(\alpha,\beta,\sigma \mid D_n)
$$

The animation illustrates that:

- uncertainty initially spans many lines;
- data concentrate the posterior;
- intercept and slope remain dependent;
- predictor spread matters for learning the slope;
- and uncertainty grows under extrapolation.

Many observations at nearly the same $x$ can estimate the mean there while leaving $\beta$ uncertain. Information about the slope depends on variation in the predictor, not only sample size.

---

## 16. Expected outcomes versus predicted observations

For a new height $H^*$, each posterior draw gives an expected weight:

$$
\mu^{(s)}(H^*)
=
\alpha^{(s)}
+
\beta^{(s)}(H^*-\overline{H})
$$

These draws approximate:

$$
p(\mu(H^*) \mid D)
$$

For a new individual, simulate:

$$
W_{\mathrm{new}}^{(s)}
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\mu^{(s)}(H^*),
\sigma^{(s)}
\right)
$$

This approximates:

$$
p(W_{\mathrm{new}} \mid H^*,D)
$$

The predictive interval is wider because it includes:

1. uncertainty about the expected relationship;
2. residual variation among individuals with the same predictor value.

A precise estimate of the mean does not imply a precise individual prediction.

---

## 17. Associational versus causal slopes

For the centered weight model:

$$
\beta
=
\frac{\partial}{\partial H}
\mathbb{E}[W \mid H]
$$

This does not automatically equal:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial h}
\mathbb{E}
\left[
W \mid \operatorname{do}(H=h)
\right]
$$

Comparing naturally taller and shorter adults is not the same as intervening to change an adult's height.

Regression parameters acquire causal meaning only through an explicit causal model, a meaningful intervention, and identification assumptions.

This is the geocentric warning expressed in causal notation.

## 18. Polynomial extensions

`03_prior_pred_OLS.r` also contains quadratic and cubic models.

Quadratic:

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha
+
\beta_1x_i
+
\beta_2x_i^2
$$

Cubic:

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha
+
\beta_1x_i
+
\beta_2x_i^2
+
\beta_3x_i^3
$$

These models are linear in their parameters even though their predicted curves are nonlinear in $x$.

Adding terms resembles adding epicycles: the model can describe increasingly complicated patterns without identifying the generating mechanism.

Higher-order terms make prior predictive checking especially important because small coefficient changes can produce extreme curves outside the observed range.

Section 4.5 generalizes this idea with spline basis functions:

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha
+
\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_kB_{ki}
$$

This section belongs to Chapter 4 but is secondary to the main A03 topics named in the official lecture description.

## 19. Common errors exposed by A03

### Error 1: Treating the fitted line as a causal mechanism

A line describes a conditional mean unless causal assumptions establish more.

### Error 2: Reversing a regression

A model for $p(Y \mid X)$ is not a model for $p(X \mid Y)$.

### Error 3: Choosing priors coefficient by coefficient

Priors should be evaluated through the functions and observations they generate.

### Error 4: Using the observed trend to justify the prior sign

Sign restrictions should come from pre-data scientific knowledge.

### Error 5: Interpreting an unsupported intercept

Centering places the intercept in a meaningful region.

### Error 6: Ignoring posterior covariance

Joint draws are required for coherent lines and predictions.

### Error 7: Confusing a mean interval with a prediction interval

Individual observations remain variable around the expected relationship.

### Error 8: Assuming Gaussian residuals from the raw histogram

The model concerns the conditional distribution.

### Error 9: Ignoring heavy tails

A Gaussian likelihood may underestimate extreme outcomes.

### Error 10: Trusting `quap` automatically

Quadratic approximation is local and can fail for non-Gaussian posterior geometry.

### Error 11: Extrapolating because the equation permits it

The mathematical line extends indefinitely; the scientific relationship usually does not.

### Error 12: Equating prediction with explanation

A geocentric model may predict appearances while misunderstanding causes.

## 20. CRM application: customer revenue

Let:

- $R_i$ be next-30-day revenue;
- $F_i$ be recent purchase frequency;
- $\overline{F}$ be mean recent frequency.

A baseline model is:

$$
R_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma)
$$

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha
+
\beta(F_i-\overline{F})
$$

Prior predictive checks should ask:

- Does the model generate large negative revenues?
- Does one extra purchase imply an impossible revenue increase?
- Does the residual scale reflect customer heterogeneity?
- Is a Gaussian likelihood plausible for a nonnegative, right-skewed outcome?

The expected-revenue distribution is:

$$
p(\mu(F) \mid D)
$$

The individual predictive distribution is:

$$
p(R_{\mathrm{new}} \mid F,D)
$$

The latter is wider and more relevant for customer-level risk.

A positive slope does not prove that forcing one additional purchase would increase future revenue by $\beta$. Frequency may reflect affinity, income, tenure, product need, previous campaigns, or seasonality.

The model is descriptive unless a causal design identifies an intervention effect.

## 22. Lecture takeaways

1. Linear regression is a useful approximation, not a literal causal theory.
2. Predictive success does not establish the true generating mechanism.
3. Least squares arises from a Gaussian observation model with a linear mean.
4. Bayesian regression adds priors and a joint posterior over lines and residual scale.
5. Gaussian distributions often arise from aggregating many small influences.
6. Gaussian models are not universal and may underestimate extreme events.
7. A regression model separates the observation distribution from the model of its mean.
8. Modeling $Y$ from $X$ differs from modeling $X$ from $Y$.
9. Centering improves interpretation and often posterior geometry.
10. Priors should be checked through their implications for functions and observations.
11. Generative simulation tests parameter recovery before real-data analysis.
12. Quadratic approximation locally replaces the posterior with a multivariate Gaussian.
13. Posterior covariance matters for coherent prediction.
14. Uncertainty about an expected outcome is narrower than uncertainty about a new observation.
15. Polynomial terms can improve fit without revealing mechanism.
16. Extrapolation is a scientific assumption.
17. A regression coefficient remains associational unless a causal design gives it interventional meaning.
