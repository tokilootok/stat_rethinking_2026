## 1. Main thesis

The central argument is:

> **Categories are easy to encode statistically, but their coefficients and contrasts acquire meaning only after the scientific and causal question has been specified.**

A categorical regression can answer several different questions:

- How does the expected outcome differ across categories?
- How does the predictive distribution differ across categories?
- Does a continuous predictor have a different association in each category?
- What is the total causal effect associated with changing a category?
- What is the direct causal effect after holding a mediator fixed?
- Which categories must be included to identify the effect of another cause?

These questions are not interchangeable.

The workflow is:

$$ \text{scientific question} \rightarrow \text{causal model} \rightarrow \text{estimand} \rightarrow \text{categorical encoding} \rightarrow \text{Bayesian regression} \rightarrow \text{posterior contrast} \rightarrow \text{interpretation} $$

A04 connects three ideas:

1. categorical variables require explicit statistical representation;
2. posterior contrasts should be computed directly rather than inferred from overlapping intervals;
3. the correct model depends on the causal estimand, not merely on the columns available in the dataset.

## 3. Categories are discrete and unordered

A categorical variable assigns observations to named groups.

Examples include:

- sex category;
- treatment arm;
- geographic region;
- product family;
- acquisition channel;
- customer segment;
- experimental condition.

For a nominal category, the labels have no meaningful numerical distance.

Suppose region is encoded as:

$$ 1=\text{France} $$

$$ 2=\text{Germany} $$

$$ 3=\text{Spain} $$

The regression:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+\beta R_i $$

would treat these codes as measured amounts. It would impose equal increments from France to Germany and from Germany to Spain.

For a nominal variable, the codes should instead be used as names or indexes.

The central distinction is:

$$ \text{index value} \neq \text{measured amount} $$

### 3.1 Nominal versus ordered categories

An ordered category has a known ranking, such as low, medium, and high. Even then, equal numerical spacing should not be assumed automatically.

A04 mainly concerns unordered categories. Ordered categorical outcomes and predictors require more specialized models later in the book and course.

## 4. The Howell height–weight–sex example

The official A04 histogram script selects adult observations from `Howell1` and compares height and weight distributions by sex category.

Let:

- $S$ denote sex category;
- $H$ denote adult height;
- $W$ denote adult weight.

The histograms describe:

$$ p(H \mid S) $$

and:

$$ p(W \mid S) $$

They reveal category-associated distributional differences, but they do not establish causal direction.

A plausible causal model is:

$$ S \rightarrow H \rightarrow W $$

with an additional path:

$$ S \rightarrow W $$

In this model, category affects weight partly through height and partly through other pathways.

But the data alone do not prove this graph. Other causal structures can generate similar scatterplots and histograms.

The scientific principle is:

$$ \text{causes are not contained in the data table} $$

---

## 5. Structural representation

A simplified structural model is:

$$ S_i \sim p(S) $$

$$ H_i = f_H(S_i,U_i) $$

$$ W_i = f_W(H_i,S_i,V_i) $$

where:

- $U_i$ contains unobserved causes of height;
- $V_i$ contains unobserved causes of weight.

If $U_i$ and $V_i$ are independent, they do not confound the height–weight relation.

If a common unobserved cause $C_i$ influences both:

$$ C_i \rightarrow H_i $$

and:

$$ C_i \rightarrow W_i $$

then the height–weight association is confounded.

A statistical regression can implement assumptions from a causal model. It cannot determine the causal model by itself.

---

## 6. Different questions imply different estimands

The same variables support several distinct estimands.

### 6.1 Descriptive category difference

$$ \Delta_S^{\mathrm{assoc}} = \mathbb{E}[W \mid S=2] - \mathbb{E}[W \mid S=1] $$

This is an observed-population association.

### 6.2 Total causal effect

$$ \Delta_S^{\mathrm{total}} = \mathbb{E}[W \mid \mathrm{do}(S=2)] - \mathbb{E}[W \mid \mathrm{do}(S=1)] $$

This includes every causal path from $S$ to $W$, including mediation through height.

### 6.3 Controlled direct effect

At a fixed height $h$:

$$ \Delta_S^{\mathrm{direct}}(h) = \mathbb{E}[W \mid \mathrm{do}(S=2),\mathrm{do}(H=h)] - \mathbb{E}[W \mid \mathrm{do}(S=1),\mathrm{do}(H=h)] $$

This blocks the pathway through height.

### 6.4 Effect of height on weight

$$ \Delta_H(h_1,h_0) = \mathbb{E}[W \mid \mathrm{do}(H=h_1)] - \mathbb{E}[W \mid \mathrm{do}(H=h_0)] $$

If $S$ causes both $H$ and $W$, then $S$ is a common cause that must be accounted for when estimating the effect of height.

### 6.5 Adjustment depends on the estimand

To estimate the total effect of $S$ on $W$, conditioning on mediator $H$ would remove part of the target effect.

To estimate a direct effect of $S$, conditioning on $H$ may be necessary.

To estimate the causal effect of $H$ on $W$, conditioning on common cause $S$ may be necessary.

Therefore:

$$ \text{there is no universally correct adjustment set} $$

## 7. Binary indicator coding

Define:

$$ M_i = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{category 2}\\ 0 & \text{category 1} \end{cases} $$

A baseline-plus-difference model is:

$$ Y_i \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma) $$

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+\beta_M M_i $$

For category 1:

$$ \mu_i=\alpha $$

For category 2:

$$ \mu_i=\alpha+\beta_M $$

Thus:

- $\alpha$ is the expected outcome for the reference category;
- $\beta_M$ is the expected difference between category 2 and category 1.

### 7.1 The reference category is a parameterization choice

Changing which category receives zero changes the interpretation of $\alpha$ and the sign of $\beta_M$.

It need not change fitted category expectations when priors are transformed consistently.

### 7.2 Prior asymmetry

Suppose:

$$ \alpha \sim \mathrm{Normal}(m_\alpha,s_\alpha) $$

$$ \beta_M \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0,s_\beta) $$

Then:

$$ \mu_1=\alpha $$

and:

$$ \mu_2=\alpha+\beta_M $$

Under independent priors:

$$ \mathrm{Var}(\mu_1) = s_\alpha^2 $$

$$ \mathrm{Var}(\mu_2) = s_\alpha^2+s_\beta^2 $$

Category 2 receives greater prior uncertainty merely because it is represented as a deviation from category 1.

This is one reason McElreath favors index coding.

## 8. Index-variable coding

Let:

$$ S_i \in \{1,2,\ldots,K\} $$

The integers are category addresses.

A category-specific mean model is:

$$ Y_i \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma) $$

$$ \mu_i = \alpha_{S_i} $$

with symmetric priors:

$$ \alpha_j \sim \mathrm{Normal}(m_\alpha,s_\alpha) $$

for:

$$ j=1,\ldots,K $$

### 8.1 Advantages

Index coding:

- treats categories symmetrically;
- extends directly to many categories;
- makes category expectations easy to interpret;
- supports arbitrary posterior contrasts;
- extends naturally to category-specific slopes;
- prepares for multilevel models.

### 8.2 Rethinking-style model

```r
m_cat <- quap(
  alist(
    W ~ dnorm(mu, sigma),
    mu <- a[S],
    a[S] ~ dnorm(50, 10),
    sigma ~ dexp(1)
  ),
  data = dat
)
```

Here `S` selects the relevant element of vector `a`.

The model:

$$ \mu_i=\alpha_{S_i} $$

is not the same as:

$$ \mu_i=\alpha+\beta S_i $$

The first uses the integer as an index. The second treats it as a quantity.

## 9. Contrasts are posterior quantities

For categories 2 and 1:

$$ \Delta = \alpha_2-\alpha_1 $$

For each joint posterior draw:

$$ \Delta^{(s)} = \alpha_2^{(s)}-\alpha_1^{(s)} $$

The resulting draws approximate:

$$ p(\Delta \mid D) $$

### 9.1 Why draw-by-draw computation matters

The category parameters may be posterior-correlated.

$$ \mathrm{Var}(\alpha_2-\alpha_1) = \mathrm{Var}(\alpha_2) + \mathrm{Var}(\alpha_1) - 2\mathrm{Cov}(\alpha_2,\alpha_1) $$

Draw-by-draw subtraction preserves this covariance automatically.

### 9.2 R implementation

```r
post <- extract.samples(m_cat)
contrast_21 <- post$a[, 2] - post$a[, 1]
precis(contrast_21)
```

Relevant summaries may include:

- posterior mean or median;
- compatibility interval;
- probability the contrast is positive;
- probability it exceeds a practical threshold;
- expected decision loss.

---

## 10. Never infer a difference from interval overlap

It is not legitimate to inspect two marginal intervals and infer the uncertainty of their difference.

The relevant object is:

$$ p(\alpha_2-\alpha_1 \mid D) $$

not two separate displays of:

$$ p(\alpha_1 \mid D) $$

and:

$$ p(\alpha_2 \mid D) $$

Intervals can overlap while the contrast is concentrated away from zero. They can also fail to overlap while a predictive individual-level contrast remains highly uncertain.

The same rule applies to:

- posterior intervals;
- confidence intervals;
- category-specific regression lines;
- probabilities;
- nonlinear predictions.

Always compute the desired contrast directly.

---

## 11. Difference in means versus difference between observations

Two contrasts answer different questions.

### 11.1 Expected-outcome contrast

$$ \Delta_\mu^{(s)} = \mu_2^{(s)}-\mu_1^{(s)} $$

This represents uncertainty about the category mean difference.

### 11.2 Predictive contrast

Simulate a new outcome from each category:

$$ \widetilde{Y}_1^{(s)} \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( \mu_1^{(s)}, \sigma^{(s)} \right) $$

$$ \widetilde{Y}_2^{(s)} \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( \mu_2^{(s)}, \sigma^{(s)} \right) $$

Then:

$$ \Delta_Y^{(s)} = \widetilde{Y}_2^{(s)} - \widetilde{Y}_1^{(s)} $$

This distribution includes individual-level residual variation and is therefore wider.

It can estimate:

$$ \Pr \left( \widetilde{Y}_2>\widetilde{Y}_1 \mid D \right) $$

A precisely estimated difference in category means can coexist with substantial overlap among individual outcomes.

## 12. One line per category

Categories can alter intercepts and slopes:

$$ W_i \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma) $$

$$ \mu_i = \alpha_{S_i} + \beta_{S_i} (H_i-\overline{H}) $$

For category $j$:

- $\alpha_j$ is expected weight at average height;
- $\beta_j$ is the category-specific height–weight slope.

The expected category contrast at height $h$ is:

$$ \Delta_\mu(h) = \left[ \alpha_2+\beta_2(h-\overline{H}) \right] - \left[ \alpha_1+\beta_1(h-\overline{H}) \right] $$

Therefore:

$$ \Delta_\mu(h) = (\alpha_2-\alpha_1) + (\beta_2-\beta_1)(h-\overline{H}) $$

When slopes differ, the category contrast is not a single number. It varies with height.

This is an interaction expressed with index variables.

---

## 13. Stratification and causal roles

The model:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha_{S_i} + \beta_{S_i}x_i $$

fits a separate conditional relationship in each category.

The category may be included because it is:

- the treatment of interest;
- a confounder;
- an effect modifier;
- a mediator;
- needed for transport or standardization;
- merely predictive.

The algebra does not identify which role applies. The causal model does.

---

## 14. Total effect of category on weight

Under:

$$ S \rightarrow H \rightarrow W $$

and:

$$ S \rightarrow W $$

a category-only model:

$$ W_i \sim \mathrm{Normal} (\alpha_{S_i},\sigma) $$

can represent the total category difference:

$$ \Delta_S = \alpha_2-\alpha_1 $$

provided the observed category contrast identifies the causal contrast under the assumed design and causal model.

This total contrast includes the path through height.

If height is added:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha_{S_i} + \beta(H_i-\overline{H}) $$

the category contrast now compares categories at a common height. The estimand has changed toward a direct conditional effect.

---

## 15. Estimating the effect of height

If $S$ causes both height and weight, the unadjusted height–weight relation contains the path:

$$ H \leftarrow S \rightarrow W $$

A stratified model can close that path:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha_{S_i} + \beta(H_i-\overline{H}) $$

or allow effect modification:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha_{S_i} + \beta_{S_i}(H_i-\overline{H}) $$

The common-slope model assumes the effect of height is the same in every category.

The category-specific model does not.

### 15.1 Standardization

A population intervention prediction can average category-specific expectations over the target population:

$$ \mathbb{E} [W \mid \mathrm{do}(H=h)] = \sum_s \mathbb{E}[W \mid H=h,S=s] p(S=s) $$

This is standardization or g-computation.

A regression coefficient is often only an intermediate quantity. The estimand is the standardized intervention contrast.

---

## 16. Direct effects and mediation

Conditioning on height attempts to isolate the direct path:

$$ S \rightarrow W $$

from the mediated path:

$$ S \rightarrow H \rightarrow W $$

But a direct-effect interpretation requires strong assumptions.

There must be adequate control of confounding for:

- the category–outcome relation;
- the mediator–outcome relation;
- any treatment-induced mediator–outcome confounding.

The exact requirements depend on whether the target is:

- a controlled direct effect;
- a natural direct effect;
- an interventional direct effect;
- a descriptive conditional contrast.

A04 introduces why the model depends on the question. A06 and A07 develop confounding and controls more formally.

---

## 17. Full generative simulation for a total effect

A total-effect simulation can model both mediator and outcome:

$$ H_i \sim \mathrm{Normal} (\eta_{S_i},\tau) $$

$$ W_i \sim \mathrm{Normal} (\mu_i,\sigma) $$

$$ \mu_i = \alpha_{S_i} + \beta_{S_i} (H_i-\overline{H}) $$

For each posterior draw and intervention state $s$:

$$ H_s^{(r)} \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( \eta_s^{(r)}, \tau^{(r)} \right) $$

$$ W_s^{(r)} \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( \alpha_s^{(r)} + \beta_s^{(r)} (H_s^{(r)}-\overline{H}), \sigma^{(r)} \right) $$

Then:

$$ \Delta_S^{(r)} = W_2^{(r)}-W_1^{(r)} $$

This propagates the category effect through the mediator as well as the outcome model.

It demonstrates why a complete causal estimator may require several linked equations rather than one coefficient.

## 18. Simulation-based validation in the official script

The official validation script defines:

$$ W_i = bH_i+U_i $$

with:

$$ U_i \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0,\sigma) $$

It uses values such as:

$$ b=0.5 $$

and:

$$ \sigma=5 $$

The fitted model is:

$$ W_i \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma) $$

$$ \mu_i = a+bH_i $$

with priors on $a$, $b$, and $\sigma$.

### 18.1 Repeated recovery experiment

The script:

1. simulates a height sample;
2. simulates weights using the known slope;
3. fits the Bayesian regression;
4. stores the posterior summary for $b$;
5. repeats the process;
6. compares intervals with the true value.

For $R$ repetitions, empirical coverage is:

$$ \widehat{C} = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^{R} \mathbb{I} \left[ L_r \leq b_{\mathrm{true}} \leq U_r \right] $$

### 18.2 What the validation establishes

It can reveal:

- implementation errors;
- biased recovery;
- poor interval calibration;
- boundary problems;
- unsuitable priors at the simulated sample size;
- failure of quadratic approximation.

### 18.3 What it does not establish

It does not prove that real height and weight are generated by a linear, Gaussian, unconfounded process.

Robustness scenarios could include:

- category-specific slopes;
- nonlinear relationships;
- heavy-tailed residuals;
- measurement error;
- omitted common causes;
- mediator–outcome confounding;
- imbalanced categories.

## 19. Priors for category models

Index coding permits symmetric priors:

$$ \alpha_j \sim \mathrm{Normal} (m_\alpha,s_\alpha) $$

For slopes:

$$ \beta_j \sim \mathrm{LogNormal} (m_\beta,s_\beta) $$

when a positive direction is known beforehand, or:

$$ \beta_j \sim \mathrm{Normal} (0,s_\beta) $$

when either direction is plausible.

### 19.1 Prior predictive contrasts

For each prior draw:

$$ \mu_j^{(r)}(h) = \alpha_j^{(r)} + \beta_j^{(r)} (h-\overline{H}) $$

Inspect the prior distribution of:

$$ \alpha_2-\alpha_1 $$

and, with different slopes:

$$ \mu_2(h)-\mu_1(h) $$

across relevant values of $h$.

Priors that appear reasonable coefficient by coefficient can imply implausible category differences or curves.

## 20. More than two categories

For $K$ categories:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha_{S_i} $$

with:

$$ S_i \in \{1,\ldots,K\} $$

A pairwise contrast is:

$$ \Delta_{jk} = \alpha_j-\alpha_k $$

A contrast against a weighted average is:

$$ \Delta_j = \alpha_j - \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k\alpha_k $$

where:

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{K}w_k=1 $$

Weights may represent:

- equal weighting;
- observed population proportions;
- a target population;
- business importance;
- policy relevance.

The category parameters are primitive model quantities. The scientific estimand determines how to combine them.

---

## 21. Categories have different causal roles

A categorical variable can be:

### 21.1 Treatment

Examples include no offer, €5, €10, and €20 offers.

### 21.2 Confounder

A category may influence both treatment assignment and outcome.

### 21.3 Effect modifier

$$ \tau(s) = \mathbb{E} [Y(1)-Y(0) \mid S=s] $$

### 21.4 Mediator

Treatment may alter a categorical intermediate state that affects the outcome.

### 21.5 Selection variable

Inclusion in the sample may depend on category and outcome-related causes.

### 21.6 Predictive label

A category may improve prediction without having a justified causal role.

The category's role comes from the scientific graph and study design, not from its storage type.

## 22. CRM application: discount categories

Suppose:

$$ A_i \in \{0,5,10,20\} $$

Although these levels are ordered and measured in euros, a linear model:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+\beta A_i $$

assumes constant response per euro.

An index model avoids that assumption:

$$ Y_i \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma) $$

$$ \mu_i = \alpha_{g_i} $$

where $g_i$ indexes the offer level.

### 22.1 Offer-specific effects

$$ \tau_5 = \mathbb{E} [Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A=5)] - \mathbb{E} [Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A=0)] $$

$$ \tau_{10} = \mathbb{E} [Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A=10)] - \mathbb{E} [Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A=0)] $$

$$ \tau_{20} = \mathbb{E} [Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A=20)] - \mathbb{E} [Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A=0)] $$

An incremental contrast is:

$$ \tau_{10-5} = \mathbb{E} [Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A=10)] - \mathbb{E} [Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A=5)] $$

### 22.2 Profit estimand

$$ \Pi(a) = \mathbb{E} \left[ \text{margin}(a) - \text{offer cost}(a) \right] $$

The optimal action is:

$$ a^* = \arg\max_a \mathbb{E} [\Pi(a) \mid D] $$

The offer with the highest purchase response may not maximize profit.

### 22.3 Confounded assignment

If high-affinity customers preferentially receive larger offers:

$$ \mathbb{E}[Y \mid A=a] $$

does not necessarily equal:

$$ \mathbb{E} [Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A=a)] $$

Index coding solves representation. It does not solve confounding.

## 23. CRM application: segments and heterogeneous effects

Let $S_i$ index customer segment and $A_i$ index offer category.

A flexible model is:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha_{S_i,A_i} $$

The segment-specific treatment contrast is:

$$ \tau_s(a,a_0) = \alpha_{s,a} - \alpha_{s,a_0} $$

Sparse segment–offer cells can be unstable. Later multilevel models can partially pool them:

$$ \alpha_{s,a} \sim \mathrm{Normal} (\overline{\alpha}_a,\sigma_a) $$

A04 establishes the index-and-contrast logic that multilevel modeling later generalizes.

## 24. Implications for CausalOps

| A04 concept | CausalOps implication |

| Category code | Store labels separately from numeric indexes |
| Category role | Declare treatment, confounder, mediator, modifier, selection, or predictive role |
| Indicator coding | Record reference category and prior asymmetry |
| Index coding | Prefer symmetric category parameters when appropriate |
| Posterior contrast | Represent as a first-class estimand |
| Mean contrast | Distinguish from predictive observation contrast |
| Category-specific lines | Support vector parameters and interactions |
| Total versus direct effect | Bind adjustment set to the estimand |

CausalOps should flag:

## 25. Common errors exposed by A04

### Error 1: Treating category indexes as quantities

Index labels are addresses, not measured amounts.

### Error 2: Assuming dummy coding is neutral

Baseline coding changes interpretation and can induce prior asymmetry.

### Error 3: Ignoring the reference category

The coefficient's sign and meaning depend on coding.

### Error 4: Comparing interval overlap

The uncertainty of a difference must be computed directly.

### Error 5: Confusing mean and individual contrasts

Predictive differences include residual variation.

### Error 6: Adding every category as a control

A category may be a mediator, collider, or consequence of treatment.

### Error 7: Believing index coding solves confounding

It solves representation, not identification.

### Error 8: Conditioning on a mediator while claiming a total effect

Adjustment changes the estimand.

### Error 9: Omitting a common cause

An unadjusted continuous slope may be confounded.

### Error 10: Assuming a constant category contrast

Different slopes make the contrast depend on $x$.

### Error 11: Imposing a linear dose response on ordered offers

Ordering does not imply linearity.

### Error 12: Validating only one simulation

Repeated simulations are needed for bias and coverage.

## 27. Review questions (Detailed Answers & Multi-Language Implementation)

### Question 1: Why is a category index different from a continuous predictor?
**Answer:**  
A continuous predictor represents a measured quantitative magnitude where numerical ordering, distance, and ratios carry arithmetic meaning (e.g. $160\text{ cm} - 150\text{ cm} = 10\text{ cm}$). A category index is simply an integer memory address or label designating a discrete group (e.g. $1 = \text{Female}, 2 = \text{Male}$). Multiplying a category index by a single slope coefficient $\beta S_i$ imposes a false linear constraint that group 2 must differ from baseline by twice the amount of group 1, which is nonsense for nominal categories.

---

### Question 2: What do $\alpha$ and $\beta_M$ represent under binary indicator coding?
**Answer:**  
In binary indicator coding $\mu_i = \alpha + \beta_M M_i$ (where $M_i \in \{0, 1\}$):
- $\alpha$ represents the expected outcome of the baseline reference category ($M_i = 0$).
- $\beta_M$ represents the expected difference (contrast) between the target category ($M_i = 1$) and the baseline category ($M_i = 0$), so $\mu(M=1) = \alpha + \beta_M$.

### Question 3: Why can baseline coding create asymmetric prior uncertainty?
**Answer:**  
In indicator coding $\mu_1 = \alpha$ and $\mu_2 = \alpha + \beta_M$, if independent prior distributions are assigned to $\alpha \sim \mathrm{Normal}(m_\alpha, s_\alpha)$ and $\beta_M \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0, s_\beta)$, the prior variance of the reference category is $\mathrm{Var}(\mu_1) = s_\alpha^2$, whereas the prior variance of the secondary category is $\mathrm{Var}(\mu_2) = s_\alpha^2 + s_\beta^2$. Thus, category 2 is assigned greater prior variance solely as an artifact of parameter choice, rather than domain scientific knowledge.

---

### Question 4: How does index coding remove this asymmetry?
**Answer:**  
Index coding assigns a separate parameter $\alpha_j$ to each category directly: $\mu_i = \alpha_{S_i}$. Each $\alpha_j$ can be assigned an identical, symmetric prior $\alpha_j \sim \mathrm{Normal}(m_\alpha, s_\alpha)$ for all $j \in \{1, \ldots, K\}$, ensuring that all categories begin with identical prior expectations and identical prior variances.

---

### Question 5: Why must a contrast use joint posterior draws?
**Answer:**  
Category parameters in a posterior distribution are correlated ($\mathrm{Cov}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2 \mid D) \neq 0$). The variance of a difference is $\mathrm{Var}(\alpha_2 - \alpha_1) = \mathrm{Var}(\alpha_2) + \mathrm{Var}(\alpha_1) - 2\mathrm{Cov}(\alpha_2, \alpha_1)$. Computing the contrast row-by-row across joint posterior draws ($\Delta^{(s)} = \alpha_2^{(s)} - \alpha_1^{(s)}$) explicitly preserves this covariance structure.

### Question 6: Why is interval overlap not a valid comparison?
**Answer:**  
Inspecting whether two marginal compatibility intervals (e.g. $p(\alpha_1 \mid D)$ and $p(\alpha_2 \mid D)$) overlap is visually misleading. Two marginal distributions can overlap substantially even when their difference $p(\alpha_2 - \alpha_1 \mid D)$ is strictly positive with near 100% probability because joint covariance reduces the variance of the difference. Conversely, two non-overlapping mean intervals can coexist with almost complete overlap in individual-level predictions $\widetilde{Y}$.

---

### Question 7: How does $\Delta_\mu$ differ from $\Delta_Y$?
**Answer:**  
- **Expected-Outcome Contrast ($\Delta_\mu$):** Quantifies epistemic uncertainty about the difference between group average means ($\Delta_\mu^{(s)} = \mu_2^{(s)} - \mu_1^{(s)}$). As $N \to \infty$, $\mathrm{Var}(\Delta_\mu) \to 0$.
- **Predictive Observation Contrast ($\Delta_Y$):** Quantifies uncertainty in the outcome difference between two individual observations sampled from each category ($\Delta_Y^{(s)} = \widetilde{Y}_2^{(s)} - \widetilde{Y}_1^{(s)}$ where $\widetilde{Y}_j^{(s)} \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\mu_j^{(s)}, \sigma^{(s)})$). Even as $N \to \infty$, this variance remains bounded by $2\sigma^2$.

### Question 8: How do category-specific slopes make the contrast depend on height?
**Answer:**  
In a category-specific slope model $\mu_i = \alpha_{S_i} + \beta_{S_i}(H_i - \overline{H})$, the expected contrast between category 2 and category 1 at height $h$ is $\Delta_\mu(h) = (\alpha_2 - \alpha_1) + (\beta_2 - \beta_1)(h - \overline{H})$. If $\beta_2 \neq \beta_1$, the expected category contrast varies continuously as a function of height $h$.

### Question 9: Which model targets the total effect under $S \rightarrow H \rightarrow W$ and $S \rightarrow W$?
**Answer:**  
To target the **total causal effect** of sex category $S$ on weight $W$ under the DAG $S \to H \to W$ and $S \to W$, one must estimate the model $W_i \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\alpha_{S_i}, \sigma)$ without conditioning on height $H$. Including mediator $H$ blocks the indirect causal pathway $S \to H \to W$, restricting the estimate to a direct conditional effect.

---

### Question 10: Why does conditioning on $H$ change the estimand?
**Answer:**  
Conditioning on height $H$ holds height constant, transforming the estimand from the total causal effect $\mathbb{E}[W \mid \mathrm{do}(S)]$ to a controlled direct effect $\mathbb{E}[W \mid \mathrm{do}(S), \mathrm{do}(H=h)]$. It removes the indirect effect mediated through height growth.

### Question 11: Why may $S$ be needed when estimating the effect of $H$ on $W$?
**Answer:**  
If sex category $S$ acts as a common cause influencing both height $H$ ($S \to H$) and weight $W$ ($S \to W$), then $S$ constitutes a classic backdoor confounder for the causal path $H \to W$. Estimating the causal effect of height on weight without controlling for $S$ produces backdoor bias. Conditioning on $S$ (via index intercepts or category-specific slopes) closes this confounding path.

---

### Question 12: What assumptions are needed for a direct-effect interpretation?
**Answer:**  
To interpret the coefficient of $S$ in a model conditioning on mediator $H$ as a direct causal effect:
1. No unobserved confounding between treatment $S$ and outcome $W$.
2. No unobserved confounding between mediator $H$ and outcome $W$.
3. No unobserved confounding between treatment $S$ and mediator $H$.
4. No treatment-induced mediator-outcome confounding (no variable affected by $S$ that confounds $H \to W$).

### Question 13: What does the official repeated-simulation script test?
**Answer:**  
The official script `LA04_sim heights validate example.r` executes a **generative parameter recovery experiment** (simulation-based calibration/validation). It tests whether the statistical estimation pipeline (`quap` or Stan) correctly recovers true data-generating parameter values ($b_{\text{true}}$) with nominal interval coverage when fed synthetic data drawn from the assumed model.

# R Code: Parameter Recovery Loop
sim_validate <- function(N=100, b_true=0.5, sd_true=5) {
  H <- runif(N, 130, 170)
  W <- b_true * H + rnorm(N, 0, sd_true)
  fit <- lm(W ~ H)
  ci <- confint(fit)["H", ]
  return(c(est = coef(fit)["H"], covered = (b_true >= ci[1] && b_true <= ci[2])))
}
set.seed(42); res <- replicate(100, sim_validate())
cat(sprintf("Mean Estimate: %.4f, 95%% Coverage Rate: %.2f%%\n", mean(res["est",]), mean(res["covered",])*100))

### Question 14: Why does small-world recovery not establish large-world validity?
**Answer:**  
Small-world recovery verifies mathematical identifiability and software correctness: *if* the real world perfectly matched the assumed data-generating process, the model would recover the true parameters. Large-world validity fails when the real data-generating process violates assumed linearity, Gaussian errors, unconfoundedness, or structural independence.

---

### Question 15: How would you encode four discount offers without assuming linear response?
**Answer:**  
For four discrete discount levels ($€0, €5, €10, €20$), assign an index variable $g_i \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$. Specify the expected outcome as $\mu_i = \alpha_{g_i}$. This assigns an independent parameter $\alpha_k$ to each offer level, allowing arbitrary non-linear responses (e.g. diminishing marginal returns or threshold effects) without assuming constant response per euro.

---

### Question 16: Which offer contrasts matter for €5, €10, and €20 treatments?
**Answer:**  
1. **Incremental Contrasts against Baseline (€0):** $\tau_5 = \alpha_2 - \alpha_1$, $\tau_{10} = \alpha_3 - \alpha_1$, $\tau_{20} = \alpha_4 - \alpha_1$.
2. **Stepwise Incremental Contrasts:** $\tau_{10-5} = \alpha_3 - \alpha_2$, $\tau_{20-10} = \alpha_4 - \alpha_3$.

---

### Question 17: Why may the highest-response offer fail to maximize profit?
**Answer:**  
Profit is defined as expected margin minus offer cost: $\Pi(a) = \text{Margin}(a) - \text{Cost}(a)$. Although a €20 discount may generate the highest revenue response, its marginal revenue gain over a €10 discount may be €4, while its incremental cost is €10. The optimal action $a^* = \arg\max_a \mathbb{E}[\Pi(a) \mid D]$ maximizes net profit, not gross volume.

---

### Question 18: When should category estimates be partially pooled?
**Answer:**  
Category estimates should be partially pooled (via multilevel models $\alpha_j \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\overline{\alpha}, \sigma_\alpha)$) when sample sizes per category are small or imbalanced, when there are many categories, or when estimating cell means in multi-factor interactions (e.g. customer segment $\times$ offer type). Partial pooling shrinks noisy, small-sample category estimates toward the population average.

---

### Question 19: What category metadata should CausalOps store?
**Answer:**  
CausalOps should store:
- Level labels and integer index mappings (e.g. `1: Control, 2: Variant_A`).
- Causal role declaration (`treatment`, `confounder`, `mediator`, `effect_modifier`, `selection_variable`).
- Coding style (`index_coding` vs `indicator_coding`) and reference baseline.
- Ordering property (`nominal` vs `ordered`) and whether linear spacing is assumed.
- Target population weights $w_k$ for standardization.

---

### Question 20: How should a treatment category be distinguished from a predictive segment?
**Answer:**  
- **Treatment Category:** Represents an actionable, external intervention ($\mathrm{do}(A = a)$) that can be manipulated by a decision maker.
- **Predictive Segment:** Represents an intrinsic, observable customer attribute or pre-treatment characteristic ($S$) used for stratification, conditioning, or heterogeneous effect modification ($\tau_s(a)$).

## 31. Appendix B: Solution to Homework A04

### Problem Statement
> **Homework A04 Question:**  
> Using the Howell1 dataset, consider only the people younger than 13 years old. In this sample, estimate the causal effect of each month of growth on weight. Be sure to perform a prior predictive simulation to check and justify your priors. Also try to perform a validation of your model on synthetic data.

---

### 1. Mathematical Construction & Prior Justification

#### Data Transformation & Causal DAG
1. **Filtering & Variable Transformation:**
   Filter the `Howell1` dataset for $A_{\text{years}} < 13$. Convert age in years to age in months:
   $$A_{\text{months}, i} = 12 \times A_{\text{years}, i}$$
   The sample contains $N = 146$ children aged $0$ to $144$ months, with weights ranging from $4.25\text{ kg}$ to $32.5\text{ kg}$.

2. **Causal Graph & Target Estimand:**
   Consider the causal DAG:
   $$A_{\text{months}} \longrightarrow H \longrightarrow W \quad \text{and} \quad A_{\text{months}} \longrightarrow W$$
   The target estimand is the **Total Causal Effect of each month of age/growth on weight** ($\mathbb{E}[W \mid \mathrm{do}(A_{\text{months}} + 1)] - \mathbb{E}[W \mid \mathrm{do}(A_{\text{months}})]$).
   Because height $H$ is a mediator on the pathway from age to weight, height MUST NOT be included in the regression model for the total effect.

3. **Model Specification:**
   $$W_i \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\mu_i, \sigma)$$
   $$\mu_i = \alpha + \beta_{\text{month}} A_{\text{months}, i}$$

4. **Prior Selection & Justification:**
   - **Intercept $\alpha$ (Birth Weight at $A_{\text{months}} = 0$):**  
     Newborn weight at birth ($0$ months) averages $\approx 3.3\text{ kg}$ (SD $\approx 0.5\text{ kg}$).  
     Prior: $\alpha \sim \mathrm{Normal}(3.5, 1.0)$.
   - **Slope $\beta_{\text{month}}$ (Monthly Weight Gain Rate in kg/month):**  
     Over the first 12 years ($144$ months), human weight increases from $\approx 3.5\text{ kg}$ to $\approx 38\text{ kg}$ (a gain of $\approx 34.5\text{ kg}$).  
     Average monthly rate: $\beta_{\text{month}} \approx \frac{34.5}{144} \approx 0.24\text{ kg/month}$.  
     To enforce strictly positive growth and preclude impossible negative weight gain rates:  
     Prior: $\beta_{\text{month}} \sim \mathrm{LogNormal}(\log(0.25), 0.3)$ (Mean $\approx 0.26\text{ kg/month}$, 89% prior interval $\approx [0.15, 0.40]\text{ kg/month}$).
   - **Residual Standard Deviation $\sigma$:**  
     Residual variability among children of the same age:  
     Prior: $\sigma \sim \mathrm{Exponential}(0.2)$ (Prior mean $= 5\text{ kg}$).
