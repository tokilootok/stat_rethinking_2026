## 1. Main thesis

The central argument is:

> **Categories are easy to encode statistically, but their coefficients and contrasts acquire meaning only after the scientific and causal question has been specified.**

A categorical regression can answer several different questions:

- How does the expected outcome differ across categories?
- How does the predictive distribution differ across categories?
- Does a continuous predictor have a different association in each category?
- What is the total causal effect associated with changing a category?
- What is the direct causal effect after holding a mediator fixed?
- Which categories must be included to identify the effect of another cause?

These questions are not interchangeable.

The workflow is:

$$
\text{scientific question}
\rightarrow
\text{causal model}
\rightarrow
\text{estimand}
\rightarrow
\text{categorical encoding}
\rightarrow
\text{Bayesian regression}
\rightarrow
\text{posterior contrast}
\rightarrow
\text{interpretation}
$$

A04 connects three ideas:

1. categorical variables require explicit statistical representation;
2. posterior contrasts should be computed directly rather than inferred from overlapping intervals;
3. the correct model depends on the causal estimand, not merely on the columns available in the dataset.

## 3. Categories are discrete and unordered

A categorical variable assigns observations to named groups.

Examples include:

- sex category;
- treatment arm;
- geographic region;
- product family;
- acquisition channel;
- customer segment;
- experimental condition.

For a nominal category, the labels have no meaningful numerical distance.

Suppose region is encoded as:

$$
1=\text{France}
$$

$$
2=\text{Germany}
$$

$$
3=\text{Spain}
$$

The regression:

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha+\beta R_i
$$

would treat these codes as measured amounts. It would impose equal increments from France to Germany and from Germany to Spain.

For a nominal variable, the codes should instead be used as names or indexes.

The central distinction is:

$$
\text{index value}
\neq
\text{measured amount}
$$

### 3.1 Nominal versus ordered categories

An ordered category has a known ranking, such as low, medium, and high. Even then, equal numerical spacing should not be assumed automatically.

A04 mainly concerns unordered categories. Ordered categorical outcomes and predictors require more specialized models later in the book and course.

## 4. The Howell height–weight–sex example

The official A04 histogram script selects adult observations from `Howell1` and compares height and weight distributions by sex category.

Let:

- $S$ denote sex category;
- $H$ denote adult height;
- $W$ denote adult weight.

The histograms describe:

$$
p(H \mid S)
$$

and:

$$
p(W \mid S)
$$

They reveal category-associated distributional differences, but they do not establish causal direction.

A plausible causal model is:

$$
S
\rightarrow
H
\rightarrow
W
$$

with an additional path:

$$
S
\rightarrow
W
$$

In this model, category affects weight partly through height and partly through other pathways.

But the data alone do not prove this graph. Other causal structures can generate similar scatterplots and histograms.

The scientific principle is:

$$
\text{causes are not contained in the data table}
$$

## 5. Structural representation

A simplified structural model is:

$$
S_i
\sim
p(S)
$$

$$
H_i
=
f_H(S_i,U_i)
$$

$$
W_i
=
f_W(H_i,S_i,V_i)
$$

where:

- $U_i$ contains unobserved causes of height;
- $V_i$ contains unobserved causes of weight.

If $U_i$ and $V_i$ are independent, they do not confound the height–weight relation.

If a common unobserved cause $C_i$ influences both:

$$
C_i
\rightarrow
H_i
$$

and:

$$
C_i
\rightarrow
W_i
$$

then the height–weight association is confounded.

A statistical regression can implement assumptions from a causal model. It cannot determine the causal model by itself.

## 6. Different questions imply different estimands

The same variables support several distinct estimands.

### 6.1 Descriptive category difference

$$
\Delta_S^{\mathrm{assoc}}
=
\mathbb{E}[W \mid S=2]
-
\mathbb{E}[W \mid S=1]
$$

This is an observed-population association.

### 6.2 Total causal effect

$$
\Delta_S^{\mathrm{total}}
=
\mathbb{E}[W \mid \operatorname{do}(S=2)]
-
\mathbb{E}[W \mid \operatorname{do}(S=1)]
$$

This includes every causal path from $S$ to $W$, including mediation through height.

### 6.3 Controlled direct effect

At a fixed height $h$:

$$
\Delta_S^{\mathrm{direct}}(h)
=
\mathbb{E}[W \mid \operatorname{do}(S=2),\operatorname{do}(H=h)]
-
\mathbb{E}[W \mid \operatorname{do}(S=1),\operatorname{do}(H=h)]
$$

This blocks the pathway through height.

### 6.4 Effect of height on weight

$$
\Delta_H(h_1,h_0)
=
\mathbb{E}[W \mid \operatorname{do}(H=h_1)]
-
\mathbb{E}[W \mid \operatorname{do}(H=h_0)]
$$

If $S$ causes both $H$ and $W$, then $S$ is a common cause that must be accounted for when estimating the effect of height.

### 6.5 Adjustment depends on the estimand

To estimate the total effect of $S$ on $W$, conditioning on mediator $H$ would remove part of the target effect.

To estimate a direct effect of $S$, conditioning on $H$ may be necessary.

To estimate the causal effect of $H$ on $W$, conditioning on common cause $S$ may be necessary.

Therefore:

$$
\text{there is no universally correct adjustment set}
$$

## 7. Binary indicator coding

Define:

$$
M_i
=
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{category 2}\\
0 & \text{category 1}
\end{cases}
$$

A baseline-plus-difference model is:

$$
Y_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma)
$$

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha+\beta_M M_i
$$

For category 1:

$$
\mu_i=\alpha
$$

For category 2:

$$
\mu_i=\alpha+\beta_M
$$

Thus:

- $\alpha$ is the expected outcome for the reference category;
- $\beta_M$ is the expected difference between category 2 and category 1.

### 7.1 The reference category is a parameterization choice

Changing which category receives zero changes the interpretation of $\alpha$ and the sign of $\beta_M$.

It need not change fitted category expectations when priors are transformed consistently.

### 7.2 Prior asymmetry

Suppose:

$$
\alpha
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(m_\alpha,s_\alpha)
$$

$$
\beta_M
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(0,s_\beta)
$$

Then:

$$
\mu_1=\alpha
$$

and:

$$
\mu_2=\alpha+\beta_M
$$

Under independent priors:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\mu_1)
=
s_\alpha^2
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\mu_2)
=
s_\alpha^2+s_\beta^2
$$

Category 2 receives greater prior uncertainty merely because it is represented as a deviation from category 1.

This is one reason McElreath favors index coding.

## 8. Index-variable coding

Let:

$$
S_i
\in
\{1,2,\ldots,K\}
$$

The integers are category addresses.

A category-specific mean model is:

$$
Y_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma)
$$

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha_{S_i}
$$

with symmetric priors:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(m_\alpha,s_\alpha)
$$

for:

$$
j=1,\ldots,K
$$

### 8.1 Advantages

Index coding:

- treats categories symmetrically;
- extends directly to many categories;
- makes category expectations easy to interpret;
- supports arbitrary posterior contrasts;
- extends naturally to category-specific slopes;
- prepares for multilevel models.

### 8.2 Rethinking-style model

```r
m_cat <- quap(
  alist(
    W ~ dnorm(mu, sigma),
    mu <- a[S],
    a[S] ~ dnorm(50, 10),
    sigma ~ dexp(1)
  ),
  data = dat
)
```

Here `S` selects the relevant element of vector `a`.

The model:

$$
\mu_i=\alpha_{S_i}
$$

is not the same as:

$$
\mu_i=\alpha+\beta S_i
$$

The first uses the integer as an index. The second treats it as a quantity.

## 9. Contrasts are posterior quantities

For categories 2 and 1:

$$
\Delta
=
\alpha_2-\alpha_1
$$

For each joint posterior draw:

$$
\Delta^{(s)}
=
\alpha_2^{(s)}-\alpha_1^{(s)}
$$

The resulting draws approximate:

$$
p(\Delta \mid D)
$$

### 9.1 Why draw-by-draw computation matters

The category parameters may be posterior-correlated.

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\alpha_2-\alpha_1)
=
\operatorname{Var}(\alpha_2)
+
\operatorname{Var}(\alpha_1)
-
2\operatorname{Cov}(\alpha_2,\alpha_1)
$$

Draw-by-draw subtraction preserves this covariance automatically.

### 9.2 R implementation

```r
post <- extract.samples(m_cat)
contrast_21 <- post$a[, 2] - post$a[, 1]
precis(contrast_21)
```

Relevant summaries may include:

- posterior mean or median;
- compatibility interval;
- probability the contrast is positive;
- probability it exceeds a practical threshold;
- expected decision loss.

---

## 10. Never infer a difference from interval overlap

It is not legitimate to inspect two marginal intervals and infer the uncertainty of their difference.

The relevant object is:

$$
p(\alpha_2-\alpha_1 \mid D)
$$

not two separate displays of:

$$
p(\alpha_1 \mid D)
$$

and:

$$
p(\alpha_2 \mid D)
$$

Intervals can overlap while the contrast is concentrated away from zero. They can also fail to overlap while a predictive individual-level contrast remains highly uncertain.

The same rule applies to:

- posterior intervals;
- confidence intervals;
- category-specific regression lines;
- probabilities;
- nonlinear predictions.

Always compute the desired contrast directly.

## 11. Difference in means versus difference between observations

Two contrasts answer different questions.

### 11.1 Expected-outcome contrast

$$
\Delta_\mu^{(s)}
=
\mu_2^{(s)}-\mu_1^{(s)}
$$

This represents uncertainty about the category mean difference.

### 11.2 Predictive contrast

Simulate a new outcome from each category:

$$
\widetilde{Y}_1^{(s)}
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\mu_1^{(s)},
\sigma^{(s)}
\right)
$$

$$
\widetilde{Y}_2^{(s)}
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\mu_2^{(s)},
\sigma^{(s)}
\right)
$$

Then:

$$
\Delta_Y^{(s)}
=
\widetilde{Y}_2^{(s)}
-
\widetilde{Y}_1^{(s)}
$$

This distribution includes individual-level residual variation and is therefore wider.

It can estimate:

$$
\Pr
\left(
\widetilde{Y}_2>\widetilde{Y}_1
\mid D
\right)
$$

A precisely estimated difference in category means can coexist with substantial overlap among individual outcomes.

## 12. One line per category

Categories can alter intercepts and slopes:

$$
W_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma)
$$

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha_{S_i}
+
\beta_{S_i}
(H_i-\overline{H})
$$

For category $j$:

- $\alpha_j$ is expected weight at average height;
- $\beta_j$ is the category-specific height–weight slope.

The expected category contrast at height $h$ is:

$$
\Delta_\mu(h)
=
\left[
\alpha_2+\beta_2(h-\overline{H})
\right]
-
\left[
\alpha_1+\beta_1(h-\overline{H})
\right]
$$

Therefore:

$$
\Delta_\mu(h)
=
(\alpha_2-\alpha_1)
+
(\beta_2-\beta_1)(h-\overline{H})
$$

When slopes differ, the category contrast is not a single number. It varies with height.

This is an interaction expressed with index variables.

---

## 13. Stratification and causal roles

The model:

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha_{S_i}
+
\beta_{S_i}x_i
$$

fits a separate conditional relationship in each category.

The category may be included because it is:

- the treatment of interest;
- a confounder;
- an effect modifier;
- a mediator;
- needed for transport or standardization;
- merely predictive.

The algebra does not identify which role applies. The causal model does.

---

## 14. Total effect of category on weight

Under:

$$
S
\rightarrow
H
\rightarrow
W
$$

and:

$$
S
\rightarrow
W
$$

a category-only model:

$$
W_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
(\alpha_{S_i},\sigma)
$$

can represent the total category difference:

$$
\Delta_S
=
\alpha_2-\alpha_1
$$

provided the observed category contrast identifies the causal contrast under the assumed design and causal model.

This total contrast includes the path through height.

If height is added:

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha_{S_i}
+
\beta(H_i-\overline{H})
$$

the category contrast now compares categories at a common height. The estimand has changed toward a direct conditional effect.

---

## 15. Estimating the effect of height

If $S$ causes both height and weight, the unadjusted height–weight relation contains the path:

$$
H
\leftarrow
S
\rightarrow
W
$$

A stratified model can close that path:

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha_{S_i}
+
\beta(H_i-\overline{H})
$$

or allow effect modification:

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha_{S_i}
+
\beta_{S_i}(H_i-\overline{H})
$$

The common-slope model assumes the effect of height is the same in every category.

The category-specific model does not.

### 15.1 Standardization

A population intervention prediction can average category-specific expectations over the target population:

$$
\mathbb{E}
[W \mid \operatorname{do}(H=h)]
=
\sum_s
\mathbb{E}[W \mid H=h,S=s]
p(S=s)
$$

This is standardization or g-computation.

A regression coefficient is often only an intermediate quantity. The estimand is the standardized intervention contrast.

---

## 16. Direct effects and mediation

Conditioning on height attempts to isolate the direct path:

$$
S
\rightarrow
W
$$

from the mediated path:

$$
S
\rightarrow
H
\rightarrow
W
$$

But a direct-effect interpretation requires strong assumptions.

There must be adequate control of confounding for:

- the category–outcome relation;
- the mediator–outcome relation;
- any treatment-induced mediator–outcome confounding.

The exact requirements depend on whether the target is:

- a controlled direct effect;
- a natural direct effect;
- an interventional direct effect;
- a descriptive conditional contrast.

A04 introduces why the model depends on the question. A06 and A07 develop confounding and controls more formally.

## 17. Full generative simulation for a total effect

A total-effect simulation can model both mediator and outcome:

$$
H_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
(\eta_{S_i},\tau)
$$

$$
W_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
(\mu_i,\sigma)
$$

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha_{S_i}
+
\beta_{S_i}
(H_i-\overline{H})
$$

For each posterior draw and intervention state $s$:

$$
H_s^{(r)}
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\eta_s^{(r)},
\tau^{(r)}
\right)
$$

$$
W_s^{(r)}
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\alpha_s^{(r)}
+
\beta_s^{(r)}
(H_s^{(r)}-\overline{H}),
\sigma^{(r)}
\right)
$$

Then:

$$
\Delta_S^{(r)}
=
W_2^{(r)}-W_1^{(r)}
$$

This propagates the category effect through the mediator as well as the outcome model.

It demonstrates why a complete causal estimator may require several linked equations rather than one coefficient.

## 18. Simulation-based validation in the official script

The official validation script defines:

$$
W_i
=
bH_i+U_i
$$

with:

$$
U_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma)
$$

It uses values such as:

$$
b=0.5
$$

and:

$$
\sigma=5
$$

The fitted model is:

$$
W_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma)
$$

$$
\mu_i
=
a+bH_i
$$

with priors on $a$, $b$, and $\sigma$.

### 18.1 Repeated recovery experiment

The script:

1. simulates a height sample;
2. simulates weights using the known slope;
3. fits the Bayesian regression;
4. stores the posterior summary for $b$;
5. repeats the process;
6. compares intervals with the true value.

For $R$ repetitions, empirical coverage is:

$$
\widehat{C}
=
\frac{1}{R}
\sum_{r=1}^{R}
\mathbb{I}
\left[
L_r
\leq
b_{\mathrm{true}}
\leq
U_r
\right]
$$

### 18.2 What the validation establishes

It can reveal:

- implementation errors;
- biased recovery;
- poor interval calibration;
- boundary problems;
- unsuitable priors at the simulated sample size;
- failure of quadratic approximation.

### 18.3 What it does not establish

It does not prove that real height and weight are generated by a linear, Gaussian, unconfounded process.

Robustness scenarios could include:

- category-specific slopes;
- nonlinear relationships;
- heavy-tailed residuals;
- measurement error;
- omitted common causes;
- mediator–outcome confounding;
- imbalanced categories.

## 19. Priors for category models

Index coding permits symmetric priors:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
(m_\alpha,s_\alpha)
$$

For slopes:

$$
\beta_j
\sim
\operatorname{LogNormal}
(m_\beta,s_\beta)
$$

when a positive direction is known beforehand, or:

$$
\beta_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
(0,s_\beta)
$$

when either direction is plausible.

### 19.1 Prior predictive contrasts

For each prior draw:

$$
\mu_j^{(r)}(h)
=
\alpha_j^{(r)}
+
\beta_j^{(r)}
(h-\overline{H})
$$

Inspect the prior distribution of:

$$
\alpha_2-\alpha_1
$$

and, with different slopes:

$$
\mu_2(h)-\mu_1(h)
$$

across relevant values of $h$.

Priors that appear reasonable coefficient by coefficient can imply implausible category differences or curves.

## 20. More than two categories

For $K$ categories:

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha_{S_i}
$$

with:

$$
S_i
\in
\{1,\ldots,K\}
$$

A pairwise contrast is:

$$
\Delta_{jk}
=
\alpha_j-\alpha_k
$$

A contrast against a weighted average is:

$$
\Delta_j
=
\alpha_j
-
\sum_{k=1}^{K}
w_k\alpha_k
$$

where:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_k=1
$$

Weights may represent:

- equal weighting;
- observed population proportions;
- a target population;
- business importance;
- policy relevance.

The category parameters are primitive model quantities. The scientific estimand determines how to combine them.

## 21. Categories have different causal roles

A categorical variable can be:

### 21.1 Treatment

Examples include no offer, €5, €10, and €20 offers.

### 21.2 Confounder

A category may influence both treatment assignment and outcome.

### 21.3 Effect modifier

$$
\tau(s)
=
\mathbb{E}
[Y(1)-Y(0) \mid S=s]
$$

### 21.4 Mediator

Treatment may alter a categorical intermediate state that affects the outcome.

### 21.5 Selection variable

Inclusion in the sample may depend on category and outcome-related causes.

### 21.6 Predictive label

A category may improve prediction without having a justified causal role.

The category's role comes from the scientific graph and study design, not from its storage type.

## 22. CRM application: discount categories

Suppose:

$$
A_i
\in
\{0,5,10,20\}
$$

Although these levels are ordered and measured in euros, a linear model:

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha+\beta A_i
$$

assumes constant response per euro.

An index model avoids that assumption:

$$
Y_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma)
$$

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha_{g_i}
$$

where $g_i$ indexes the offer level.

### 22.1 Offer-specific effects

$$
\tau_5
=
\mathbb{E}
[Y \mid \operatorname{do}(A=5)]
-
\mathbb{E}
[Y \mid \operatorname{do}(A=0)]
$$

$$
\tau_{10}
=
\mathbb{E}
[Y \mid \operatorname{do}(A=10)]
-
\mathbb{E}
[Y \mid \operatorname{do}(A=0)]
$$

$$
\tau_{20}
=
\mathbb{E}
[Y \mid \operatorname{do}(A=20)]
-
\mathbb{E}
[Y \mid \operatorname{do}(A=0)]
$$

An incremental contrast is:

$$
\tau_{10-5}
=
\mathbb{E}
[Y \mid \operatorname{do}(A=10)]
-
\mathbb{E}
[Y \mid \operatorname{do}(A=5)]
$$

### 22.2 Profit estimand

$$
\Pi(a)
=
\mathbb{E}
\left[
\text{margin}(a)
-
\text{offer cost}(a)
\right]
$$

The optimal action is:

$$
a^*
=
\arg\max_a
\mathbb{E}
[\Pi(a) \mid D]
$$

The offer with the highest purchase response may not maximize profit.

### 22.3 Confounded assignment

If high-affinity customers preferentially receive larger offers:

$$
\mathbb{E}[Y \mid A=a]
$$

does not necessarily equal:

$$
\mathbb{E}
[Y \mid \operatorname{do}(A=a)]
$$

Index coding solves representation. It does not solve confounding.

## 23. CRM application: segments and heterogeneous effects

Let $S_i$ index customer segment and $A_i$ index offer category.

A flexible model is:

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha_{S_i,A_i}
$$

The segment-specific treatment contrast is:

$$
\tau_s(a,a_0)
=
\alpha_{s,a}
-
\alpha_{s,a_0}
$$

Sparse segment–offer cells can be unstable. Later multilevel models can partially pool them:

$$
\alpha_{s,a}
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
(\overline{\alpha}_a,\sigma_a)
$$

A04 establishes the index-and-contrast logic that multilevel modeling later generalizes.

## 24. Implications for CausalOps

| A04 concept | CausalOps implication |
|---|---|
| Category code | Store labels separately from numeric indexes |
| Category role | Declare treatment, confounder, mediator, modifier, selection, or predictive role |
| Indicator coding | Record reference category and prior asymmetry |
| Index coding | Prefer symmetric category parameters when appropriate |
| Posterior contrast | Represent as a first-class estimand |
| Mean contrast | Distinguish from predictive observation contrast |
| Category-specific lines | Support vector parameters and interactions |
| Total versus direct effect | Bind adjustment set to the estimand |
| Standardization | Average conditional predictions over a target population |
| Simulation validation | Test recovery and interval coverage |
| Sparse categories | Trigger partial-pooling recommendations |
| Ordered offers | Avoid imposing linear response without justification |

### 24.3 Adjustment validation

CausalOps should flag:

- a total-effect model that conditions on a mediator;
- a direct-effect model lacking mediator–outcome confounders;
- a height-effect model that omits a common cause;
- category numbers incorrectly treated as continuous;
- causal claims based only on category coding.

The categorical model may be technically correct while the causal estimator is wrong.

## 25. Common errors exposed by A04

### Error 1: Treating category indexes as quantities

Index labels are addresses, not measured amounts.

### Error 2: Assuming dummy coding is neutral

Baseline coding changes interpretation and can induce prior asymmetry.

### Error 3: Ignoring the reference category

The coefficient's sign and meaning depend on coding.

### Error 4: Comparing interval overlap

The uncertainty of a difference must be computed directly.

### Error 5: Confusing mean and individual contrasts

Predictive differences include residual variation.

### Error 6: Adding every category as a control

A category may be a mediator, collider, or consequence of treatment.

### Error 7: Believing index coding solves confounding

It solves representation, not identification.

### Error 8: Conditioning on a mediator while claiming a total effect

Adjustment changes the estimand.

### Error 9: Omitting a common cause

An unadjusted continuous slope may be confounded.

### Error 10: Assuming a constant category contrast

Different slopes make the contrast depend on $x$.

### Error 11: Imposing a linear dose response on ordered offers

Ordering does not imply linearity.

### Error 12: Validating only one simulation

Repeated simulations are needed for bias and coverage.

## 26. Lecture takeaways

1. Nominal category codes should not be treated as measurements.
2. Indicator coding represents a reference category plus deviations.
3. Index coding gives each category its own parameter and supports symmetric priors.
4. Index coding extends directly to many categories and multilevel models.
5. Posterior contrasts should be computed draw by draw.
6. Marginal interval overlap is not a valid test of a difference.
7. A contrast of expectations differs from a contrast of new observations.
8. Category-specific slopes allow contrasts to vary over a continuous predictor.
9. Categories can be treatments, confounders, mediators, modifiers, selection variables, or predictive labels.
10. The causal role comes from the scientific model, not the variable's data type.
11. Different estimands require different adjustment sets.
12. Conditioning on a mediator changes a total-effect question.
13. A common cause must be handled when estimating another causal effect.
14. Index coding does not solve causal confounding.
15. Repeated simulation tests estimator recovery and interval calibration.
16. Ordered treatment levels need not have a linear response.
17. Regression parameters are often intermediate quantities; the final result is a posterior contrast or standardized intervention prediction.

## 29. Official code guide

### `LA04_histograms height weight sex Howell1.r`

Purpose:

- selects adult Howell observations;
- bins heights and weights;
- compares category-specific distributions;
- motivates the distinction between category association and causal structure.

Core lesson:

$$
p(H \mid S)
\quad\text{and}\quad
p(W \mid S)
$$

show association, not the direction or mechanism of causation.

### `LA04_sim heights validate example.r`

Purpose:

- defines a known linear data-generating process;
- simulates height and weight;
- fits a Bayesian Gaussian regression with `quap`;
- repeats the analysis;
- compares posterior intervals with the known slope.

Core generator:

$$
W_i
=
bH_i+U_i
$$

with:

$$
U_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma)
$$

Core validation question:

$$
\text{Does the estimator recover }b_{\mathrm{true}}
\text{ with appropriate uncertainty?}
$$
