## 1. Main thesis

The central argument is:

> **The target of an analysis and the plan used to estimate that target are different objects.**

An **estimand** states exactly what scientific quantity the analysis is intended to learn.

An **estiplan** states how the analyst intends to move from assumptions and observations to an estimate of that quantity.

The workflow is:

$$ \text{scientific question} \rightarrow \text{causal model} \rightarrow \text{estimand} \rightarrow \text{identification} \rightarrow \text{estiplan} \rightarrow \text{estimate} \rightarrow \text{diagnosis and sensitivity} $$

A fitted regression is only one component of this workflow.

A complete estiplan may include:

1. defining interventions and comparison states;
2. defining the target population;
3. selecting variables required for identification;
4. transforming variables and calibrating priors;
5. fitting one or more statistical models;
6. generating posterior predictions under interventions;
7. standardizing over a population distribution;
8. constructing the required contrast;
9. propagating posterior uncertainty;
10. checking support, fit, and estimator recovery.

The practical principle is:

$$ \text{regression coefficient} \neq \text{estimand in general} $$

A coefficient may be an intermediate model parameter while the scientific result is a posterior contrast of standardized predictions.

## 3. Four distinct objects

### 3.1 Estimand

The estimand is the quantity the study seeks to learn.

Examples include:

$$ \mathbb{E}[Y] $$

$$ \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X=x] $$

$$ \mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)] $$

$$ \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \operatorname{do}(A=a_1)] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \operatorname{do}(A=a_0)] $$

An estimand must specify enough detail to remove ambiguity about:

- the outcome;
- the intervention or exposure;
- the comparison;
- the target population;
- the aggregation rule;
- the time horizon;
- and the measurement scale.

### 3.2 Estimator

An estimator is a rule mapping possible datasets to estimates:

$$ \widehat{\tau} = g(\mathcal{D}) $$

Examples include a difference in sample means, a standardized regression contrast, or an inverse-probability weighted mean.

### 3.3 Estimate

The estimate is the output of the estimator for the observed data:

$$ \widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{obs}} = g(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}) $$

### 3.4 Estiplan

An estiplan is broader than the estimator. It is the complete planned sequence connecting the scientific model to the reported estimate.

A useful representation is:

$$ \mathcal{P} = \left( \mathcal{G}, \tau, \mathcal{I}, \mathcal{M}, \mathcal{T}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{C}, \mathcal{V} \right) $$

where:

- $\mathcal{G}$ is the causal or generative model;
- $\tau$ is the estimand;
- $\mathcal{I}$ is the identification argument;
- $\mathcal{M}$ is the statistical model or collection of models;
- $\mathcal{T}$ is the intervention or prediction transformation;
- $\mathcal{A}$ is the averaging or standardization operation;
- $\mathcal{C}$ is the final contrast;
- $\mathcal{V}$ is the validation and diagnostic program.

The same estimand can be targeted by several estiplans. The same fitted model can contribute to several estimands.

## 4. Why coefficients are often not the target

Consider:

$$ Y_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma) $$

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+\beta_A A_i+\beta_X X_i $$

It is tempting to declare:

$$ \tau=\beta_A $$

This is justified only under restrictive conditions, including:

- a linear response in $A$;
- no relevant treatment interactions;
- correct adjustment for confounding;
- no harmful post-treatment controls;
- a meaningful intervention on $A$;
- and a target matching the conditional slope.

With heterogeneous effects, the target may instead be:

$$ \tau = \mathbb{E}_X \left[ \mathbb{E}[Y \mid A=1,X] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid A=0,X] \right] $$

The regression produces conditional expectations. Prediction, averaging, and subtraction produce the estimand.

The coefficient is scaffolding. The outcome-scale posterior contrast is the scientific result.

## 5. Identification before estimation

An estimand is not automatically identifiable from observed data.

Suppose:

$$ \tau = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)] $$

Only one potential outcome is observed for each unit:

$$ Y_i = A_iY_i(1) + (1-A_i)Y_i(0) $$

Under conditional exchangeability:

$$ Y(a) \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid X $$

plus consistency and positivity, the intervention mean is identified by:

$$ \mathbb{E}[Y(a)] = \int \mathbb{E}[Y \mid A=a,X=x] p(x) \,dx $$

For discrete $X$:

$$ \mathbb{E}[Y(a)] = \sum_x \mathbb{E}[Y \mid A=a,X=x] p(x) $$

This formula already specifies the skeleton of an estiplan:

1. estimate the conditional outcome model;
2. set treatment to $a$;
3. predict for each covariate profile;
4. average over the target distribution.

Identification describes what observable quantities recover the estimand under assumptions. Estimation describes how those quantities are approximated from finite data.

## 6. The `WaffleDivorce` example

The official repository script uses the `WaffleDivorce` data for US states.

The main variables are:

- $D$: divorce rate;
- $M$: marriage rate;
- $A$: median age at marriage;
- $W$: Waffle House density;
- $S$: southern-region indicator.

The humorous marginal pattern is:

$$ W \leftrightarrow D $$

States with more Waffle Houses tend to have higher divorce rates.

This does not imply:

$$ W \rightarrow D $$

A regional variable could influence both:

$$ S \rightarrow W $$

$$ S \rightarrow D $$

The Waffle House regression can summarize geography without representing the effect of opening restaurants.

### 6.1 Several possible questions

The same dataset can support different questions:

- Which states have high predicted divorce rates?
- What is the association between Waffle House density and divorce?
- What is the effect of opening more Waffle Houses?
- What is the total effect of median age at marriage on divorce?
- What is the effect of marriage rate after accounting for age at marriage?

Each question defines a different estimand and may require a different estiplan.

---

## 7. Standardization in the divorce analysis

The official script standardizes divorce rate, marriage rate, and median age at marriage:

$$ D_i^* = \frac{D_i-\overline{D}}{s_D} $$

$$ M_i^* = \frac{M_i-\overline{M}}{s_M} $$

$$ A_i^* = \frac{A_i-\overline{A}}{s_A} $$

Standardization helps with:

- prior calibration;
- interpretation in standard-deviation units;
- numerical geometry;
- visual comparison of coefficients.

It does not:

- define the estimand;
- remove confounding;
- justify a causal graph;
- or solve lack of support.

A standardized coefficient answers a transformed-scale question. The final result may need conversion to original units for scientific or policy interpretation.

## 8. Prior predictive simulation

The script first considers very broad priors such as:

$$ \alpha \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,10) $$

$$ \beta_M \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,10) $$

$$ \beta_A \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,10) $$

For standardized variables, these priors imply extreme outcome surfaces over modest predictor values.

The script then uses tighter priors:

$$ \alpha \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,0.2) $$

$$ \beta_M \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,0.5) $$

$$ \beta_A \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,0.5) $$

with:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+\beta_M M_i^*+\beta_A A_i^* $$

Prior predictive simulation checks whether the statistical component of the estiplan generates plausible relationships before conditioning on data.

It does not validate the causal graph or adjustment set.

## 9. Three regressions, three conditional questions

### 9.1 Divorce from marriage rate

$$ D_i^* \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma) $$

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+\beta_M M_i^* $$

This estimates:

$$ \mathbb{E}[D^* \mid M^*] $$

under a linear Gaussian model.

### 9.2 Divorce from age at marriage

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+\beta_A A_i^* $$

This estimates:

$$ \mathbb{E}[D^* \mid A^*] $$

### 9.3 Divorce from both predictors

The official script fits:

$$ D_i^* \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma) $$

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+\beta_M M_i^*+\beta_A A_i^* $$

with:

$$ \sigma \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(1) $$

This estimates:

$$ \mathbb{E}[D^* \mid M^*,A^*] $$

The coefficient $\beta_M$ describes marriage-rate variation among states with similar median age at marriage, under the additive linear model.

The coefficient $\beta_A$ describes age-at-marriage variation among states with similar marriage rates.

These conditional coefficients are not automatically total causal effects.

## 10. A candidate causal graph

A common scientific interpretation is:

$$ A \rightarrow M $$

$$ A \rightarrow D $$

with a possible additional path:

$$ M \rightarrow D $$

Here:

- median age at marriage influences marriage rate;
- median age at marriage influences divorce rate;
- marriage rate may itself influence divorce rate.

This graph is an assumption informed by scientific reasoning. The observed correlations do not uniquely determine it.

The graph determines which regression and post-processing operations correspond to which estimand.

---

## 11. Estimand 1: total effect of age at marriage

Suppose the target is:

$$ \tau_A^{\mathrm{total}} = \mathbb{E} \left[ D \mid \operatorname{do}(A=a_1) \right] - \mathbb{E} \left[ D \mid \operatorname{do}(A=a_0) \right] $$

Under the candidate graph, part of the effect may operate through:

$$ A \rightarrow M \rightarrow D $$

Conditioning on $M$ blocks this mediated pathway.

Therefore, an estiplan for the total effect should not simply report $\beta_A$ from the model that conditions on $M$.

A simple total-effect estiplan is:

1. fit $D$ as a function of $A$ and valid pre-exposure confounders;
2. predict under $A=a_1$ and $A=a_0$;
3. average over the target population;
4. subtract the intervention means.

In a linear model without interactions:

$$ \tau_A^{\mathrm{total}} = \beta_A(a_1-a_0) $$

But the posterior contrast is the more general representation.

---

## 12. Estimand 2: direct effect of age at marriage

A controlled direct effect at marriage rate $m$ is:

$$ \tau_A^{\mathrm{direct}}(m) = \mathbb{E} \left[ D \mid \operatorname{do}(A=a_1), \operatorname{do}(M=m) \right] - \mathbb{E} \left[ D \mid \operatorname{do}(A=a_0), \operatorname{do}(M=m) \right] $$

An estiplan may use:

$$ \mu = \alpha+\beta_A A+\beta_M M $$

and compare predictions while holding $M=m$.

A direct-effect interpretation requires stronger assumptions than a total-effect analysis. In particular, mediator–outcome confounding must be addressed, and the precise direct-effect definition must be explicit.

The coefficient $\beta_A$ is not automatically a natural direct effect.

---

## 13. Estimand 3: effect of marriage rate

Suppose the target is:

$$ \tau_M(m_1,m_0) = \mathbb{E} \left[ D \mid \operatorname{do}(M=m_1) \right] - \mathbb{E} \left[ D \mid \operatorname{do}(M=m_0) \right] $$

Under the candidate graph, age at marriage is a common cause:

$$ M \leftarrow A \rightarrow D $$

An estiplan therefore adjusts for $A$:

1. fit $D$ conditional on $M$ and $A$;
2. set $M=m_1$ for every state while retaining each state's $A$;
3. predict divorce under that intervention;
4. repeat with $M=m_0$;
5. average each prediction set;
6. subtract.

Formally:

$$ \mathbb{E}[D \mid \operatorname{do}(M=m)] = \int \mathbb{E}[D \mid M=m,A=a] p(a) \,da $$

This is standardization.

The coefficient $\beta_M$ equals the standardized contrast per unit only under the additive linear model without interactions.

---

## 14. Estimand 4: predictive divorce risk

A predictive question differs from a causal question.

For a state with $M=m$ and $A=a$, the expected outcome is:

$$ \mathbb{E} \left[ D_{\mathrm{new}} \mid M=m,A=a,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}} \right] $$

The predictive distribution is:

$$ p \left( D_{\mathrm{new}} \mid M=m,A=a,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}} \right) $$

A predictive estiplan may include a variable solely because it improves forecasts, even when that variable would be a harmful control for a causal effect.

A variable can therefore be:

- useful for prediction;
- harmful for identification;
- or irrelevant to the chosen estimand.

Model specification cannot be judged independently of the task.

## 15. Posterior standardization

Suppose:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+\beta_A A_i+\beta_M M_i $$

For posterior draw $s$, generate counterfactual expectations under $M=m$:

$$ \mu_i^{(s)}(m) = \alpha^{(s)} + \beta_A^{(s)}A_i + \beta_M^{(s)}m $$

Average over the target population:

$$ \overline{\mu}^{(s)}(m) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mu_i^{(s)}(m) $$

Then compute:

$$ \tau_M^{(s)} = \overline{\mu}^{(s)}(m_1) - \overline{\mu}^{(s)}(m_0) $$

The collection of $\tau_M^{(s)}$ values approximates the posterior distribution of the estimand.

All transformations are performed draw by draw, preserving joint posterior uncertainty.

### 15.1 The averaging distribution is part of the target

Averaging over observed states estimates an effect for the observed state distribution.

A policy target may require:

$$ p_{\mathrm{target}}(A) $$

Then:

$$ \mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{target}}[D(m)] = \int \mathbb{E}[D \mid M=m,A=a] p_{\mathrm{target}}(a) \,da $$

The target population is part of the estimand, not an implementation detail.

---

## 16. Conditioning is not intervention

An ordinary conditional prediction asks about:

$$ p(D \mid M=m,A=a) $$

An interventional prediction asks about:

$$ p(D \mid \operatorname{do}(M=m)) $$

Conditioning describes units naturally observed with $M=m$.

Intervention represents forcing $M$ to $m$, thereby breaking the ordinary causes of $M$ while preserving the rest of the causal system.

They coincide only under identification assumptions.

The key distinction is:

$$ \text{conditioning operation} \neq \text{intervention operation} $$

Prediction software can substitute a chosen value into a model. The causal interpretation comes from the graph and assumptions, not from the substitution command.

## 17. Three plot types from Chapter 5

### 17.1 Predictor residual plots

If marriage rate is modeled from age at marriage:

$$ M_i = \gamma_0+ \gamma_A A_i+ R_i $$

then $R_i$ represents marriage-rate variation not predicted by age under the auxiliary model.

Plotting divorce against $R$ helps visualize the conditional coefficient for $M$.

This is a tool for understanding the fitted statistical model, not a complete causal analysis.

### 17.2 Posterior prediction plots

Posterior prediction plots compare fitted predictions with observations.

They help diagnose:

- prediction errors;
- outliers;
- systematic lack of fit;
- whether the model reproduces relevant patterns.

They do not establish identification.

### 17.3 Counterfactual plots

Counterfactual plots display predictions under hypothetical manipulations.

They are directly connected to causal estimands, but inherit every assumption in the graph and estiplan.

A counterfactual plot can be computed perfectly and remain scientifically wrong when the identification strategy is wrong.

## 18. Spurious association

Under:

$$ M \leftarrow A \rightarrow D $$

$M$ and $D$ can be associated even when:

$$ M \not\rightarrow D $$

The marginal regression:

$$ D \sim M $$

may therefore produce a nonzero slope when the causal effect of $M$ is zero.

The multivariable regression:

$$ D \sim M+A $$

computes a conditional association under the chosen model.

Multiple regression does not discover the correct graph automatically. It performs the comparison requested by the analyst.

---

## 19. Masked relationships

Section 5.2 presents the converse pattern: an association can be weak marginally but stronger after conditioning on another predictor.

The book's milk-energy example uses:

- $K$: milk energy;
- $N$: neocortex percentage;
- $B$: body mass.

Body mass and neocortex are associated with one another and have relationships with milk energy in opposite directions. Marginal patterns can partially cancel.

A multiple regression asks:

- among species with similar body mass, how does milk energy vary with neocortex percentage?
- among species with similar neocortex percentage, how does milk energy vary with body mass?

This is primarily a **book counterpart**. The official A05-related script is more clearly centered on the divorce example.

The lesson is that conditioning can remove spurious association or reveal masked association. Causal interpretation still depends on the graph.

## 20. One estimand, several estiplans

Suppose:

$$ \tau = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)] $$

Several plans can target it.

### 20.1 Outcome-regression estiplan

1. fit $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid A,X]$;
2. predict under $A=1$ and $A=0$;
3. standardize over $X$;
4. subtract.

### 20.2 Weighting estiplan

1. fit $p(A=1 \mid X)$;
2. construct inverse-probability weights;
3. compute weighted outcome means;
4. subtract.

### 20.3 Doubly robust estiplan

1. fit outcome and treatment models;
2. combine them in an augmented estimator;
3. compute the target contrast;
4. diagnose both components and overlap.

### 20.4 Randomized-experiment estiplan

1. verify assignment and analysis population;
2. compute arm-specific outcomes;
3. standardize when the target differs from the sample;
4. contrast arms.

The estimand remains fixed while the estimation plan changes. Each plan has different assumptions and failure modes.

## 21. One model, several estimands

Given a fitted outcome model:

$$ p(Y \mid A,X,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}) $$

one can derive several targets.

### Conditional treatment effect

$$ \tau(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid A=1,X=x] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid A=0,X=x] $$

### Sample average treatment effect

$$ \tau_{\mathrm{SATE}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \tau(X_i) $$

### Population average treatment effect

$$ \tau_{\mathrm{PATE}} = \int \tau(x)p_{\mathrm{population}}(x) \,dx $$

### Average treatment effect on the treated

$$ \tau_{\mathrm{ATT}} = \mathbb{E}[\tau(X) \mid A=1] $$

### Policy value

For policy $\pi(X)$:

$$ V(\pi) = \mathbb{E} \left[ Y\left(\pi(X)\right) \right] $$

The fitted model does not decide which output is scientifically relevant. The estimand does.

## 22. Positivity and support

A standardization estiplan requires relevant support:

$$ 0 < \Pr(A=a \mid X=x) < 1 $$

for covariate profiles in the target population.

If no states with high age at marriage also have high marriage rates, some intervention predictions may depend mainly on extrapolation.

A regression equation will still produce numbers outside the observed support.

That does not mean the estimand is reliably learned.

An estiplan should therefore include:

- treatment-support plots;
- covariate-overlap diagnostics;
- extrapolation flags;
- sensitivity to trimming;
- possible restriction of the target population.

---

## 23. Estiplan validation by simulation

A credible estiplan should be tested on synthetic data where the final estimand is known.

For simulation $r$:

1. specify a causal generative model;
2. generate intervention outcomes;
3. generate observed assignment and data;
4. execute the complete estiplan;
5. compare the estimate with the known estimand.

Let the true and estimated targets be:

$$ \tau^{(r)} $$

and:

$$ \widehat{\tau}^{(r)} $$

Then examine bias:

$$ \operatorname{Bias} = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^{R} \left( \widehat{\tau}^{(r)}-\tau^{(r)} \right) $$

and interval coverage:

$$ \widehat{C} = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^{R} \mathbb{I} \left[ L_r \leq \tau^{(r)} \leq U_r \right] $$

Validation should test the full pipeline, including prediction, standardization, and contrasts, not only recovery of regression parameters.

## 24. Common errors exposed by A05

### Error 1: Starting with the model

“Fit a multiple regression” is not a scientific question.

### Error 2: Naming a coefficient as the estimand

The target may require prediction, averaging, and contrast operations.

### Error 3: Defining an exposure without a comparison

An effect requires intervention states such as:

$$ a_1 \quad\text{versus}\quad a_0 $$

### Error 4: Omitting the target population

Sample, eligible, treated, and deployment populations can have different average effects.

### Error 5: Conditioning on a mediator for a total-effect target

The adjustment changes the estimand.

### Error 6: Omitting a common cause

A marginal association can be spurious.

### Error 7: Assuming multiple regression discovers causality

Regression computes the conditional comparison chosen by the analyst.

### Error 8: Confusing conditional prediction with intervention prediction

Setting a predictor value in software does not create causal identification.

### Error 9: Ignoring overlap

The model can extrapolate into unsupported intervention regions.

### Error 10: Reporting only parameter summaries

The scientific result often lives on the outcome scale as a posterior contrast.

### Error 11: Validating only predictive fit

Good retrodiction does not establish estimand recovery.

### Error 12: Losing uncertainty during post-processing

Predictions, averages, and contrasts should be computed draw by draw.

## 25. CRM application: discount uplift

Suppose the question is:

> What is the average 30-day incremental margin from assigning a €10 discount rather than no discount to eligible customers?

Let:

- $A=1$ denote the €10 offer;
- $A=0$ denote no offer;
- $Y$ denote 30-day net margin;
- $E=1$ denote eligibility.

### 25.1 Estimand

$$ \tau = \mathbb{E} \left[ Y(1)-Y(0) \mid E=1 \right] $$

The eligibility restriction is part of the target.

### 25.2 Identification assumption

Let $X$ contain observed common causes of offer assignment and margin, such as tenure, prior orders, product affinity, and previous campaign response.

The assumption is:

$$ Y(a) \perp\!\!\!\perp A \mid X,E=1 $$

### 25.3 Outcome-regression estiplan

1. define the eligible population;
2. fit $p(Y \mid A,X,E=1)$;
3. draw posterior parameters;
4. predict every eligible customer under $A=1$;
5. predict every eligible customer under $A=0$;
6. subtract customer-level predictions;
7. average the contrasts;
8. summarize posterior uncertainty;
9. diagnose overlap and extrapolation;
10. validate on simulated CRM data.

For posterior draw $s$:

$$ \tau^{(s)} = \frac{1}{N_E} \sum_{i:E_i=1} \left[ \mu_i^{(s)}(1) - \mu_i^{(s)}(0) \right] $$

### 25.4 Why the treatment coefficient may not be the ATE

With effect heterogeneity:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha + \beta_A A_i + \beta_X X_i + \beta_{AX}A_iX_i $$

the conditional effect is:

$$ \tau_i = \beta_A+\beta_{AX}X_i $$

The population estimand requires averaging over eligible customers.

## 26. CRM application: several offer amounts

Suppose:

$$ A \in \{0,5,10,20\} $$

A decision-relevant estimand is policy value:

$$ V(a) = \mathbb{E} \left[ Y(a)-C(a) \right] $$

where $C(a)$ includes discount and contact costs.

The incremental value is:

$$ \Delta_V(a_1,a_0) = V(a_1)-V(a_0) $$

An estiplan can:

1. model outcomes flexibly by offer and customer features;
2. predict every customer's outcome under each offer;
3. subtract offer costs;
4. average value over the target population;
5. compute posterior regret for each action;
6. choose the offer or policy with lowest expected regret.

The estimand is not “the offer coefficient.” It is the decision-relevant contrast.

```yaml
estiplan_id: gcomp_margin_v1

  adjustment_set:
    - tenure
    - prior_orders_90d

  outcome_model: bayesian_outcome_regression_v3

  - fit_outcome_model
  - predict_all_under_offer_10
  - predict_all_under_no_offer
  - contrast_customer_predictions
  - average_over_eligible_population
  - summarize_posterior

  - prior_predictive_check
  - posterior_predictive_check
  - overlap_check
  - simulation_recovery
```

### 27.3 Fail-closed logic

CausalOps should refuse or downgrade a causal estimate when:

- the intervention is undefined;
- the comparison state is missing;
- the target population is unspecified;
- no identification argument is recorded;
- required adjustment variables are unavailable;
- positivity is severely violated;
- post-treatment variables are included for a total-effect target;
- the declared estiplan fails simulation recovery.

This is the operational meaning of making the workflow auditable.

## 28. Lecture takeaways

1. An estimand is the scientific quantity to be learned.
2. An estiplan is the complete plan connecting assumptions and data to an estimate.
3. A statistical model is only one component of an estiplan.
4. Identification must be established before estimation.
5. Regression coefficients are not automatically causal effects or final estimands.
6. Prediction, averaging, and contrast operations often construct the actual target.
7. The target population is part of the estimand.
8. The Waffle House pattern illustrates how association can be detached from causal meaning.
9. Multiple regression computes conditional relationships; it does not discover the causal graph.
10. A total-effect estiplan should not condition on a mediator.
11. A confounded exposure may require adjustment and standardization.
12. Conditional prediction and intervention prediction are different operations.
13. Predictor residual plots explain statistical conditioning, not causality.
14. Posterior prediction plots assess fit and prediction, not identification.
15. Counterfactual plots express causal implications only under the assumed graph.
16. One estimand can be targeted by several estiplans.
17. One fitted model can support several estimands through different averaging distributions and contrasts.
18. Support and positivity are properties of the estiplan, not optional diagnostics.
19. Simulation should test recovery of the final estimand, not only model parameters.
20. An auditable causal system must store estimand and estiplan separately.

## 31. Official code guide

### `05_elemental_confounds.r`

The script contains material shared with the transition into A06. The portion most directly relevant to A05 is the divorce analysis.

It:

- loads `WaffleDivorce`;
- plots Waffle House density against divorce rate;
- plots marriage rate against divorce;
- plots median age at marriage against divorce;
- plots median age at marriage against marriage rate;
- visualizes stratification over a continuous variable;
- standardizes the principal variables;
- performs prior predictive checks;
- fits the multivariable divorce model.

The fitted model is:

$$ D_i^* \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma) $$

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+\beta_M M_i^*+\beta_A A_i^* $$

with:

$$ \alpha \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,0.2) $$

$$ \beta_M \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,0.5) $$

$$ \beta_A \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,0.5) $$

$$ \sigma \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(1) $$

The same script also contains fork, pipe, collider, descendant, selection-bias, and d-separation simulations. Those sections are developed more directly in A06 and A07.
