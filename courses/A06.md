## 1. Main thesis

The central argument is:

> **Conditioning is a causal operation on an assumed graph, not a universally beneficial statistical refinement.**

Adding a variable to a regression changes which comparisons are made. Depending on the variable's position in the causal graph, conditioning may:

- close a noncausal backdoor path;
- block part of the causal effect of interest;
- open a path that was previously closed;
- create selection distortion;
- or partially condition on an unobserved structure through a descendant or proxy.

The workflow is:

$$
\text{scientific question}
\rightarrow
\text{causal DAG}
\rightarrow
\text{enumerate paths}
\rightarrow
\text{classify open and closed paths}
\rightarrow
\text{identify backdoor paths}
\rightarrow
\text{choose an adjustment set}
\rightarrow
\text{estimate the target contrast}
$$

The statistical model does not determine the adjustment set. The causal model does.

## 3. Conditioning

Conditioning means restricting comparisons to observations with the same or similar values of another variable.

In a regression such as:

$$
Y_i
=
\alpha
+
\beta_X X_i
+
\beta_Z Z_i
+
\varepsilon_i
$$

$\beta_X$ describes variation in $Y$ associated with variation in $X$ while holding $Z$ fixed under the model.

This is a statistical statement.

Its causal meaning depends on what $Z$ is:

- a common cause;
- a mediator;
- a collider;
- a descendant;
- a proxy;
- or an irrelevant predictor.

The same algebra can therefore support a valid causal comparison, a different estimand, or a biased comparison.

---

## 4. Paths and information flow

A **path** is any sequence of connected edges between two variables, ignoring arrow direction while tracing the sequence.

Suppose the exposure is $X$ and the outcome is $Y$.

A path can transmit statistical association when it is open.

A path is blocked when its structure and conditioning status prevent information from flowing through it.

The elementary structures are:

1. fork;
2. pipe;
3. collider;
4. descendant of another node, especially a collider.

Every larger DAG can be analyzed by decomposing its paths into these local structures.

---

## 5. The fork

A fork has the structure:

$$
X
\leftarrow
Z
\rightarrow
Y
$$

$Z$ is a common cause of $X$ and $Y$.

### 5.1 Marginal association

Even when $X$ has no causal effect on $Y$, they can be associated because both respond to $Z$:

$$
X
\not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp
Y
$$

### 5.2 Conditioning on the common cause

Conditioning on $Z$ closes the path:

$$
X
\perp\!\!\!\perp
Y
\mid Z
$$

This is the elementary confounding pattern.

### 5.3 Simulation in the official script

The script generates:

$$
Z
\sim
\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.5)
$$

and makes both $X$ and $Y$ strongly depend on $Z$.

Marginally, $X$ and $Y$ are correlated. Within $Z=0$ and within $Z=1$, the correlation largely disappears.

The adjustment works because $Z$ blocks an open backdoor path.

---

## 6. The pipe

A pipe has the structure:

$$
X
\rightarrow
Z
\rightarrow
Y
$$

$Z$ mediates the effect of $X$ on $Y$.

### 6.1 Unconditioned path

The path is open, so changes in $X$ can propagate through $Z$ to $Y$.

The total effect includes this mediated pathway.

### 6.2 Conditioning on the mediator

Conditioning on $Z$ blocks the pipe:

$$
X
\perp\!\!\!\perp
Y
\mid Z
$$

when the pipe is the only path connecting them.

This is useful only when the estimand deliberately excludes the mediated effect.

### 6.3 Total versus direct effect

If the graph also contains:

$$
X
\rightarrow
Y
$$

then the total effect combines:

$$
X
\rightarrow
Y
$$

and:

$$
X
\rightarrow
Z
\rightarrow
Y
$$

Conditioning on $Z$ removes the second component and changes the estimand.

The lesson is:

$$
\text{blocking a path}
\neq
\text{removing bias automatically}
$$

It may remove part of the effect we intended to estimate.

---

## 7. The collider

A collider has the structure:

$$
X
\rightarrow
Z
\leftarrow
Y
$$

$Z$ is a common consequence of $X$ and $Y$.

### 7.1 Unconditioned collider

The path is closed by default.

If $X$ and $Y$ have no other connection, then:

$$
X
\perp\!\!\!\perp
Y
$$

### 7.2 Conditioning on the collider

Conditioning on $Z$ opens the path:

$$
X
\not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp
Y
\mid Z
$$

Once the value of the common consequence is known, information about one cause changes what is inferred about the other.

### 7.3 Intuition

Suppose admission depends on both academic strength and athletic ability.

Among admitted applicants, learning that one person has lower academic strength implies that stronger athletic ability is more likely to explain admission.

The causes become associated inside the selected group even when they were independent in the applicant population.

This is selection distortion.

---

## 8. Collider bias is created, not removed

Confounder adjustment removes an existing noncausal path.

Collider adjustment creates a new noncausal path.

The contrast is:

### Confounder

$$
X
\leftarrow
Z
\rightarrow
Y
$$

Conditioning on $Z$ closes the path.

### Collider

$$
X
\rightarrow
Z
\leftarrow
Y
$$

Conditioning on $Z$ opens the path.

This is why variable selection cannot be based on correlation, predictive strength, or temporal proximity alone.

The arrow structure matters.

---

## 9. Descendants

A descendant is any variable causally downstream of another variable.

Suppose:

$$
X
\rightarrow
Z
\leftarrow
Y
$$

and:

$$
Z
\rightarrow
D
$$

$D$ is a descendant of the collider $Z$.

Conditioning on $D$ provides information about $Z$ and can therefore open the collider path partially:

$$
X
\rightarrow
Z
\leftarrow
Y
$$

The degree of induced association depends on how informative $D$ is about $Z$.

This is important because analysts often lack direct measurements and use:

- proxy variables;
- scores;
- eligibility flags;
- downstream behaviors;
- administrative labels.

A proxy inherits some of the causal consequences of the variable it measures.

---

## 10. D-separation

D-separation is the graphical rule for determining conditional independence in a DAG.

Two variables are d-separated given a conditioning set $S$ when every path between them is blocked.

The local rules are:

### Fork

$$
X
\leftarrow
Z
\rightarrow
Y
$$

Open unless $Z$ is conditioned on.

### Pipe

$$
X
\rightarrow
Z
\rightarrow
Y
$$

Open unless $Z$ is conditioned on.

### Collider

$$
X
\rightarrow
Z
\leftarrow
Y
$$

Closed unless $Z$ or one of its descendants is conditioned on.

The rules are local, but their implications apply to full paths in large DAGs.

---

## 11. Causal paths and backdoor paths

A causal path from $X$ to $Y$ follows arrows away from $X$ toward $Y$.

For example:

$$
X
\rightarrow
M
\rightarrow
Y
$$

A backdoor path begins with an arrow entering $X$.

For example:

$$
X
\leftarrow
C
\rightarrow
Y
$$

Backdoor paths create associations that do not arise from the causal effect of $X$ on $Y$.

To identify the total effect of $X$ on $Y$, an adjustment set should:

1. close every open backdoor path;
2. avoid blocking causal paths from $X$ to $Y$;
3. avoid opening paths through colliders or collider descendants.

---

## 12. The backdoor-analysis recipe

The second edition gives a practical graph-analysis procedure.

### Step 1: List all paths

Enumerate every path connecting the exposure $X$ and outcome $Y$.

### Step 2: Classify each path

Determine whether each path is open or closed under the proposed conditioning set.

### Step 3: Identify backdoor paths

A backdoor path has an arrow pointing into $X$ at its first edge.

### Step 4: Close open backdoors

Choose observed variables that close every open backdoor path without opening new ones.

### Step 5: Preserve the estimand

For a total effect, do not condition on descendants of $X$ that lie on causal pathways to $Y$.

### Step 6: Verify graph implications

Software such as `dagitty` can enumerate adjustment sets after the causal graph has been supplied.

The software checks the graph's logic. It does not validate the graph scientifically.

---

## 13. Confounding is a path problem

It is common to call a variable “a confounder.”

A more precise statement is that a variable lies on an open backdoor path that confounds a specified exposure–outcome effect.

The same variable can have different roles for different estimands.

For example, in:

$$
A
\rightarrow
M
\rightarrow
Y
$$

$M$ is a mediator for the effect of $A$ on $Y$.

But if another exposure $X$ affects $M$, and $M$ affects both $X$ and $Y$ in a different graph, its role may differ.

Causal roles are relational:

$$
\text{role}
=
f(
\text{graph},
\text{exposure},
\text{outcome},
\text{estimand}
)
$$

No variable has one permanent role independent of the question.

## 14. Fork simulation

The official script uses a binary common cause:

$$
Z
\sim
\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.5)
$$

and generates $X$ and $Y$ from probabilities that differ strongly by $Z$.

A stylized version is:

$$
X
\sim
\operatorname{Bernoulli}
\left(
0.1+0.8Z
\right)
$$

$$
Y
\sim
\operatorname{Bernoulli}
\left(
0.1+0.8Z
\right)
$$

There is no arrow from $X$ to $Y$.

Nevertheless:

$$
\operatorname{Cor}(X,Y)
\neq 0
$$

Marginally, $X$ predicts $Y$ because it carries information about $Z$.

Within strata of $Z$:

$$
\operatorname{Cor}(X,Y \mid Z=z)
\approx 0
$$

The simulation shows how adjustment removes common-cause association.

---

## 15. Pipe simulation

The official script generates:

$$
X
\rightarrow
Z
\rightarrow
Y
$$

A stylized form is:

$$
X
\sim
\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.5)
$$

$$
Z
\sim
\operatorname{Bernoulli}
\left(
0.1+0.8X
\right)
$$

$$
Y
\sim
\operatorname{Bernoulli}
\left(
0.1+0.8Z
\right)
$$

Marginally, $X$ and $Y$ are associated because $X$ affects $Y$ through $Z$.

Within a fixed level of $Z$, the association disappears because the mediated pathway is blocked.

This is not confounding removal. It is removal of the causal pathway.

---

## 16. Collider simulation

The official script generates independent $X$ and $Y$ and makes $Z$ likely when either cause is present:

$$
X
\sim
\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.5)
$$

$$
Y
\sim
\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.5)
$$

$$
Z
\sim
\operatorname{Bernoulli}
\left[
\Pr(Z=1 \mid X,Y)
\right]
$$

with a high probability when:

$$
X+Y>0
$$

Marginally:

$$
\operatorname{Cor}(X,Y)
\approx 0
$$

Within $Z=1$ or $Z=0$:

$$
\operatorname{Cor}(X,Y \mid Z)
\neq 0
$$

Conditioning creates the association.

---

## 17. Selection distortion in grant proposals

Chapter 6 and the official script simulate two independent proposal characteristics:

- newsworthiness;
- trustworthiness.

Let:

$$
N_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(0,1)
$$

$$
T_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(0,1)
$$

with:

$$
N_i
\perp\!\!\!\perp
T_i
$$

A selection score is:

$$
S_i
=
N_i+T_i
$$

Only proposals above a high threshold are selected.

The graph is:

$$
N
\rightarrow
S
\leftarrow
T
$$

Conditioning on selection opens the collider.

Among selected proposals, high newsworthiness can compensate for lower trustworthiness and vice versa. A negative association appears even though none exists in the full population.

This is also known as Berkson-type selection bias.

---

## 18. Selection can occur before modeling

Collider conditioning does not require explicitly adding the collider as a regression predictor.

It can occur because the dataset was selected.

Examples include analyses restricted to:

- funded proposals;
- admitted students;
- hospitalized patients;
- employed workers;
- customers who opened an email;
- customers who purchased;
- cases that reached customer support;
- published studies.

The selection criterion can act as an implicit conditioned variable.

Therefore, causal analysis must model how observations entered the dataset.

## 19. Happiness, age, and marriage

The official script animates a happiness example in which marriage status is influenced by age and happiness.

A simplified graph is:

$$
A
\rightarrow
M
\leftarrow
H
$$

where:

- $A$ is age;
- $H$ is happiness;
- $M$ is marriage status.

If age and happiness are otherwise independent, the collider $M$ blocks the path marginally.

Conditioning on marriage status opens it.

Within married people and within unmarried people, age can appear negatively associated with happiness even when age has no causal effect on happiness in the simulation.

The regression is not numerically malfunctioning. It is answering a conditioned question created by the analyst.

## 20. The haunted DAG

A DAG is “haunted” when an unobserved cause changes the role of an observed variable.

The official `06_breen_collider_animation.R` script uses:

- $G$: grandparent education;
- $P$: parent education;
- $C$: child education;
- $U$: an unobserved neighborhood or shared influence.

The structural relations include:

$$
G
\rightarrow
P
$$

$$
P
\rightarrow
C
$$

$$
U
\rightarrow
P
$$

$$
U
\rightarrow
C
$$

The script sets the direct effect:

$$
G
\rightarrow
C
$$

at zero.

### 20.1 Why conditioning on parent education is dangerous

$P$ is a collider on:

$$
G
\rightarrow
P
\leftarrow
U
\rightarrow
C
$$

Conditioning on $P$ opens this path.

Even though the true direct effect of $G$ on $C$ is zero, a regression of $C$ on both $G$ and $P$ can estimate a nonzero coefficient for $G$.

The observed parent variable becomes a collider because of an unobserved cause.

### 20.2 Animation logic

The script moves across narrow bands of parent education and fits the grandparent–child relationship within each band.

Within a fixed parental-education band, grandparent education carries information about the unobserved cause $U$.

The induced slope is a selection artifact, not a direct causal effect.

## 21. Why unobserved variables still matter

A variable need not be measured to affect identification.

If $U$ causes two observed variables, its arrows remain part of the scientific graph even when no column records $U$.

This matters because:

- an observed variable may become a collider because of $U$;
- an apparent adjustment set may fail to close a backdoor path;
- conditioning on an observed proxy may partially condition on $U$;
- no amount of sample size can repair a structurally unidentified effect.

The absence of $U$ from the data does not remove it from the world.

---

## 22. The Table 2 fallacy

A multivariable regression table often lists one coefficient for every predictor and invites a causal interpretation of each coefficient.

This is unsafe.

A single model may include variables with different causal roles:

- exposure of interest;
- common cause;
- mediator;
- collider;
- proxy;
- precision predictor.

The adjustment set appropriate for estimating one coefficient may be inappropriate for another coefficient in the same table.

Therefore, a regression table is not a list of independently identified causal effects.

Each causal effect needs its own estimand and adjustment argument.

## 23. Multicollinearity as an identification warning

Chapter 6 begins with multicollinearity.

Suppose two predictors are nearly redundant:

$$
X_1
\approx
X_2
$$

and the model is:

$$
Y_i
=
\alpha
+
\beta_1X_{1i}
+
\beta_2X_{2i}
+
\varepsilon_i
$$

The data may identify the sum:

$$
\beta_1+\beta_2
$$

more precisely than either coefficient separately.

The posterior may show strong negative covariance:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}
\left(
\beta_1,
\beta_2
\mid D
\right)
<0
$$

This is not necessarily bias. It can be an honest statement that the data cannot distinguish the separate contributions.

Prediction can remain good while parameter interpretation is weak.

The A06 scripts do not foreground the book's leg-length and milk examples, so this section is included as chapter context rather than claimed as a central recorded example.

## 24. Post-treatment bias

A post-treatment variable is caused by the exposure.

Suppose:

$$
X
\rightarrow
Z
\rightarrow
Y
$$

and the target is the total effect of $X$ on $Y$.

Conditioning on $Z$ blocks the mediated pathway.

Worse, if an unobserved variable $U$ affects both $Z$ and $Y$:

$$
X
\rightarrow
Z
\leftarrow
U
\rightarrow
Y
$$

then conditioning on $Z$ also opens a collider path.

A post-treatment control can therefore:

1. remove part of the true effect;
2. introduce new bias.

The detailed comparison of bad-control types is continued in A07.

## 25. Adjustment sets are estimand-specific

Consider:

$$
X
\rightarrow
M
\rightarrow
Y
$$

and:

$$
C
\rightarrow
X
$$

$$
C
\rightarrow
Y
$$

For the total effect of $X$ on $Y$:

- adjust for $C$;
- do not adjust for $M$.

For a controlled direct effect of $X$ not through $M$:

- $M$ must be fixed or modeled appropriately;
- additional mediation assumptions are required;
- simply adding $M$ to a regression may be insufficient.

The phrase “control for confounders” is incomplete until the estimand is stated.

## 26. Regression implementation after identification

Suppose the DAG implies that $C$ is a sufficient adjustment variable for the total effect of $X$ on $Y$.

A Gaussian outcome model could be:

$$
Y_i
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma)
$$

$$
\mu_i
=
\alpha
+
\beta_X X_i
+
\beta_C C_i
$$

But the causal estimate is preferably computed through posterior intervention predictions.

For posterior draw $s$:

$$
\mu_i^{(s)}(x)
=
\alpha^{(s)}
+
\beta_X^{(s)}x
+
\beta_C^{(s)}C_i
$$

Average over the target population:

$$
\overline{\mu}^{(s)}(x)
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\mu_i^{(s)}(x)
$$

Then compute:

$$
\tau^{(s)}
=
\overline{\mu}^{(s)}(x_1)
-
\overline{\mu}^{(s)}(x_0)
$$

The DAG selects the adjustment variables. The statistical model and posterior post-processing implement the estiplan.

## 27. DAG software

The book uses `dagitty` to inspect adjustment sets.

A graph may be defined as:

```r
dag <- dagitty("dag {
  X -> Y
  X <- C -> Y
}")

adjustmentSets(
  dag,
  exposure = "X",
  outcome = "Y"
)
```

The software can determine whether $C$ closes the backdoor path in the supplied graph.

It cannot determine whether the scientific claim:

$$
C
\rightarrow
X
$$

and:

$$
C
\rightarrow
Y
$$

is correct.

The graph remains a scientific model that must be defended, criticized, and subjected to sensitivity analysis.

## 28. CRM application: targeting confounding

Let:

- $A$: retention campaign;
- $Y$: future revenue or churn;
- $L$: observed customer risk and history.

The graph is:

$$
L
\rightarrow
A
$$

$$
L
\rightarrow
Y
$$

$$
A
\rightarrow
Y
$$

The path:

$$
A
\leftarrow
L
\rightarrow
Y
$$

is an open backdoor path.

Conditioning on $L$ closes it under the assumption that $L$ contains all relevant common causes.

The identifying assumption is:

$$
Y(a)
\perp\!\!\!\perp
A
\mid L
$$

This is a fork where adjustment is useful.

## 29. CRM application: campaign engagement as mediator

Suppose:

- $A$: campaign sent;
- $M$: post-campaign engagement;
- $Y$: revenue.

The graph is:

$$
A
\rightarrow
M
\rightarrow
Y
$$

with a possible direct path:

$$
A
\rightarrow
Y
$$

For the total campaign effect, conditioning on $M$ blocks part of the effect.

The surviving coefficient compares campaign assignments among customers with the same realized engagement, a post-treatment comparison.

That is not the total-effect estimand.

## 30. CRM application: openers-only analysis

Suppose email opening depends on both campaign delivery and latent digital attention:

$$
A
\rightarrow
O
\leftarrow
U
\rightarrow
Y
$$

where:

- $O$: email opened;
- $U$: latent attention;
- $Y$: purchase or revenue.

Analyzing only openers conditions on $O$ and opens:

$$
A
\rightarrow
O
\leftarrow
U
\rightarrow
Y
$$

Among openers, campaign status becomes associated with latent attention.

The analysis can make a useful campaign appear harmful or an ineffective campaign appear beneficial.

The openers-only population is not merely smaller. It has a different dependence structure.

## 31. CRM application: support-contact selection

Suppose both service severity and customer persistence cause a support contact:

$$
S
\rightarrow
C
\leftarrow
P
$$

and persistence also affects retention:

$$
P
\rightarrow
Y
$$

Restricting analysis to customers who contacted support conditions on $C$.

Within support cases, severity and persistence become associated. An apparent relationship between severity and retention may therefore be distorted.

This is especially relevant when CRM datasets are assembled from event tables that record only customers who triggered a workflow.

## 32. Implications for CausalOps

A06 suggests that CausalOps must reason about paths, not merely lists of covariates.

| A06 concept | CausalOps implication |
|---|---|
| Fork | Candidate backdoor path requiring adjustment |
| Pipe | Mediated causal path that should remain open for a total effect |
| Collider | Variable that should not be conditioned on by default |
| Descendant | Proxy or downstream selection variable requiring inherited-role analysis |
| Backdoor path | First-class graph object in identification reports |
| Adjustment set | Linked to one exposure, outcome, and estimand |
| D-separation | Automated graph implication engine |
| Haunted DAG | Unobserved-variable sensitivity scenarios |
| Selection distortion | Sample-construction audit |
| Table 2 fallacy | Prevent causal labels on all coefficients in one model |
| Multicollinearity | Identifiability warning rather than automatic variable deletion |
| Simulation | Verify bias under alternative conditioning sets |

### 32.1 Path audit artifact

```yaml
exposure: campaign_sent
outcome: revenue_30d
estimand: total_effect
paths:
  - path: campaign_sent <- churn_risk -> revenue_30d
    type: backdoor
    status_without_adjustment: open
    action: close
    candidate_controls:
      - churn_risk
  - path: campaign_sent -> email_opened -> revenue_30d
    type: causal_mediation
    status_without_adjustment: open
    action: preserve
  - path: campaign_sent -> email_opened <- digital_attention -> revenue_30d
    type: collider_backdoor
    status_without_adjustment: closed
    action: do_not_open
```

### 32.2 Control-role validation

Before a variable enters the model, CausalOps should record whether it is:

- a measured common cause;
- a treatment descendant;
- a mediator;
- a collider;
- a collider descendant;
- an outcome cause only;
- an exposure cause only;
- a proxy for an unobserved variable.

The role must be evaluated relative to the current exposure, outcome, and estimand.

### 32.3 Selection audit

The platform should ask:

- Which events caused a row to enter the modeling table?
- Was the sample restricted to responders, purchasers, openers, claimants, or support users?
- Is eligibility caused by treatment or outcome determinants?
- Are missing records descendants of treatment and latent risk?

Sample filters can condition on colliders even when the regression formula does not.

## 33. Common errors exposed by A06

### Error 1: Adding every predictor

More controls can create bias or change the estimand.

### Error 2: Defining confounders from correlation

Confounding is determined by the causal path structure.

### Error 3: Conditioning on a mediator for a total effect

This blocks part of the causal effect.

### Error 4: Conditioning on a collider

This opens a noncausal path.

### Error 5: Ignoring collider descendants

A proxy or downstream flag can partially open the same path.

### Error 6: Treating sample selection as harmless

Filtering can condition on a collider before modeling begins.

### Error 7: Assuming an unmeasured variable is irrelevant

Unobserved causes can change the role of observed variables.

### Error 8: Interpreting every regression coefficient causally

Each coefficient requires its own identification argument.

### Error 9: Using software to discover the scientific DAG

Graph software evaluates a supplied graph; it does not validate causal arrows.

### Error 10: Blocking all associations

Some open paths are the causal effect and should remain open.

### Error 11: Treating multicollinearity as bias

It often indicates weak separate identification rather than a biased posterior.

### Error 12: Validating only the final fitted model

The adjustment strategy should be tested in simulations with known causal truth.

## 34. Lecture takeaways

1. Conditioning changes the statistical paths through which information flows.
2. A fork is open until its common cause is conditioned on.
3. A pipe is open until its mediator is conditioned on.
4. A collider is closed until the collider or a descendant is conditioned on.
5. Confounder adjustment closes a noncausal backdoor path.
6. Mediator adjustment can remove part of the target total effect.
7. Collider adjustment creates a noncausal association.
8. Descendants and proxies can inherit collider problems.
9. D-separation formalizes conditional independence in a DAG.
10. A backdoor path begins with an arrow entering the exposure.
11. A valid total-effect adjustment set closes open backdoors while preserving causal paths.
12. Selection into a dataset can condition on a collider implicitly.
13. The grant-proposal example demonstrates selection distortion.
14. The happiness–marriage example shows collider bias inside strata.
15. The grandparent–parent–child example shows how an unobserved cause can haunt a DAG.
16. Regression can faithfully estimate the wrong conditioned association.
17. Multicollinearity can make separate parameters weakly identified while preserving prediction.
18. Adjustment sets are specific to an exposure, outcome, and estimand.
19. Graph software checks implications of assumptions; it does not supply the assumptions.
20. CausalOps must audit both regression controls and sample-construction filters.

## 37. Official code guide

### `05_elemental_confounds.r`

The script includes:

- binary fork simulation;
- binary pipe simulation;
- binary collider simulation;
- the `WaffleDivorce` transition from A05;
- grant-proposal selection distortion;
- happiness, age, and marriage animation;
- descendant simulation;
- continuous d-separation plots for fork, pipe, and collider structures.

Core lessons:

$$
\text{fork and pipe}
\xrightarrow{\text{condition on middle}}
\text{closed}
$$

$$
\text{collider}
\xrightarrow{\text{condition on middle}}
\text{open}
$$

### `06_breen_collider_animation.R`

The script simulates:

$$
G
\rightarrow
P
\rightarrow
C
$$

with an unobserved common cause:

$$
U
\rightarrow
P
$$

$$
U
\rightarrow
C
$$

and sets the direct $G \rightarrow C$ effect to zero.

It then fits a regression conditioning on $P$ and animates the induced grandparent–child relationship within narrow parental-education bands.

### `06_DAG_animations.r`

The script contains DAG animation infrastructure and test examples, including:

- a complex graph with several backdoor paths;
- smoking-related Table 2 fallacy graphs;
- versions with and without an unobserved cause.

Because much of the file is inside a disabled test block, it is best treated as supplementary lecture-animation support rather than evidence that every contained example was presented in A06.

### `06_simulations_bad_controls.r`

This script simulates:

- post-treatment confounding;
- case-control or outcome-descendant bias;
- precision parasites;
- bias amplification.

These examples are more directly aligned with A07 and are therefore only previewed in this guide.
