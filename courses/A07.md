## 1. Main thesis

The central argument is:

> **A control variable is good or bad only relative to a causal graph, an exposure, an outcome, and an estimand.**

There is no universal rule that more controls are better.

A control can:

- remove confounding;
- improve precision;
- leave the estimand unchanged but reduce precision;
- block part of the causal effect;
- open a collider path;
- amplify bias from an unmeasured common cause;
- or redefine the target population through selection.

The workflow is:

$$ \text{causal question} \rightarrow \text{estimand} \rightarrow \text{causal DAG} \rightarrow \text{candidate control} \rightarrow \text{path analysis} \rightarrow \text{bias and precision analysis} \rightarrow \text{simulation validation} \rightarrow \text{estimation} $$

The practical lesson is:

$$ \text{predictive association with }Y \not\Rightarrow \text{valid causal control} $$

and:

$$ \text{measured before treatment} \not\Rightarrow \text{safe to control} $$

### 2.1 Two questions that must remain separate

A control-selection question asks:

> Does including $Z$ help identify the causal effect of $X$ on $Y$?

A predictive-model question asks:

> Does including $Z$ improve predictions for new observations?

The answers can differ.

A collider may improve prediction while damaging causal identification.

A valid confounder may be essential for causal identification even if it barely improves predictive accuracy.

A variable can be causally neutral but increase the variance of the treatment-effect estimate.

## 3. What statistical control means

Consider:

$$ Y_i \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma) $$

$$ \mu_i = \alpha + \beta_X X_i + \beta_Z Z_i $$

Including $Z$ means that $\beta_X$ is identified from variation in $X$ among units with the same or similar values of $Z$, under the model.

This is statistical control.

It is not equivalent to experimental control.

An experiment manipulates or holds constant aspects of the data-generating process.

A regression conditions on recorded values after the data have been generated.

The word “control” can hide this difference.

---

## 4. Control quality has two dimensions

A candidate control should be evaluated along at least two dimensions.

### 4.1 Bias

Does conditioning on $Z$ move the estimate toward or away from the target causal effect?

A simple repeated-sampling bias measure is:

$$ \mathrm{Bias} \left( \widehat{\tau} \right) = \mathbb{E} \left[ \widehat{\tau} \right] - \tau $$

### 4.2 Precision

How variable is the estimate across repeated samples?

$$ \mathrm{Var} \left( \widehat{\tau} \right) $$

A control may be unbiased but imprecise, or biased but numerically stable.

Mean squared error combines both:

$$ \mathrm{MSE} \left( \widehat{\tau} \right) = \mathrm{Bias} \left( \widehat{\tau} \right)^2 + \mathrm{Var} \left( \widehat{\tau} \right) $$

Causal identification places bias first. Precision cannot rescue a systematically wrong estimand.

## 5. A genuinely good control: an observed common cause

Suppose:

$$ Z \rightarrow X $$

$$ Z \rightarrow Y $$

$$ X \rightarrow Y $$

The backdoor path is:

$$ X \leftarrow Z \rightarrow Y $$

It is open without adjustment.

Conditioning on $Z$ closes it.

Under an adequate causal graph and measurement model:

$$ Y(x) \perp\!\!\!\perp X \mid Z $$

The g-formula is:

$$ \mathbb{E}[Y(x)] = \int \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X=x,Z=z] p(z) \,dz $$

Here $Z$ is a good control for the total effect of $X$ on $Y$ because it removes noncausal association while preserving the causal path.

### 5.1 Good does not mean perfect

Adjustment can still fail when:

- $Z$ is measured with error;
- important common causes remain unobserved;
- positivity is weak;
- the conditional outcome model is misspecified;
- the target population differs from the observed one.

A good graphical role is necessary but not sufficient for a reliable estimate.

---

## 6. A useful precision variable

Suppose $Z$ predicts $Y$ but is independent of $X$:

$$ Z \rightarrow Y $$

$$ X \rightarrow Y $$

and there is no path between $Z$ and $X$.

Conditioning on $Z$ is not required to remove confounding.

But it can explain outcome variation unrelated to $X$, reducing residual variance.

In a linear model, this can increase precision for $\beta_X$.

This is a neutral control for bias but potentially beneficial for precision.

The exact gain depends on:

- how strongly $Z$ predicts $Y$;
- sample size;
- model complexity;
- measurement quality;
- whether interactions are required.

This case provides a contrast with the official precision-parasite simulation, where $Z$ predicts $X$ but not $Y$ except through $X$.

---

## 7. Bad control 1: post-treatment confounding

The first official bad-control simulation is:

$$ X \rightarrow Z $$

$$ U \rightarrow Z $$

$$ U \rightarrow Y $$

with a possible path:

$$ Z \rightarrow Y $$

The simulated equations are:

$$ X \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0,1) $$

$$ U \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0,1) $$

$$ Z \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( \beta_{XZ}X+U, 1 \right) $$

$$ Y \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( \beta_{ZY}Z+U, 1 \right) $$

The script evaluates the especially revealing case:

$$ \beta_{ZY}=0 $$

Then the true total effect of $X$ on $Y$ is zero.

### 7.1 Unadjusted model

The regression:

$$ Y \sim X $$

recovers an effect near zero because $X$ is independent of $U$.

### 7.2 Adjusted model

The regression:

$$ Y \sim X+Z $$

conditions on the collider:

$$ X \rightarrow Z \leftarrow U \rightarrow Y $$

This opens a noncausal path from $X$ to $Y$.

The adjusted coefficient becomes biased even though the added variable is strongly related to the exposure and may be strongly related to the outcome.

### 7.3 Why “post-treatment” is not the complete explanation

The deepest problem is the path structure.

$Z$ is post-treatment, but it is harmful here because conditioning:

1. may block mediation when $\beta_{ZY}\neq 0$;
2. opens the collider path through $U$.

Temporal order is a useful warning, but the DAG explains the bias.

---

## 8. Blocking mediation and creating bias simultaneously

When:

$$ \beta_{ZY}\neq 0 $$

$Z$ is also a mediator:

$$ X \rightarrow Z \rightarrow Y $$

For the total effect of $X$ on $Y$, conditioning on $Z$ does two things.

### It blocks a causal path

$$ X \rightarrow Z \rightarrow Y $$

### It opens a noncausal path

$$ X \rightarrow Z \leftarrow U \rightarrow Y $$

Therefore, the adjusted coefficient is not simply a clean direct effect.

Direct-effect estimation requires a dedicated estimand and stronger assumptions. Adding a mediator to an ordinary regression does not automatically produce one.

---

## 9. Bad control 2: case-control or outcome-descendant bias

The second official simulation uses:

$$ X \rightarrow Y \rightarrow Z $$

with equations:

$$ X \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0,1) $$

$$ Y \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( \beta_{XY}X, 1 \right) $$

$$ Z \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( \beta_{YZ}Y, 1 \right) $$

The true effect is:

$$ \beta_{XY} $$

### 9.1 Unadjusted model

$$ Y \sim X $$

matches the generating equation.

### 9.2 Adjusted model

$$ Y \sim X+Z $$

conditions on a descendant of the outcome.

$Z$ contains information about realized outcome variation. Holding it fixed changes the comparison among units with different $X$.

The resulting coefficient need not equal the total effect of $X$ on $Y$.

### 9.3 Connection to case-control sampling

The same logic appears when inclusion or sampling depends on the outcome or an outcome descendant.

Restricting or reweighting the sample based on case status can alter exposure–outcome associations unless the analysis is designed for that sampling scheme.

The script label “case control bias” is therefore broader than merely adding one variable to a regression. It points to outcome-dependent conditioning.

---

## 10. Outcome descendants and leakage

In predictive modeling, an outcome descendant may dramatically improve apparent accuracy.

Examples include:

- a collection action triggered by default;
- a support ticket opened after dissatisfaction;
- a cancellation code recorded after churn;
- a physician intervention triggered by deterioration;
- a refund generated after a failed purchase.

Such variables may leak information from the outcome-generating future into the prediction model.

For causal inference, they can distort the exposure coefficient.

For prospective prediction, they may be unavailable at scoring time.

Thus the same variable can create both causal bias and deployment leakage.

---

## 11. Bad or neutral control 3: the precision parasite

The third official simulation has:

$$ Z \rightarrow X \rightarrow Y $$

with no direct path:

$$ Z \not\rightarrow Y $$

The equations are:

$$ Z \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0,1) $$

$$ X \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( \beta_{ZX}Z, 1 \right) $$

$$ Y \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( \beta_{XY}X, 1 \right) $$

The unadjusted model is:

$$ Y \sim X $$

The adjusted model is:

$$ Y \sim X+Z $$

### 11.1 Bias

Both can be unbiased for $\beta_{XY}$ under this generating model.

$Z$ is not a confounder because it does not cause $Y$ except through $X$.

### 11.2 Precision

Conditioning on $Z$ removes variation in $X$ explained by $Z$.

The treatment coefficient is then estimated from residual exposure variation:

$$ X_{\perp Z} = X-\mathbb{E}[X \mid Z] $$

The variance of this residualized exposure is smaller than the variance of $X$:

$$ \mathrm{Var} \left( X_{\perp Z} \right) \leq \mathrm{Var}(X) $$

Less identifying variation can increase the variance of the $X$ coefficient.

$Z$ is therefore a **precision parasite**: it does not create bias in this idealized model, but it can make the treatment-effect estimate noisier.

---

## 12. Why an exposure predictor can harm precision

For a homoskedastic linear regression, the variance of the treatment coefficient is inversely related to the variation in $X$ remaining after adjustment:

$$ \mathrm{Var} \left( \widehat{\beta}_X \right) \propto \frac{\sigma^2} {\sum_i \left( X_i-\widehat{X}_i(Z) \right)^2} $$

A variable that strongly predicts $X$ but does not explain independent variation in $Y$ shrinks the denominator without meaningfully shrinking the outcome residual variance.

This increases uncertainty.

The lesson is not that causes of exposure should never be included. A cause of exposure that also affects outcome is a confounder and may be essential.

The precision-parasite problem applies when the variable predicts exposure but does not close a biasing path or improve outcome precision.

---

## 13. Bad control 4: the bias amplifier

The fourth official simulation adds an unobserved common cause:

$$ Z \rightarrow X $$

$$ U \rightarrow X $$

$$ U \rightarrow Y $$

$$ X \rightarrow Y $$

The equations are:

$$ Z \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0,1) $$

$$ U \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0,1) $$

$$ X \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( \beta_{ZX}Z+U, 1 \right) $$

$$ Y \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( \beta_{XY}X+U, 1 \right) $$

The script examines:

$$ \beta_{XY}=0 $$

so any estimated treatment effect is bias.

### 13.1 Unadjusted association

The unadjusted model is confounded by:

$$ X \leftarrow U \rightarrow Y $$

### 13.2 Conditioning on $Z$

$Z$ is an exposure cause but not an outcome cause.

Conditioning on $Z$ removes the part of $X$ generated by $Z$.

The remaining variation in $X$ is relatively more dominated by $U$.

As a result, the confounded component becomes a larger share of the identifying variation.

The bias can increase.

This is bias amplification.

---

## 14. Residualization view of bias amplification

Write:

$$ X = \beta_{ZX}Z + U + \varepsilon_X $$

After adjusting for $Z$, the remaining exposure is approximately:

$$ X_{\perp Z} = U+ \varepsilon_X $$

The exogenous variation induced by $Z$ has been removed, while the confounded variation due to $U$ remains.

Since:

$$ Y = \beta_{XY}X + U + \varepsilon_Y $$

$X_{\perp Z}$ is strongly associated with the omitted cause $U$.

The adjusted regression can therefore be more biased than the unadjusted one.

This is especially important when $Z$ resembles an instrumental variable but is inserted as an ordinary control instead of being used through a valid instrumental-variable estimand and estimator.

---

## 15. Pre-treatment is not sufficient for safety

$Z$ in the bias-amplifier graph can occur before $X$.

It is still potentially harmful.

Likewise, a pre-treatment variable can be:

- a collider of two causes;
- a descendant of a collider;
- an instrument-like cause of exposure;
- a severe precision parasite;
- a proxy that opens or strengthens an unwanted path.

Therefore:

$$ \text{pre-treatment} \not\Rightarrow \text{good control} $$

Temporal order helps rule out some arrows, but it does not determine the complete graph.

---

## 16. Post-treatment is a warning, not a full taxonomy

A post-treatment variable often signals danger because it may be:

- a mediator;
- a collider between treatment and latent outcome causes;
- a consequence of early outcome evolution;
- a selection variable;
- a treatment-induced confounder of later treatment.

But the phrase “never adjust for post-treatment variables” is also too crude.

Longitudinal causal estimands may require specialized g-methods that model time-varying post-treatment confounders.

Direct-effect estimands may deliberately intervene on a mediator.

The correct conclusion is:

> Post-treatment variables require an explicit longitudinal causal model and an estimand-specific method. Ordinary regression adjustment is not automatically valid.

---

## 17. Randomization does not make every control safe

Randomization breaks baseline common-cause paths into treatment:

$$ X \perp\!\!\!\perp U $$

before treatment.

But conditioning on a post-treatment collider can create dependence:

$$ X \rightarrow Z \leftarrow U \rightarrow Y $$

After conditioning on $Z$:

$$ X \not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp U \mid Z $$

Thus even a randomized treatment effect can be biased by inappropriate post-randomization adjustment.

Randomization protects the unadjusted treatment comparison. It does not validate every regression specification.

---

## 18. The Table 2 fallacy revisited

Suppose one regression includes:

$$ Y \sim X+Z_1+Z_2+Z_3 $$

The table reports one coefficient per predictor.

But the control set was usually chosen to estimate one specific effect, such as the effect of $X$ on $Y$.

The coefficient of $Z_1$ may require adjustment for a different set.

$Z_2$ may be a mediator for $X$ but a confounder for $Z_1$.

$Z_3$ may be a collider for another coefficient.

Therefore, one model does not usually identify a causal effect for every row.

Each causal coefficient needs its own:

- exposure definition;
- outcome definition;
- estimand;
- DAG;
- adjustment argument.

---

## 19. A practical control-classification table

| Candidate variable role | Bias consequence for total effect of $X$ on $Y$ | Typical precision consequence | Default action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Common cause of $X$ and $Y$ | Removes backdoor bias | May increase or decrease variance | Adjust |
| Cause of $Y$ only | Neutral for bias | Often improves precision | Often adjust |
| Cause of $X$ only | Neutral under no hidden confounding | Can reduce precision | Usually unnecessary |
| Cause of $X$ only with hidden $X$–$Y$ confounding | Can amplify bias | Often reduces precision | Avoid ordinary adjustment |
| Mediator | Blocks part of total effect | Varies | Do not adjust for total effect |
| Collider | Opens noncausal path | Can appear precise | Avoid |
| Collider descendant | Can partially open path | Varies | Usually avoid |
| Outcome descendant | Changes target comparison or selection | May look highly predictive | Avoid for ordinary causal effect |
| Treatment-induced confounder | Ordinary adjustment can fail | Varies | Use longitudinal g-methods |

This table is a decision aid, not a replacement for drawing the graph.

## 20. Simulation is the decisive teaching tool

The official script defines a function for each candidate control structure and repeats the comparison thousands of times.

For repetition $r$, compute:

$$ \widehat{\beta}_{X,\mathrm{unadj}}^{(r)} $$

and:

$$ \widehat{\beta}_{X,\mathrm{adj}}^{(r)} $$

The empirical distributions reveal:

- bias shifts;
- variance changes;
- sign reversals;
- false precision;
- sensitivity to structural coefficients.

A single fitted dataset cannot reveal these repeated-sampling properties because the true effect is unknown.

Simulation makes the true effect explicit.

---

## 21. General simulation template for controls

A reusable validation design is:

1. define a DAG;
2. translate it into structural equations;
3. choose the true causal effect;
4. generate many datasets;
5. fit the unadjusted model;
6. fit each candidate adjusted model;
7. calculate the final estimand;
8. compare bias, variance, coverage, and sign error.

For candidate set $S_k$:

$$ \widehat{\tau}_{k}^{(r)} = g_k \left( D^{(r)} \right) $$

Evaluate:

$$ \widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}_k = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^{R} \left( \widehat{\tau}_{k}^{(r)}- \tau^{(r)} \right) $$

$$ \widehat{\mathrm{RMSE}}_k = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^{R} \left( \widehat{\tau}_{k}^{(r)}- \tau^{(r)} \right)^2 } $$

and coverage:

$$ \widehat{C}_k = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^{R} \mathbb{I} \left[ L_k^{(r)} \leq \tau^{(r)} \leq U_k^{(r)} \right] $$

---

## 22. Control selection is not feature selection

Predictive feature selection seeks variables that improve a predictive criterion.

Causal control selection seeks variables that identify an intervention contrast.

A predictive algorithm may include:

- mediators;
- colliders;
- treatment descendants;
- outcome proxies;
- future information;

because these variables predict the observed outcome.

A causal estimator may need to exclude them.

Conversely, a weakly predictive confounder may be necessary because even a small common-cause path can bias an effect estimate.

No generic feature-importance score can replace causal role classification.

---

## 23. Transition: the Copernican principle for model evaluation

The official `07_copernican_model.R` script animates a Copernican orbit that can appear epicyclic from a chosen perspective.

The statistical analogy is that the observed sample should not be treated as the privileged center of the universe.

A model can be tuned to reproduce the training sample while failing on new data.

The Copernican predictive principle is:

> The current sample is one possible sample, not the uniquely privileged realization around which model quality should be judged.

This leads from in-sample fit to out-of-sample prediction.

### 23.1 Causal and predictive humility

There are two distinct decentering moves:

- causal humility: observed associations do not uniquely determine interventions;
- predictive humility: the observed sample does not uniquely determine future performance.

Both require imagining data beyond the observed table, but they address different failures.

---

## 24. Overfitting is different from bad-control bias

Overfitting occurs when a model adapts to accidental features of the observed sample and performs poorly on new data.

Bad-control bias occurs when conditioning changes the causal comparison away from the target estimand.

A model may be:

- causally biased but predictively accurate;
- causally valid but predictively unstable;
- both biased and overfit;
- neither biased nor overfit.

Therefore:

$$ \text{cross-validation} \not\Rightarrow \text{causal identification} $$

and:

$$ \text{correct adjustment set} \not\Rightarrow \text{good predictive generalization} $$

Both diagnostics are needed, but they solve different problems.

---

## 25. The official polynomial overfitting animation

The script models standardized brain volume $B$ from standardized body mass $M$ with polynomials of increasing degree.

Linear model:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+ \beta_1M_i $$

Quadratic model:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+ \beta_1M_i+ \beta_2M_i^2 $$

Degree-$K$ model:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+ \sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_kM_i^k $$

As $K$ increases, in-sample error falls.

But leave-one-out predictions become unstable because flexible curves bend around individual observations.

The training sample rewards complexity that may not generalize.

---

## 26. Leave-one-out cross-validation

For observation $i$:

1. omit $i$;
2. fit the model to $D_{-i}$;
3. predict $y_i$;
4. calculate predictive error or log predictive density.

The leave-one-out predictive density is:

$$ p \left( y_i \mid D_{-i} \right) $$

The expected log predictive density is approximated by:

$$ \mathrm{elpd}_{\mathrm{LOO}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p \left( y_i \mid D_{-i} \right) $$

The official animation displays how the fitted curve changes as each point is omitted and compares the held-out prediction with the observed value.

### 26.1 What LOO can establish

LOO estimates predictive performance under repeated sampling from a similar data-generating context.

It can compare model flexibility and regularization.

It cannot determine whether the variables form a valid causal adjustment set.

---

## 27. In-sample and out-of-sample error

Training error is:

$$ E_{\mathrm{in}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} L \left( y_i, \widehat{y}_i(D) \right) $$

Out-of-sample error is:

$$ E_{\mathrm{out}} = \mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{D}} \left[ L \left( \widetilde{y}, \widehat{y}(D) \right) \right] $$

Increasing flexibility typically lowers $E_{\mathrm{in}}$.

$E_{\mathrm{out}}$ often follows a U-shaped pattern: it improves initially, then worsens when the model fits noise.

The out-of-sample penalty is:

$$ P = E_{\mathrm{out}}-E_{\mathrm{in}} $$

This penalty generally grows with effective flexibility.

---

## 28. Regularization

The overfitting script tightens priors on polynomial coefficients.

A broad prior is:

$$ \beta_k \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0,10) $$

A regularizing prior may be:

$$ \beta_k \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0,0.5) $$

or:

$$ \beta_k \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0,0.1) $$

Regularization discourages extreme curves unless the data provide strong evidence.

It can improve out-of-sample prediction by reducing variance.

### 28.1 Regularization is not causal repair

A strong prior can stabilize a coefficient estimated under a bad control set.

It does not close a backdoor path or undo collider conditioning.

Regularization can reduce the magnitude or variance of a biased estimate, but causal identification still depends on the graph.

---

## 29. WAIC and PSIS

The official overfitting script calculates WAIC and PSIS-based scores.

Both approximate out-of-sample predictive performance using the fitted posterior.

A pointwise log predictive density is:

$$ \mathrm{lppd} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \left[ \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^{S} p \left( y_i \mid \theta^{(s)} \right) \right] $$

WAIC adjusts this for effective model complexity.

PSIS-LOO approximates leave-one-out importance sampling and supplies diagnostics for unstable observations.

These tools address predictive generalization.

They do not select causal controls automatically.

---

## 30. Why predictive model comparison cannot choose the DAG

Suppose two models are:

$$ M_1: Y \sim X+C $$

and:

$$ M_2: Y \sim X+C+Z $$

If $Z$ is a collider, $M_2$ may predict $Y$ better because $Z$ contains information about latent causes.

A predictive score may prefer $M_2$.

But the $X$ coefficient in $M_2$ may be more causally biased.

The criterion is answering:

> Which model predicts observed outcomes better?

It is not answering:

> Which model identifies the intervention effect of $X$?

Causal and predictive model comparison require different validation targets.

## 31. CRM application: risk-score adjustment

Let:

- $A$: retention offer;
- $Y$: future churn;
- $R$: pre-campaign churn risk.

Suppose:

$$ R \rightarrow A $$

$$ R \rightarrow Y $$

Then $R$ lies on:

$$ A \leftarrow R \rightarrow Y $$

It is a good control if it adequately measures the common causes used in targeting.

But if $R$ was computed using post-campaign events, it may contain mediators or outcome descendants.

The variable name “risk score” is not enough. Its feature window and construction time determine its causal role.

---

## 32. CRM application: email-open control

Let:

- $A$: email treatment;
- $O$: open;
- $U$: latent attention;
- $Y$: purchase.

Suppose:

$$ A \rightarrow O \leftarrow U \rightarrow Y $$

and perhaps:

$$ O \rightarrow Y $$

For the total effect of sending the email, controlling for $O$:

- blocks the mediated effect through opening;
- opens the collider path through attention.

The resulting coefficient is not the total effect and is not automatically a valid direct effect.

---

## 33. CRM application: eligibility as a collider

Suppose campaign eligibility $E$ depends on both predicted responsiveness $R$ and customer value $V$:

$$ R \rightarrow E \leftarrow V $$

and value affects the outcome:

$$ V \rightarrow Y $$

Restricting the study to eligible customers conditions on $E$.

Within the eligible group, responsiveness and value become associated.

This may be acceptable when the estimand is explicitly defined for the eligible population, but the selection mechanism must be modeled when interpreting associations inside that population or transporting results elsewhere.

Population definition and collider selection can coexist; one does not erase the other.

---

## 34. CRM application: channel preference as precision parasite

Suppose channel preference $Z$ strongly predicts whether a customer receives an email campaign $A$, but has no independent effect on purchase $Y$ after treatment:

$$ Z \rightarrow A \rightarrow Y $$

Under no hidden confounding, adding $Z$ may reduce the residual variation in $A$ used to estimate the campaign effect.

It can increase uncertainty without reducing bias.

If channel preference also affects purchase directly, it becomes a confounder and adjustment may be necessary.

The role cannot be inferred from predictive importance alone.

---

## 35. CRM application: instrument-like assignment rule as bias amplifier

Suppose a scheduling rule $Z$ affects campaign assignment but not revenue directly:

$$ Z \rightarrow A $$

and latent affinity $U$ affects both assignment and revenue:

$$ U \rightarrow A $$

$$ U \rightarrow Y $$

Ordinarily controlling for $Z$ can amplify the remaining affinity confounding.

A valid instrumental-variable strategy would require a different estimand and assumptions:

- relevance;
- exclusion;
- independence;
- monotonicity for a local average treatment effect interpretation.

Using $Z$ as an ordinary regression control is not equivalent to instrumental-variable estimation.

---

## 36. Implications for CausalOps

A07 requires control variables to be first-class, role-aware objects.

| A07 concept | CausalOps implication |
|---|---|
| Good confounder control | Record the backdoor path it closes |
| Outcome predictor | Mark as optional precision control |
| Post-treatment control | Block for total-effect workflows by default |
| Outcome descendant | Flag causal bias and temporal leakage |
| Precision parasite | Estimate expected variance penalty |
| Bias amplifier | Warn when exposure-only cause is adjusted under hidden confounding |
| Randomization | Preserve unadjusted treatment comparison unless justified |
| Table 2 fallacy | Do not label every coefficient causal |
| Simulation taxonomy | Benchmark candidate control sets |
| Cross-validation | Evaluate prediction separately from identification |
| Regularization | Record stabilization without claiming causal repair |
| PSIS diagnostics | Identify influential predictive observations, not causal validity |

```yaml

measurement_time: after_treatment
causal_roles:
  campaign_sent_to_purchase:
    role:
      - mediator
      - collider
    paths:
      - campaign_sent -> email_opened -> purchase
      - campaign_sent -> email_opened <- digital_attention -> purchase
estimand: total_effect
recommendation: exclude_from_adjustment
reason:
  - blocks_mediated_effect
  - opens_collider_path
```

### 36.3 Separate scoreboards

CausalOps should maintain separate evaluations:

```yaml
causal_scoreboard:
  identification_status: identified_under_declared_dag
  simulation_bias: 0.01
  interval_coverage: 0.88
  positivity_status: adequate
predictive_scoreboard:
  psis_loo_elpd: -412.6
  pareto_k_warnings: 3
  calibration_error: 0.04
```

A model should never be promoted as causally superior merely because its predictive score improves.

## 37. Common errors exposed by A07

### Error 1: Adding every pre-treatment variable

Pre-treatment variables can be colliders, instruments, or precision parasites.

### Error 2: Removing every post-treatment variable without defining the estimand

Longitudinal and direct-effect questions require more precise reasoning, though ordinary adjustment remains dangerous.

### Error 3: Selecting controls by p-value

Statistical significance does not determine causal role.

### Error 4: Selecting controls by feature importance

Predictive importance does not establish backdoor closure.

### Error 5: Treating a mediator coefficient model as a total-effect model

Conditioning blocks part of the causal effect.

### Error 6: Treating mediator adjustment as an automatic direct-effect estimator

Treatment-induced confounding and collider paths can remain.

### Error 7: Conditioning on an outcome descendant

This changes the comparison and may create outcome-dependent selection.

### Error 8: Assuming an exposure predictor improves the treatment estimate

It may reduce identifying variation and precision.

### Error 9: Controlling for an instrument-like variable under hidden confounding

This can amplify bias.

### Error 10: Interpreting every coefficient in one table causally

Adjustment sets are effect-specific.

### Error 11: Using cross-validation to select causal controls

Predictive scoring and causal identification answer different questions.

### Error 12: Assuming regularization repairs a wrong DAG

Priors stabilize estimates but do not create identification.

## 38. Lecture takeaways

1. A control is good or bad only relative to an estimand and causal graph.
2. Statistical control is conditioning, not experimental manipulation.
3. Controls affect both bias and precision.
4. An observed common cause is a good control because it closes a backdoor path.
5. An outcome predictor independent of exposure can improve precision.
6. A post-treatment variable can block mediation and open a collider path.
7. An outcome descendant can create case-control or selection distortion.
8. A cause of exposure only can reduce precision without reducing bias.
9. Under hidden confounding, an exposure-only cause can amplify bias.
10. Pre-treatment status is not sufficient for safety.
11. Randomization does not validate post-treatment adjustment.
12. Direct-effect estimation requires a dedicated estimand and assumptions.
13. Every causal coefficient needs its own adjustment argument.
14. Simulation reveals the bias and variance consequences of candidate controls.
15. Feature selection is not control selection.
16. Overfitting and bad-control bias are distinct failure modes.
17. Cross-validation estimates predictive generalization, not causal validity.
18. Regularization reduces unstable flexibility but cannot repair identification.
19. Predictive and causal scoreboards should remain separate.
20. CausalOps should record why each variable is included or excluded.

## 41. Official code guide

### `06_simulations_bad_controls.r`

This is the principal A07 script.

It repeatedly simulates and compares unadjusted and adjusted regressions for four structures.

#### Post-treatment confounding

$$ X \rightarrow Z \leftarrow U \rightarrow Y $$

with a possible $Z \rightarrow Y$ mediated path.

#### Case-control or outcome-descendant bias

$$ X \rightarrow Y \rightarrow Z $$

#### Precision parasite

$$ Z \rightarrow X \rightarrow Y $$

#### Bias amplifier

$$ Z \rightarrow X \leftarrow U \rightarrow Y $$

The script uses repeated simulation densities rather than one fitted example, making bias and variance changes visible.

### `07_copernican_model.R`

The script animates a Copernican orbit that displays an epicyclic-looking path relative to the Sun.

It supports the chapter-7 analogy that the observed sample should not be treated as a privileged center for judging predictive performance.

### `07_overfitting_animations.r`

The script:

- fits polynomial brain-volume models of increasing degree;
- performs leave-one-out fits;
- animates held-out prediction errors;
- compares in-sample and out-of-sample errors;
- calculates WAIC and PSIS;
- tightens priors to demonstrate regularization.

This is predictive-model material. It is included to clarify the transition beyond A07's causal-control problem, not to redefine causal control selection as cross-validation.
