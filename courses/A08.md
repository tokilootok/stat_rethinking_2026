## 1. Main thesis

MCMC is not a model and it is not evidence that a model is scientifically adequate.

It is a numerical method for approximating expectations under a posterior distribution:

$$
p(\theta\mid y)=\frac{p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta)}{p(y)}.
$$

When direct integration is unavailable, a Markov chain produces dependent draws:

$$
\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)},\ldots,\theta^{(S)}.
$$

Under appropriate conditions, posterior expectations can be approximated by ergodic averages:

$$
\mathbb E[f(\theta)\mid y]
\approx
\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} f\!\left(\theta^{(s)}\right).
$$

The approximation has two separate ways to fail:

1. the statistical model may be wrong for the scientific question;
2. the chain may fail to explore the posterior defined by that model.

The A08 workflow therefore has two obligations:

$$
\text{generative model}
\longrightarrow
\text{identified parameterization}
\longrightarrow
\text{MCMC}
\longrightarrow
\text{computational diagnostics}
\longrightarrow
\text{posterior validation}.
$$

Passing computational diagnostics licenses posterior computation. It does not validate the likelihood, priors, causal assumptions, or scientific interpretation.

## 3. What MCMC computes

Bayesian updating defines the posterior only up to a normalizing constant:

$$
p(\theta\mid y)
\propto
p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta).
$$

The marginal likelihood

$$
p(y)=\int p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta
$$

may be difficult to calculate.

MCMC algorithms need relative target density, not necessarily the integral itself. This makes it possible to explore high-dimensional posterior distributions whose normalization is unknown.

The word **Monte Carlo** refers to using random draws for numerical approximation.

The word **Markov chain** means that the next state depends on the present state:

$$
p(\theta^{(s+1)}\mid\theta^{(s)},\ldots,\theta^{(1)})
=
p(\theta^{(s+1)}\mid\theta^{(s)}).
$$

This does not mean the draws are independent. Dependence is expected and is measured through autocorrelation and effective sample size.

## 4. Conditions behind the approximation

For an MCMC estimator to converge reliably to the target posterior, the transition mechanism must satisfy specific measure-theoretic conditions.

### Core Mathematical Definitions

- **Target Posterior Distribution $\pi(\theta)$:** In Bayesian inference, $\pi(\theta) \equiv p(\theta \mid y)$ denotes the target probability density (or probability measure) over parameter space $\Theta$.
- **Transition Kernel $K(\theta' \mid \theta)$:** The conditional probability density $K(\theta' \mid \theta) \equiv p(\theta^{(s+1)} = \theta' \mid \theta^{(s)} = \theta)$ of proposing and moving from current state $\theta$ to next state $\theta'$ in one step. For continuous parameters, $\int_{\Theta} K(\theta' \mid \theta) \, d\theta' = 1$ for all $\theta \in \Theta$.

### Mathematical Structure of the Transition Kernel

The **transition kernel** (or Markov transition operator) $K(\theta' \mid \theta)$ is the mathematical engine of an MCMC algorithm. It defines how the chain transitions from current state $\theta^{(s)} = \theta$ to next state $\theta^{(s+1)} = \theta'$.

#### Measure-Theoretic Definition

Formally, for a continuous parameter space $\Theta \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ equipped with Borel $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{B}(\Theta)$, a transition kernel $K(\theta, A)$ maps a point $\theta \in \Theta$ and a measurable set $A \in \mathcal{B}(\Theta)$ to a probability:

$$
K(\theta, A) = P(\theta^{(s+1)} \in A \mid \theta^{(s)} = \theta).
$$

It satisfies two fundamental measure-theoretic axioms:
1. For every fixed state $\theta \in \Theta$, $K(\theta, \cdot)$ is a valid probability measure on $\Theta$, satisfying $K(\theta, \Theta) = \int_{\Theta} K(\theta' \mid \theta) \, d\theta' = 1$.
2. For every fixed measurable set $A \in \mathcal{B}(\Theta)$, $K(\cdot, A)$ is a measurable function of $\theta$.

#### Canonical MCMC Decomposition: Proposal + Acceptance

In practical MCMC algorithms (such as Metropolis-Hastings and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo), the transition kernel $K(\theta, d\theta')$ is decomposed into a **candidate proposal mechanism** and a **stochastic acceptance rule**:

1. **Proposal Density $q(\theta' \mid \theta)$:** Proposes a candidate state $\theta'$ given current position $\theta$.
2. **Acceptance Probability $\alpha(\theta, \theta') \in [0, 1]$:** Evaluates whether to move to candidate $\theta'$ or remain at $\theta$.

Mathematically, for any candidate state $\theta' \neq \theta$, the continuous transition density is:

$$
K(\theta' \mid \theta) = q(\theta' \mid \theta) \, \alpha(\theta, \theta').
$$

The probability of rejecting the proposal and remaining at state $\theta$ is:

$$
r(\theta) = 1 - \int_{\Theta \setminus \{\theta\}} q(\theta' \mid \theta) \, \alpha(\theta, \theta') \, d\theta'.
$$

Combining both terms gives the complete transition measure operator:

$$
K(\theta, d\theta') = q(\theta' \mid \theta) \, \alpha(\theta, \theta') \, d\theta' + r(\theta) \, \delta_\theta(d\theta'),
$$

where $\delta_\theta(\cdot)$ denotes the Dirac delta measure concentrated at $\theta$.

#### Multi-Step Transitions & The Chapman-Kolmogorov Equation

The probability density of moving from $\theta$ to $\theta'$ in $n$ steps is governed by the $n$-step transition kernel $K^{(n)}(\theta' \mid \theta) = p(\theta^{(s+n)} = \theta' \mid \theta^{(s)} = \theta)$, which satisfies the **Chapman-Kolmogorov equation**:

$$
K^{(n+m)}(\theta' \mid \theta) = \int_{\Theta} K^{(n)}(\theta'' \mid \theta) \, K^{(m)}(\theta' \mid \theta'') \, d\theta''.
$$

In operator notation, if $\mu_s(\cdot)$ represents the marginal probability distribution of the chain at step $s$, applying the transition operator yields:

$$
\mu_{s+1} = \mu_s K \implies \mu_s = \mu_0 K^s.
$$

Under ergodicity, as step count $s \rightarrow \infty$, the distribution $\mu_s$ converges to the invariant target posterior $\pi(\cdot)$ regardless of initial state distribution $\mu_0$.

#### Specific Transition Kernels across MCMC Algorithms

- **Random-Walk Metropolis Kernel**: $q(\theta' \mid \theta) = \mathcal{N}(\theta, \mathbf{\Sigma})$, with acceptance ratio $\alpha(\theta, \theta') = \min\left(1, \frac{\pi(\theta')}{\pi(\theta)}\right)$.
- **Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) Kernel**: Draws random auxiliary momentum $\mathbf{p} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{M})$ and integrates Hamilton's equations along gradient field $\nabla_\theta \log \pi(\theta)$ for $L$ leapfrog steps to generate deterministic proposal $\theta'$, followed by a Metropolis acceptance step based on energy conservation $\Delta H$.
- **Gibbs Sampler Kernel**: Updates parameters one coordinate at a time directly from full conditional distributions $\pi(\theta_i \mid \theta_{-i})$, resulting in acceptance probability $\alpha \equiv 1$ for each coordinate kernel $K_i$.

### Fundamental Properties Required for MCMC Convergence

1. **Invariance (Stationarity / Preservation of Target Distribution):**
   A distribution $\pi(\theta)$ is *invariant* (or stationary) under transition kernel $K$ if executing one transition step preserves $\pi$:
   $$
   \int_{\Theta} \pi(\theta) K(\theta' \mid \theta) \, d\theta = \pi(\theta').
   $$
   *Intuition:* If the current state $\theta^{(s)}$ follows the target posterior distribution $\pi$, the state at the next step $\theta^{(s+1)}$ will also follow $\pi$.

2. **Irreducibility ($\pi$-Irreducibility / Full Communication):**
   A Markov chain is $\pi$-irreducible if, from any initial state $\theta^{(0)} \in \Theta$, the chain has a non-zero probability of entering any measurable subset $A \subseteq \Theta$ with positive target mass ($\pi(A) > 0$) in a finite number of steps $n \ge 1$:
   $$
   P(\theta^{(n)} \in A \mid \theta^{(0)}) > 0.
   $$
   *Intuition:* The chain can explore the entire relevant parameter space and is never trapped in an isolated region or blocked by probability barriers.

3. **Aperiodicity (Absence of Deterministic Cycles):**
   A chain is aperiodic if it does not trap exploration in a fixed deterministic cycle. Formally, for any state, the greatest common divisor ($\mathrm{gcd}$) of all possible return step lengths $n \ge 1$ to a set $A$ is 1.
   *Intuition:* The chain does not oscillate strictly back and forth between disjoint sub-regions (e.g., $A \rightarrow B \rightarrow A \rightarrow B$), enabling empirical averages to settle smoothly into steady probabilities.

4. **Ergodicity (The Markov Chain Law of Large Numbers):**
   A Markov chain that is positive Harris recurrent, irreducible, and aperiodic is **ergodic**. Ergodicity ensures two fundamental limit theorems:
   - **Distributional Convergence (Total Variation Distance):**
     $$
     \lim_{s \to \infty} \| P(\theta^{(s)} \in \cdot \mid \theta^{(0)}) - \pi(\cdot) \|_{\mathrm{TV}} = 0.
     $$
   - **Ergodic Theorem (Sample Average Consistency):** For any integrable function $f(\theta)$ under $\pi$ ($\mathbb{E}_\pi[|f|] < \infty$), the time average along a single chain trajectory converges almost surely to the spatial expectation under the true posterior:
     $$
     \lim_{S \to \infty} \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^{S} f(\theta^{(s)}) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f(\theta)] = \int_{\Theta} f(\theta) \pi(\theta) \, d\theta.
     $$
   *Intuition:* Long-run time averages along a single sample path equal the theoretical spatial expectations over the entire target distribution.

### Detailed Balance (Reversibility)

**Detailed balance** is a local equilibrium condition stating that the probability flux from state $\theta$ to $\theta'$ equals the reverse flux from $\theta'$ to $\theta$:

$$
\pi(\theta) K(\theta' \mid \theta) = \pi(\theta') K(\theta \mid \theta').
$$

Integrating both sides over $\theta$ demonstrates that detailed balance is a **sufficient condition for invariance**:

$$
\int_{\Theta} \pi(\theta) K(\theta' \mid \theta) \, d\theta = \int_{\Theta} \pi(\theta') K(\theta \mid \theta') \, d\theta = \pi(\theta') \int_{\Theta} K(\theta \mid \theta') \, d\theta = \pi(\theta').
$$

*Note:* While Metropolis-Hastings and standard Hamiltonian Monte Carlo satisfy detailed balance (making them reversible chains), detailed balance is not strictly necessary for MCMC. Non-reversible MCMC algorithms exist as long as global invariance holds.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Practical Implication:** Finite chain outputs never provide a mathematical proof of convergence. Diagnostics (such as $\widehat{R}$, ESS, and traceplots) are designed to detect *failures* of these properties, but cannot guarantee that no unobserved failure remains.

## 5. King Markov's archipelago

Suppose island $i$ has population proportional to $i$, for $i=1,\ldots,10$.

The king wants visits to occur in proportion to population but does not know the total population.

From the current island he proposes one neighboring island and accepts the move with probability:

$$
\alpha=\min\left(1,\frac{P_{\text{proposed}}}{P_{\text{current}}}\right).
$$

The normalizing sum $\sum_i P_i$ cancels from this ratio.

```{r fig-king-markov}
#| fig-cap: "King Markov MCMC simulation: Empirical island visit frequencies vs theoretical population target"
set.seed(202608)

position <- integer(n_steps)
current <- 10L

for (s in seq_len(n_steps)) {
  position[s] <- current
  proposal <- current + sample(c(-1L, 1L), 1L)
  if (proposal < 1L) proposal <- 10L
  if (proposal > 10L) proposal <- 1L
  if (runif(1) < min(1, proposal / current)) current <- proposal
}

# Empirical visit frequencies vs target probabilities
freq_emp <- prop.table(table(position))
freq_theo <- (1:10) / sum(1:10)

cat("Empirical visit frequencies:\n")
print(round(freq_emp, 4))
cat("\nTheoretical population frequencies (P(i) ~ i):\n")

        main = "King Markov Island Visits vs Target Distribution")
```

- the trace `position`, which is dependent in time;
- its empirical frequency table;
- the target probability vector.

The trace can look locally sticky while its long-run frequencies still approximate the target.

## 6. Metropolis–Hastings, not only Metropolis

### Purpose and Historical Significance

The original Metropolis algorithm (Metropolis et al., 1953) required **symmetric proposal distributions**, meaning that the probability of proposing candidate state $\theta'$ from current state $\theta$ equaled the probability of proposing $\theta$ from $\theta'$:

$$
q(\theta'\mid\theta) = q(\theta\mid\theta').
$$

This restricted samplers to symmetric random walks around the current state (such as $\theta' \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\theta, \sigma^2 I)$). 

Hastings (1970) extended the algorithm to allow **asymmetric proposal distributions** $q(\theta'\mid\theta) \neq q(\theta\mid\theta')$. This generalization grants complete freedom to propose candidates from non-symmetric candidate generators—such as log-normal multipliers, truncated distributions, or gradient-guided physics trajectories.

### The General Metropolis–Hastings Formula

The general acceptance probability for moving from $\theta$ to candidate $\theta'$ is:

$$
\alpha(\theta,\theta')=
\min\left[
1,
\frac{\pi(\theta')q(\theta\mid\theta')}
     {\pi(\theta)q(\theta'\mid\theta)}
\right].
$$

Notice the ratio term:

$$
\text{Acceptance Ratio} = \underbrace{\frac{\pi(\theta')}{\pi(\theta)}}_{\text{Target Density Ratio}} \times \underbrace{\frac{q(\theta\mid\theta')}{q(\theta'\mid\theta)}}_{\text{Hastings Proposal Correction}}.
$$

### Mathematical Proof: Why the Hastings Correction is Required

To guarantee that the Markov chain converges to target distribution $\pi(\theta)$, the transition kernel $K(\theta'\mid\theta) = q(\theta'\mid\theta)\alpha(\theta,\theta')$ (for $\theta' \neq \theta$) must satisfy **detailed balance**:

$$
\pi(\theta) K(\theta'\mid\theta) = \pi(\theta') K(\theta\mid\theta').
$$

Substituting $K(\theta'\mid\theta) = q(\theta'\mid\theta)\alpha(\theta,\theta')$ yields:

$$
\pi(\theta) q(\theta'\mid\theta) \alpha(\theta,\theta') = \pi(\theta') q(\theta\mid\theta') \alpha(\theta',\theta).
$$

Rearranging the ratio of acceptance probabilities:

$$
\frac{\alpha(\theta,\theta')}{\alpha(\theta',\theta)} = \frac{\pi(\theta') q(\theta\mid\theta')}{\pi(\theta) q(\theta'\mid\theta)}.
$$

Choosing $\alpha(\theta,\theta') = \min\left(1, \frac{\pi(\theta') q(\theta\mid\theta')}{\pi(\theta) q(\theta'\mid\theta)}\right)$ satisfies this equality exactly. 

*Intuition:* If a proposal mechanism makes it easier to jump from $\theta \rightarrow \theta'$ than to jump back from $\theta' \rightarrow \theta$ ($q(\theta'\mid\theta) > q(\theta\mid\theta')$), the sampler would naturally over-visit state $\theta'$. The Hastings factor $\frac{q(\theta\mid\theta')}{q(\theta'\mid\theta)} < 1$ penalizes the forward move to balance the probability flux.

### Concrete Example: Asymmetric Scale Proposal

Suppose parameter $\theta = \sigma > 0$ is a positive scale parameter. A natural proposal is a multiplicative step $\theta' = \theta \cdot e^\epsilon$ where $\epsilon \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0, s^2)$.

The proposal density is log-normal:

$$
q(\theta'\mid\theta) = \frac{1}{\theta'\sqrt{2\pi s^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(\log\theta' - \log\theta)^2}{2s^2}\right).
$$

The reverse proposal density is:

$$
q(\theta\mid\theta') = \frac{1}{\theta\sqrt{2\pi s^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(\log\theta - \log\theta')^2}{2s^2}\right).
$$

The Hastings proposal ratio simplifies to:

$$
\frac{q(\theta\mid\theta')}{q(\theta'\mid\theta)} = \frac{\theta'}{\theta}.
$$

Without this correction factor $\frac{\theta'}{\theta}$, multiplicative steps toward larger values of $\theta$ would be systematically over-accepted, severely biasing the posterior estimate.

> [!NOTE]
> **Summary Takeaway**: The purpose of Section 6 is to demonstrate that Hastings' formula decouples *proposal design* $q(\theta'\mid\theta)$ from the *target distribution* $\pi(\theta)$. This mathematical freedom is what enables modern HMC and NUTS to use complex, asymmetric gradient trajectories while maintaining exact posterior convergence.

## 7. Random-walk tuning trade-off

A Gaussian random-walk proposal might be:

$$
\theta'=\theta+\epsilon,qquad
\epsilon\sim\mathrm{Normal}(0,s^2I).
$$

If $s$ is too small:

- acceptance is high;
- successive values are similar;
- autocorrelation is high;
- global exploration is slow.

If $s$ is too large:

- proposals often land in low-density regions;
- rejection is high;
- repeated states dominate the trace.

Acceptance rate alone is therefore not a quality measure. A chain that accepts nearly every microscopic proposal can be very inefficient.

## 8. High dimension and the typical set

### Mode vs Typical Set: The Fundamental Paradox

In low-dimensional spaces ($K=1$ or $K=2$), we naturally associate probability mass with regions of high probability density (the posterior **mode**). In high dimensions, however, **the mode contains virtually zero probability mass**.

Probability mass in a differential region around radius $r$ is the product of two competing forces:

$$
\text{Probability Mass}(r) \propto \underbrace{\pi(r)}_{\text{Target Density (Decreases with } r)} \times \underbrace{\text{Volume}(r)}_{\text{Hyperspherical Volume (Grows exponentially with } r^{K-1})}.
$$

1. **At the Mode ($r = 0$)**: Density $\pi(0)$ is maximal, but the differential volume $d\theta$ around a single point is near zero.
2. **At Far Distance ($r \to \infty$)**: Differential volume $S_K r^{K-1}$ is enormous, but probability density $\pi(r)$ drops exponentially to zero.
3. **The Typical Set**: The narrow intermediate hyperspherical shell where the product of density and volume is maximized. This thin shell contains almost 100% of the posterior probability mass!

### Mathematical Example: Standard $K$-Dimensional Gaussian

Consider a $K$-dimensional standard Gaussian target $\boldsymbol{\theta} \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}_K)$.

The Euclidean distance squared from the mode $\boldsymbol{\theta} = \mathbf{0}$ is:

$$
r^2 = \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^K \theta_i^2 \sim \chi^2_K.
$$

The expected distance of a draw from the mode is:

$$
\mathbb{E}[r] \approx \sqrt{K}, \quad \text{with standard deviation } \operatorname{sd}(r) \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.
$$

- In $K = 1$ dimension: $\mathbb{E}[r] \approx 1$. Most probability mass is near the mode $r = 0$.
- In $K = 100$ dimensions: $\mathbb{E}[r] \approx 10$. Virtually **all draws** lie in a thin shell at distance $r \in [9.3, 10.7]$. The probability of observing a draw near the mode ($r < 3$) is less than $10^{-15}$!

### Empirical R Simulation: The Typical Set Shift

The executable R code below draws 10,000 samples across dimensions $K \in \{1, 5, 20, 100, 500\}$ and plots the distribution of distances $r = \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|$ from the mode:


### Why Samplers and Optimizers Differ

- **Optimizers (MAP / `quap`)**: Search exclusively for the mode ($r = 0$). In high dimensions, the mode is a completely unrepresentative point.
- **Random-Walk Metropolis**: Fails in high dimensions because a random vector step almost always falls *outside* the paper-thin typical set into regions of near-zero density, causing rejection ($\alpha \to 0$), or steps inward toward the zero-volume mode.
- **Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC)**: Uses vector gradients $\nabla \log \pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ to slide continuously *along* the contours of the typical set shell without falling off into zero-density space!

## 9. Gibbs sampling

For $\theta=(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_K)$, Gibbs sampling cycles through full conditional distributions:

$$
\theta_k^{(s+1)}sim
p(\theta_k\mid\theta_{-k},y).
$$

There is no rejection when an exact conditional draw is available.

But acceptance equal to one does not imply efficient exploration. When parameters are strongly correlated, coordinate-wise updates can move slowly along a narrow ridge.

Gibbs sampling does not universally require conjugacy, but convenient exact Gibbs updates require conditional distributions from which one can sample. Modern general-purpose HMC avoids the need to derive such conditionals one at a time.

## 9.5 Why Hamiltonian Monte Carlo? From Random Walks to Physics-Guided Exploration

Before examining the formal energy equations of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC), it is essential to understand **why Bayesian computation underwent a paradigm shift from Metropolis-Hastings and Gibbs sampling to gradient-guided Hamiltonian dynamics.**

### The Double Bottleneck of Traditional MCMC

Traditional MCMC samplers suffer from two fundamental failure modes in practical Bayesian models:

1. **The Metropolis Random-Walk Diffusion Bottleneck**:
   Random-walk Metropolis proposes steps by adding isotropic random noise $\boldsymbol{\theta}' = \boldsymbol{\theta} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$. In high dimensions, random vectors point almost perpendicularly to the thin shell of the **typical set**. A random step almost always lands in zero-density space (causing high rejection $\alpha \to 0$) or steps inward toward the zero-volume mode. To maintain acceptable acceptance rates, step size $s$ must be shrunk as $\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/2})$, forcing the chain to take $\mathcal{O}(K^2)$ steps to travel a distance $D$. The sampler acts like a drunkard's random walk, diffusing painfully slowly through parameter space.

2. **The Gibbs Sampling Correlation Ridge Trap**:
   Gibbs sampling updates parameters one coordinate at a time ($\theta_k \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-k}, y$). While every Gibbs proposal is accepted ($\alpha = 1$), coordinate-wise updates are forced to take $90^\circ$ orthogonal steps. When parameters are strongly correlated or form narrow diagonal ridges, Gibbs sampling gets trapped taking microscopic zig-zag steps, causing severe autocorrelation and slow exploration.

### The Hamiltonian Solution: Turning Inference into Physics

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (Duane et al., 1987; Neal, 2011) eliminates random walks by replacing blind proposals with **continuous vector field physics**:

- **Target Surface as Potential Energy**: The negative log posterior $-\log p(\boldsymbol{\theta}, y)$ is treated as a continuous physical landscape (a "bowl"). High posterior density corresponds to **low potential energy** $U(\boldsymbol{\theta})$.
- **Artificial Momentum as Kinetic Energy**: We augment parameter position $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ with fictitious momentum vectors $\mathbf{p} \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{M})$, imparting kinetic energy $K(\mathbf{p}) = \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathbf{p}$.
- **Gliding along the Typical Set**: Instead of guessing random steps, we flick a frictionless marble across the surface. The continuous vector gradient $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \log p(\boldsymbol{\theta}, y)$ exerts a gravitational force pulling the particle toward high density, while momentum carries it smoothly along the typical set shell across huge parameter distances in a single iteration.

### Key Architectural Comparison

| Feature | Random-Walk Metropolis | Gibbs Sampling | Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC / NUTS) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Proposal Mechanism** | Random Gaussian shock $\boldsymbol{\theta} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ | Direct draw from full conditional $p(\theta_k \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-k})$ | Gradient-driven leapfrog physical trajectory |
| **Information Used** | Target density ratio $\frac{\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}')}{\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}$ | Full conditional distributions $p(\theta_k \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-k})$ | Vector gradient $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \log p(\boldsymbol{\theta}, y)$ |
| **High-Dimensional Scaling** | $\mathcal{O}(K^2)$ steps (diffusive failure) | Degenerates under parameter correlation | $\mathcal{O}(K^{1/4})$ steps (coherent long moves) |
| **Correlated Parameters** | Severe rejection or extreme sticking | Slow $90^\circ$ orthogonal zig-zag motion | Glides smoothly along diagonal ridges |
| **Geometric Diagnostics** | Silent non-mixing or sticky chains | Silent slow mixing along ridges | Explicit **divergent transitions** signal failure |

## 10. Hamiltonian augmentation

HMC augments the parameters $q$ with an auxiliary momentum $p$.

Define potential energy:

$$
U(q)=-\log p(q,y),
$$

and kinetic energy:

$$
K(p)=\frac{1}{2}p^TM^{-1}p.
$$

The Hamiltonian is:

$$
H(q,p)=U(q)+K(p).
$$

The joint density is proportional to:

$$
\exp[-H(q,p)].
$$

After marginalizing momentum, the distribution of $q$ is the desired posterior.

The mass matrix $M$ rescales and rotates the geometry. A well-adapted mass matrix makes differently scaled and correlated directions easier to traverse.

---

## 11. Hamilton's equations

The continuous trajectory follows:

$$
\frac{dq}{dt}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}=M^{-1}p,
$$

$$
\frac{dp}{dt}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}=-\nabla U(q).
$$

Because

$$
-\nabla U(q)=\nabla\log p(q,y),
$$

the trajectory uses local derivatives of the log joint density.

Automatic differentiation supplies these derivatives. The analyst still determines the model and its parameterization; automatic differentiation does not repair an unidentified model.

---

## 12. Leapfrog integration

Computers approximate the continuous trajectory with finite steps of size $\epsilon$:

$$
p_{t+\epsilon/2}
=
p_t-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\nabla U(q_t),
$$

$$
q_{t+\epsilon}
=
q_t+\epsilon M^{-1}p_{t+\epsilon/2},
$$

$$
p_{t+\epsilon}
=
p_{t+\epsilon/2}-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\nabla U(q_{t+\epsilon}).
$$

Leapfrog is reversible and volume preserving. It does **not** preserve energy exactly at finite step size.

The residual energy error is corrected by a Metropolis acceptance step:

$$
\alpha=\min\left(1,\exp[-H(q',p')+H(q,p)]\right).
$$

This correction is why small numerical integration error need not bias the stationary target.

---

## 13. Step size, trajectory length, and mass matrix

Three tuning objects matter:

| Quantity | Too small or poor | Too large or poor |
|---|---|---|
| Step size $\epsilon$ | More gradient evaluations than needed | Energy error and divergences |
| Trajectory length | Random-walk-like movement | Retracing and wasted computation |
| Mass matrix $M$ | Poor scaling and slow directions | Unstable or expensive adaptation |

The official script visualizes these trade-offs with `HMC2()` and compares HMC to a random-walk Metropolis chain.

That comparison is more informative than memorizing an acceptance-rate target: HMC uses gradients to make distant, coherent proposals through the typical set.

---

## 14. NUTS

The No-U-Turn Sampler dynamically builds candidate trajectories and stops expansion when continued integration begins to double back.

NUTS removes the need to choose a fixed number of leapfrog steps manually.

It does not remove the need for:

- a differentiable log density;
- an identified model;
- sensible priors;
- appropriate parameterization;
- diagnostic review.

NUTS is the default engine behind Stan, `rethinking::ulam()` when using Stan, and PyMC's standard continuous-variable sampler.

---

## 15. Warmup is not ordinary burn-in

During Stan warmup, the algorithm performs adaptation experiments.

It learns quantities such as:

- step size;
- mass matrix;
- adaptation schedule state.

The transition kernel changes during adaptation, so those draws are not retained as posterior draws.

Old random-walk terminology often calls discarded early posterior transitions **burn-in**. Stan warmup is conceptually different because it is active tuning, not merely waiting for a fixed chain to forget its initial value.

After warmup, adaptation stops and the fixed sampling kernel generates retained draws.

---

## 16. Multiple chains and initialization

One chain cannot reveal that another initial region leads to a different mode.

Use at least four chains for a final analysis when practical, with dispersed randomized initial values.

Multiple chains help assess whether exploration is consistent across starting points. They do not guarantee discovery of every isolated mode.

Initialization failures can indicate:

- impossible transformed parameters;
- invalid likelihood values;
- a prior that places mass in numerically extreme regions;
- an algebra or indexing error;
- a real geometric pathology.

Do not repeatedly change seeds until warnings disappear. Diagnose why some initial states fail.

---

## 17. A computational acceptance contract

Before interpreting a fit, record and assess:

| Diagnostic | Desired evidence | Failure implies |
|---|---|---|
| Independent chains | Same stationary region | Possible non-mixing or multimodality |
| Rank-normalized split R-hat | Near 1; conventionally $\le 1.01$ | Between/within-chain inconsistency |
| Bulk ESS | Adequate for central summaries | Noisy means/medians or sticky chain |
| Tail ESS | Adequate for tail summaries | Noisy intervals/tail probabilities |
| MCSE | Small relative to required precision | Monte Carlo answer still too noisy |
| Divergences | Zero after warmup | Numerical trajectory missed geometry |
| Max treedepth | No saturation | Trajectory limit truncates exploration |
| BFMI/energy | No poor-energy warning | Momentum fails to explore energy levels |
| Trace/rank plots | Mixing and stationarity | Localized chain pathology |
| Pairs plots | No divergence cluster | Problematic parameter geometry |

Thresholds are screening conventions, not scientific laws. Required ESS and MCSE depend on the estimand and desired numerical precision.

---

## 18. Rank-normalized split R-hat

The modern diagnostic is not merely the historical variance-ratio formula.

It:

- splits chains;
- ranks pooled draws;
- normalizes ranks;
- also checks folded draws to detect scale differences.

Conceptually, R-hat compares within-chain and between-chain variation. If chains sample the same stable target, the ratio approaches one.

An R-hat above 1.01 is a reason to investigate. The correct response is not to discard the inconvenient parameter rows. The fit must be repaired or the uncertainty about computation reported.

R-hat near one is necessary but not sufficient. Chains can all become trapped in the same incomplete region.

---

## 19. Trace plots and rank plots

A trace plot shows parameter value against iteration for every chain.

Healthy traces should be stationary, overlapping, and rapidly moving through their supported range.

A rank plot pools draws, ranks them, and displays the rank distribution by chain. If chains explore the same marginal distribution, each chain should occupy ranks approximately uniformly.

Rank plots expose situations hidden by overplotted fuzzy trace lines:

- one chain systematically high;
- one chain systematically low;
- scale differences;
- local sticking.

## 20. Autocorrelation and effective sample size

At lag $k$, let $\rho_k$ denote chain autocorrelation.

A simplified integrated autocorrelation time is:

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{int}}
=
1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho_k.
$$

The intuitive effective sample size is:

$$
\mathrm{ESS}\approx\frac{S}{\tau_{\mathrm{int}}}.
$$

Modern ESS estimators use rank-normalization, chain splitting, and stable truncation rules, so this expression is intuition rather than their complete implementation.

Negative autocorrelation can make ESS exceed the raw number of draws. That is not an error: alternating exploration can contain more information for some estimands than the same number of independent-looking but less strategically placed draws.

Bulk ESS targets central summaries. Tail ESS targets extreme quantiles and interval endpoints.

---

## 21. Monte Carlo standard error

Posterior standard deviation describes uncertainty about the parameter.

Monte Carlo standard error describes uncertainty caused by using a finite simulation to estimate a posterior summary.

For a posterior mean:

$$
\operatorname{MCSE}(\bar\theta)
\approx
\frac{\operatorname{sd}(\theta\mid y)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{ESS}}}.
$$

These should never be conflated.

If an estimate must be reported to two decimal places, its MCSE should be materially smaller than 0.01. If not, draw more samples **after** repairing any chain pathology.

---

## 22. Divergent transitions

A divergence occurs when the numerical Hamiltonian trajectory accumulates an unexpectedly large integration error.

Divergences often reveal regions of high curvature such as:

- funnels;
- boundaries;
- weakly identified scale products;
- strongly correlated latent parameters;
- overly broad priors.

They are not cosmetic warnings.

A chain may show R-hat equal to 1.00 and still have biased exploration if divergent trajectories systematically avoid part of the posterior.

The diagnostic question is not only “how many?” but “where?” Plot divergent draws against suspected parameters and derived quantities.

---

## 23. Treedepth and energy diagnostics

NUTS expands a binary tree of leapfrog trajectories.

Reaching maximum treedepth means the sampler wanted a longer trajectory than allowed. This can indicate inefficient geometry even without divergence.

Increasing `max_treedepth` may be reasonable after diagnosing the model, but it makes each affected transition more expensive and does not repair bad geometry.

Energy Bayesian fraction of missing information (E-BFMI) compares energy movement induced by refreshed momentum with the energy variation required by the target. Low BFMI indicates poor exploration across energy levels.

Stan interfaces report these diagnostics directly:

```{r diagnostics-summary-demo}
attr(m_demo, "cstanfit")$diagnostic_summary()
attr(m_demo, "cstanfit")$cmdstan_diagnose()
```

## 24. Neal's funnel

Consider:

$$
v\sim\mathrm{Normal}(0,3),
$$

$$
x_k\sim\mathrm{Normal}(0,\exp(v/2)).
$$

When $v$ is negative, the conditional scale of $x_k$ becomes tiny. When $v$ is positive, it becomes broad.

One global step size must navigate both the narrow neck and wide mouth. A step safe in the neck can be painfully small in the mouth; a step efficient in the mouth can diverge in the neck.

This is a property of posterior geometry, not an insufficient number of iterations.

---

## 25. Centered and non-centered parameterizations

A centered varying effect is:

$$
h_j\sim\mathrm{Normal}(\mu_h,\sigma_h).
$$

A non-centered form is:

$$
z_j\sim\mathrm{Normal}(0,1),qquad
h_j=\mu_h+\sigma_h z_j.
$$

The two forms define the same generative distribution but different computational coordinates.

Non-centering often helps when group information is weak or the group scale is near zero. Centering can be more efficient when each group is strongly informed by data.

There is no universal winner. Compare diagnostics and efficiency using the same posterior estimands.

---

## 26. Repair order

When a chain fails, use this order:

1. verify data types, indexes, and units;
2. verify likelihood and linear predictor algebra;
3. identify exact or approximate symmetries;
4. inspect prior predictive implications;
5. standardize badly scaled variables;
6. strengthen scientifically unreasonable weak priors;
7. reparameterize funnels and ridges;
8. inspect divergence pairs and energy diagnostics;
9. only then adjust `adapt_delta` or `max_treedepth`;
10. draw more samples only after the chain is valid.

More iterations reduce Monte Carlo noise. They do not repair non-identification, label switching, or systematically missed regions.

## 28. Bad-chain experiment 2: sign switching

Suppose the wine model contains:

$$
\mu_i=D_{J_i}(Q_{W_i}-H_{J_i}).
$$

If $D_j$ may be positive or negative, then the simultaneous transformation

$$
(D,Q,H)\mapsto(-D,-Q,-H)
$$

leaves every $\mu_i$ unchanged.

The likelihood therefore has two equivalent orientations. Different chains can occupy different modes, or a chain can switch signs.

Symptoms include:

- very high R-hat;
- bimodal marginal distributions;
- opposing chain signs;
- apparently stable predictions despite unstable parameters.

The repair is an identifying constraint:

$$
D_j>0.
$$

A log scale implements this naturally:

$$
D_j=\exp(\ell_j).
$$

Increasing `adapt_delta` cannot distinguish observationally identical orientations.

## 29. Bad-chain experiment 3: centered hierarchy

Simulate sparse judges whose between-judge variation is small.

Fit both:

$$
H_j\sim\mathrm{Normal}(\alpha_H,\sigma_H)
$$

and:

$$
z_j\sim\mathrm{Normal}(0,1),qquad
H_j=\alpha_H+\sigma_Hz_j.
$$

Compare:

- divergent transitions;
- bulk and tail ESS for $\sigma_H$;
- pairs plots of $H_j$ against $\sigma_H$;
- ESS per second;
- agreement of posterior contrasts.

This is a computational comparison, not a contest over posterior means. Equivalent parameterizations should yield compatible posterior estimands when both sample correctly.

## 30. From MCMC to the wine problem

`Wines2012` contains 180 ratings:

- 20 wines;
- 9 judges;
- every judge rates every wine;
- a numerical score from 7 to 19.5;
- wine origin, France or United States/New Jersey;
- judge nationality, French or American.

The crossed design is important. Because all judges rate all wines, judge tendencies can be separated from wine tendencies much more cleanly than if each wine were rated by a different subset.

The outcome used in the lecture is standardized:

$$
S_i=\frac{\text{score}_i-\overline{\text{score}}}
{\operatorname{sd}(\text{score})}.
$$

It remains continuous and is modeled with a Gaussian likelihood.

## 32. Incremental model 0: wine means

The first lecture model ignores judges:

$$
S_i\sim\mathrm{Normal}(Q_{W_i},\sigma),
$$

$$
Q_w\sim\mathrm{Normal}(0,1),qquad
\sigma\sim\mathrm{Exponential}(1).
$$

This estimates one quality parameter per wine.

It attributes systematic differences among judges to residual variation. Consequently, it can estimate average wine scores but cannot explain why some judges use higher baselines or a wider score range.

---

## 33. Incremental model 1: wine origin

Let $X_w=-0.5$ for French wine and $+0.5$ for American wine.

A partially pooled quality model is:

$$
Q_w\sim\mathrm{Normal}(\beta_OX_w,1).
$$

The coefficient $\beta_O$ is the average US-minus-France location difference on the latent standardized quality scale.

This formulation avoids fitting both a completely unrestricted wine dummy vector and a redundant origin dummy in the same linear predictor. Origin locates the prior mean of each wine quality; each wine retains its own deviation.

With only 20 wines, the result pertains to this modeled population and its exchangeability assumptions. It is not a timeless causal effect of national origin.

---

## 34. Item-response interpretation

In educational IRT:

- a person has latent ability;
- an item has difficulty;
- an item may discriminate strongly or weakly among abilities.

In this wine application:

- the wine has latent quality $Q_w$;
- the judge has harshness $H_j$;
- the judge has discrimination $D_j$.

The judge response function is:

$$
\mu_{jw}=D_j(Q_w-H_j).
$$

Harshness is the wine quality required for the judge's expected standardized score to be zero.

Discrimination is the slope of expected score against latent wine quality.

If $D_j$ is near zero, the judge gives similar expected scores to wines of different quality. If $D_j$ is large, quality differences are amplified across the scoring scale.

---

## 35. Observation model

For rating $i$ made by judge $J_i$ of wine $W_i$:

$$
S_i\sim\mathrm{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma_S),
$$

$$
\mu_i=D_{J_i}(Q_{W_i}-H_{J_i}).
$$

The Gaussian likelihood is a pragmatic model for the standardized numeric score.

It assumes one residual scale and conditional independence after accounting for wine and judge parameters.

Posterior predictive checks should challenge these assumptions by judge, wine, flight, and score range.

Calling this an item-response model does not force a Bernoulli likelihood. IRT is a latent response-function architecture; the response distribution should match the observed outcome.

---

## 36. Three identification problems

### 36.1 Orientation

Signed discrimination creates the exact sign symmetry described above.

Repair:

$$
D_j=\exp(\ell_j)>0.
$$

### 36.2 Location

Without anchors, adding a constant $c$ to every wine quality and harshness leaves their difference unchanged:

$$
(Q_w+c)-(H_j+c)=Q_w-H_j.
$$

Repair: center the latent quality distribution through a defined prior mean and estimate harshness relative to that reference.

### 36.3 Scale

For $c>0$:

$$
D_j(Q_w-H_j)
=
\frac{D_j}{c}\{cQ_w-cH_j\}.
$$

If the latent quality scale and discrimination scale are both free, the likelihood contains a scale ridge.

Repair: fix the conditional latent quality standard deviation to one. This defines the unit in which harshness and discrimination are interpreted.

Identification is part of model specification, not a post-processing option.

---

## 37. Nationality model for harshness

Let $A_j=-0.5$ for French judges and $+0.5$ for American judges.

Use a non-centered hierarchy:

$$
z^{H}_j\sim\mathrm{Normal}(0,1),
$$

$$
H_j=alpha_H+\delta_HA_j+\sigma_Hz^{H}_j.
$$

Then:

$$
\mathbb E[H_j\mid A_j=+0.5]
-
\mathbb E[H_j\mid A_j=-0.5]
=\delta_H.
$$

Positive $\delta_H$ means American judges are harsher on average. Negative $\delta_H$ means they are more generous on average.

The hierarchy retains individual judge heterogeneity instead of replacing each judge with a nationality average.

---

## 38. Nationality model for discrimination

Model log discrimination:

$$
z^{D}_j\sim\mathrm{Normal}(0,1),
$$

$$
\ell_j=alpha_D+\delta_DA_j+\sigma_Dz^{D}_j,
$$

$$
D_j=\exp(\ell_j).
$$

The natural group contrast is multiplicative:

$$
\frac{D_{\mathrm{US}}}{D_{\mathrm{FR}}}
=
\exp(\delta_D).
$$

A ratio of one means equal average discrimination. A ratio above one means American judges have steeper response functions on average.

Interpreting $\delta_D$ itself as an additive discrimination difference would be wrong because it is on the log scale.

---

## 39. Complete generative model

The fitted model is:

$$
S_i\sim\mathrm{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma_S),
$$

$$
\mu_i=\exp(\ell_{J_i})(Q_{W_i}-H_{J_i}),
$$

$$
Q_w\sim\mathrm{Normal}(\delta_OX_w,1),
$$

$$
H_j=\alpha_H+\delta_HA_j+\sigma_Hz^H_j,
\qquad z^H_j\sim\mathrm{Normal}(0,1),
$$

$$
\ell_j=\alpha_D+\delta_DA_j+\sigma_Dz^D_j,
\qquad z^D_j\sim\mathrm{Normal}(0,1).
$$

Priors are:

$$
\alpha_H,\delta_H,\delta_O\sim\mathrm{Normal}(0,0.5),
$$

$$
\alpha_D,\delta_D\sim\mathrm{Normal}(0,0.35),
$$

$$
\sigma_H,\sigma_D\sim\mathrm{Exponential}(2),
$$

$$
\sigma_S\sim\mathrm{Exponential}(1).
$$

These priors are defined on standardized-score and log-slope scales. They must be evaluated through prior predictive response functions, not judged from parameter widths alone.

---

## 40. Why this model differs from the first A08 draft

The earlier draft placed nationality means inside judge priors but did not fully explain the latent symmetries.

The corrected model makes four commitments explicit:

1. discrimination is positive through a log parameter;
2. latent quality has fixed unit scale;
3. nationality coefficients are direct centered contrasts;
4. judge deviations use non-centered coordinates.

It also avoids deciding nationality difference from overlap between two marginal intervals. The posterior distribution of the contrast is computed directly.

---

## 41. Prior predictive checks

Before fitting, draw parameters from the prior and inspect judge response functions:

$$
q\mapsto D_j(q-H_j).
$$

Relevant checks include:

- expected scores over $q\in[-3,3]$;
- fraction of replicated standardized scores beyond $\pm4$;
- implied raw scores outside the observed scoring scale;
- prior distribution of $D_j$;
- nationality contrasts before data;
- probability that one judge uses an implausibly steep response function.

## 50. Why 180 ratings do not mean 180 judges

The nationality contrast varies at judge level.

There are nine independent judge identities, not 180 independent nationality units.

Repeated ratings improve estimation of each judge's response function. They do not create 180 independent replications of the French–American comparison.

This is a general multilevel principle:

> The effective replication level for a group contrast is the level at which group membership varies.

Ignoring this structure would produce overconfident nationality comparisons.

## 54. Parameter recovery

Before trusting the real-data interpretation, simulate from the fitted model class with known parameters.

Workflow:

1. preserve the observed 9-by-20 crossed design;
2. choose known $\delta_H$, $\delta_D$, and $\delta_O$;
3. generate judge latent effects;
4. generate wine qualities;
5. generate all 180 scores;
6. fit the same model;
7. verify recovery of contrasts and latent response functions;
8. repeat across weak, moderate, and strong signal scenarios.

Parameter recovery answers whether this design and implementation can recover known truth under the assumed model.

It does not show that the assumed model generated the real data.

## 55. Recovery simulation

```{r recovery-simulation}
simulate_irt <- function(
    judge_country_c,
    wine_origin_c,
    delta_H = 0.4,
    delta_D = log(1.3),
    delta_O = -0.2,
    sigma_H = 0.35,
    sigma_D = 0.20,
    sigma_score = 0.7,
    seed = 202608) {
  set.seed(seed)
  NJ <- length(judge_country_c)
  NW <- length(wine_origin_c)

  H <- delta_H * judge_country_c + sigma_H * rnorm(NJ)
  log_D <- delta_D * judge_country_c + sigma_D * rnorm(NJ)
  D <- exp(log_D)
  Q <- rnorm(NW, delta_O * wine_origin_c, 1)

  grid <- expand.grid(judge_id = seq_len(NJ), wine_id = seq_len(NW))
  mu <- D[grid$judge_id] * (Q[grid$wine_id] - H[grid$judge_id])
  grid$score_z <- rnorm(nrow(grid), mu, sigma_score)
  grid
}

sim_df <- simulate_irt(judge_country_c, wine_origin_c)
head(sim_df)
```

- interval coverage;
- RMSE;
- diagnostics failure rate;

## 56. Simulation-based calibration

SBC repeats:

1. draw parameters from the prior;
2. simulate data from those parameters;
3. fit the model;
4. rank each true parameter among its posterior draws.

Across replications, ranks should be approximately uniform if the generative code and inference implementation are calibrated.

SBC can reveal:

- coding bugs;
- inconsistent parameterizations;

## 59. Common errors exposed by A08

### Error 1: Calling MCMC exact

MCMC is asymptotically valid under conditions and has finite-simulation error.

### Error 2: Treating warmup as posterior draws

Stan changes its transition mechanism during adaptation.

### Error 3: Using one chain

One chain cannot reveal between-chain disagreement.

### Error 4: Declaring success from R-hat alone

Divergences, treedepth, BFMI, ESS, MCSE, and visual diagnostics remain relevant.

### Error 5: Using a universal ESS cutoff

Required precision depends on the estimand and raw draw count.

### Error 6: Thinning by default

Thinning usually throws away information. Retain draws unless storage or downstream constraints justify it.

### Error 7: Increasing iterations to repair geometry

More draws reproduce the same pathology more precisely.

### Error 8: Raising `adapt_delta` first

Identification, priors, scaling, and parameterization come first.

### Error 9: Allowing signed discrimination

This creates an exact orientation symmetry.

### Error 10: Estimating every latent scale freely

Quality and discrimination then trade scale along a ridge.

### Error 11: Treating 180 ratings as 180 judges

Nationality varies across only nine judges.

### Error 12: Comparing interval overlap

Compute the posterior of the contrast directly.

### Error 13: Interpreting log discrimination additively

Exponentiation yields the group ratio on the natural scale.

### Error 14: Calling Gaussian-score IRT Bernoulli

The A08 wine response is continuous. A09 introduces Bernoulli and binomial event models.

### Error 15: Presenting an optimizer as MCMC

Adam with weight decay produces an optimized point, not posterior chains.

### Error 16: Equating computational convergence with model truth

Well-sampled wrong models remain wrong models.

## 69. When should MCMC be used?

**Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)** is a family of algorithms for drawing dependent simulations from a probability distribution that is difficult to sample directly. A **Markov chain** is a sequence in which the distribution of the next simulated state depends on the current state; **Monte Carlo** means using random simulation for numerical calculation.

The distribution that the algorithm is intended to explore is called the **target distribution**. In Bayesian inference, this target is usually the posterior distribution:

$$
p(\theta\mid y)
\propto
p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta).
$$

The notation is:

- $y=(y_1,\ldots,y_N)$: the observed data, containing $N$ observational units;
- $\theta=(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_d)$: the vector of $d$ unknown scalar parameters;
- $p(\theta)$: the **prior distribution**, representing assumptions about $\theta$ before observing $y$. A **regularizing prior** assigns low probability to implausibly extreme parameter values and thereby stabilizes weakly informed directions;
- $p(y\mid\theta)$: the **likelihood**, viewed as a function of $\theta$ for the observed data;
- $p(\theta\mid y)$: the **posterior distribution**, representing uncertainty about $\theta$ after combining prior and data;
- $p(a\mid b)$: a conditional probability mass or probability density, read “the distribution of $a$ given $b$”;
- $p$: a probability mass function for discrete values or a probability density for continuous values;
- $\propto$: “proportional to,” meaning that the two sides differ only by a positive constant that does not depend on $\theta$.

The omitted proportionality constant is the **marginal likelihood**, also called the **evidence**:

$$
p(y)=\int p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta.
$$

The integral is taken over every possible value of the parameter vector $\theta$. MCMC is useful when this integral cannot be calculated analytically, but the product $p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta)$ can be evaluated up to its normalizing constant.

Suppose $h(\theta)$ is a declared posterior quantity of interest, such as one coefficient, a predicted probability, or a treatment contrast. Its posterior expectation is:

$$
\mathbb E[h(\theta)\mid y]
=
\int h(\theta)p(\theta\mid y)\,d\theta.
$$

Here $\mathbb E[\cdot\mid y]$ denotes an average with respect to the posterior distribution conditional on the observed data.

During **warmup**, the initial adaptation phase, the sampler tunes numerical settings and its draws are discarded. After warmup, let $\theta^{(s)}$ denote the parameter vector generated at retained iteration $s$, and let $S$ be the total number of retained iterations. MCMC replaces the integral by a sample average:

$$
\mathbb E[h(\theta)\mid y]
\approx
\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}h(\theta^{(s)}).
$$

This is called an **ergodic average**: under suitable Markov-chain conditions and as $S$ increases, the average along the dependent chain approaches the expectation under the target distribution. The draws need not be independent, but strong serial dependence reduces the information they contain.

This is powerful, but it is not automatic. Three conditions must be separated:

1. **Statistical validity:** the likelihood, prior distributions, and **estimand**—the precisely defined quantity to be estimated—form a meaningful model.
2. **Identification:** distinct scientifically meaningful parameter values imply sufficiently distinct distributions for the observed data, possibly with help from explicit prior information.
3. **Computational validity:** independently initialized Markov chains explore all relevant regions of high posterior probability accurately enough.

Passing the third condition does not establish the first two.

### 69.1 Domains where MCMC is especially useful

MCMC is most valuable when uncertainty must be propagated through a model with several interacting levels or latent quantities.

Typical domains include:

Here a **latent quantity** is a variable that belongs to the model but is not observed directly—for example, a person's ability or a time-varying volatility. It must be inferred from its effects on observed data.

The specialized terms in the table mean:

- **item response theory (IRT):** a family of models connecting an unobserved trait, such as ability or wine quality, to item- or judge-specific responses;
- **g-computation:** calculation of an outcome under specified interventions by averaging model-based conditional predictions over a target population;
- **state-space model:** a model separating an unobserved state process from the noisy observations generated by that process;
- **Gaussian process:** a probability model in which every finite collection of function values has a joint multivariate Gaussian distribution;
- **ordinary differential equation (ODE):** an equation describing how a continuously varying state changes through its derivatives.

| Domain | Typical model | Why MCMC is useful |
|---|---|---|
| Multilevel studies | varying intercepts and slopes | propagates group and population uncertainty jointly |
| Psychometrics | IRT, latent abilities, item effects | estimates latent traits and measurement uncertainty |
| Epidemiology | prevalence, transmission, missing infection states | combines observation and process models |
| Ecology | occupancy, capture–recapture, population dynamics | handles imperfect detection and latent populations |
| Clinical research | longitudinal, survival, measurement-error models | accommodates censoring, partial pooling, and nonlinear effects |
| Causal inference | g-computation, measurement error, latent confounding sensitivity | propagates uncertainty in nuisance and causal parameters |
| Time series | state-space and stochastic-volatility models | infers unobserved states and parameters together |
| Spatial analysis | Gaussian processes and spatial hierarchies | represents correlated latent fields |
| Engineering and physics | calibration, inverse problems, ODE models | quantifies uncertainty in nonlinear mechanistic parameters |
| Finance | stochastic volatility, hierarchical risk models | represents heavy tails, time-varying latent states, and parameter uncertainty |

MCMC is not restricted to causal inference. It is a posterior-computation method. Whether its output has a causal interpretation depends on the design, graph, assumptions, and estimand—not on the sampler.

### 69.2 Minimum conditions for application

Before choosing MCMC, verify the following.

#### A coherent generative model

The model must specify how the observed data could have arisen:

$$
\theta\sim p(\theta),
\qquad
y\sim p(y\mid\theta).
$$

The symbol $\sim$ is read “is distributed as.” The **support** is the set of values a variable is allowed to take. The support must be correct: a probability must stay in $[0,1]$, a scale must be positive, and an ordered quantity must remain ordered.

#### A proper posterior

The target distribution must integrate to a finite value:

$$
\int p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta < \infty.
$$

The differential $d\theta$ indicates integration over the complete parameter space. A **proper posterior** has finite total mass and can therefore be normalized to integrate to one. An **improper prior** has infinite total mass; it can sometimes yield a proper posterior, but this must be established rather than assumed. A chain can produce finite-looking values even when no valid posterior exists.

#### An identified parameterization

A **parameterization** is the chosen coordinate representation of the unknown quantities. Equivalent parameter configurations must be removed or constrained when they are not scientifically distinct.

Examples include:

- fixing the location and scale of an IRT latent trait;
- enforcing positive discrimination when the sign has no separate meaning;
- choosing a reference category;
- ordering mixture components when labels are otherwise exchangeable;
- separating a global intercept from a set of unconstrained group offsets.

#### A computable target density

For Metropolis-type MCMC, the density must be evaluable up to a multiplicative constant.

**Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC)** augments continuous parameters with **artificial momentum**, an auxiliary variable that determines the direction and scale of movement, and uses derivatives to propose distant states. The **No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS)** is an HMC algorithm that adapts trajectory length. Their log target density should normally be differentiable: small changes in a continuous parameter should produce a well-defined local slope. The **gradient** is the vector containing one such partial derivative for each parameter, and computing it must be affordable.

Stan cannot sample discrete parameters with HMC because gradients do not define movement between isolated values. Such parameters must be **marginalized**—summed out of the posterior—enumerated, or otherwise reformulated. Other frameworks may combine HMC with a discrete sampler, but **mixing**, the speed at which a chain forgets its starting state and explores the target, can become the bottleneck.

#### Enough replication for the scientific question

MCMC cannot manufacture information.

In the A08 wine model, 180 ratings do not provide 180 independent replications for the nationality effect. The nationality comparison is primarily informed by nine judges. This is a statistical limitation even if computation is flawless.

### 69.3 Dimension: how many parameters?

There is no universal maximum dimension for MCMC.

The important quantity is not only the nominal number of parameters $d$, but the posterior geometry:

- **correlation:** posterior dependence between parameter directions;
- **condition number:** a measure of how unequal the posterior scales or curvatures—the rates at which its log density changes—are across principal directions; a large value indicates a long, narrow geometry that is harder to explore;
- **funnels:** posterior regions whose width changes sharply as a scale parameter changes;
- **multimodality:** the presence of separated regions, called modes, with high posterior density;
- **boundaries:** edges of the allowed parameter space, such as a standard deviation approaching zero;
- **sparsity and conditional independence:** structure in which many parameters do not interact directly once other variables are known;
- **local latent variables:** unobserved quantities attached to individual observations or groups rather than shared globally;
- **gradient-evaluation cost:** the time and memory required to calculate the log density and all its parameter derivatives once.

A well-scaled posterior with 1,000 parameters can be easier for HMC than a two-parameter funnel.

The following regimes are indicative rather than hard limits:

| Continuous dimension | Practical interpretation for HMC/NUTS |
|---:|---|
| $d<20$ | usually easy if the posterior is identified and differentiable without sharp discontinuities; visual diagnostics remain possible |
| $20\le d<200$ | routine for many regressions and moderate hierarchical models |
| $200\le d<2{,}000$ | often feasible with good parameterization, vectorized array operations, and a diagonal or structured metric |
| $2{,}000\le d<20{,}000$ | feasible in some sparse or hierarchical models, but gradient cost, adaptation, memory, and diagnostics become central |
| $d\ge20{,}000$ | possible only for favorable structure and adequate hardware; local latent variables and saved draws often dominate cost |

These ranges are not performance guarantees.

#### Dense versus diagonal adaptation

In HMC, the **mass matrix** scales the artificial momentum and therefore the sampler movement in different parameter directions. A **dense** mass matrix contains **off-diagonal entries**, the cells outside its main top-left-to-bottom-right diagonal, and can adapt to pairwise posterior correlations. Its storage grows approximately as:

$$
O(d^2).
$$

The notation $O(d^2)$ describes **quadratic growth**: doubling $d$ multiplies the leading storage requirement by roughly four. Dense adaptation therefore becomes expensive as $d$ grows.

A **diagonal metric** stores one scale per parameter but no off-diagonal correlations. It grows as $O(d)$, meaning approximately linearly in $d$ and is much more scalable, but the model should be parameterized so that extreme correlations are not left for the sampler to solve.

#### Draw-storage cost

If $C$ is the number of chains, $S$ the retained draws per chain, and $P$ the number of stored scalar quantities, then 64-bit storage uses eight bytes per number. The minimum raw storage is approximately:

$$
8CSP\ \text{bytes}.
$$

For four chains, 2,000 draws, and 1,000 stored scalars, this is about 64 MB before metadata and diagnostics.

For 100,000 stored scalars, it is about 6.4 GB. Generated quantities and posterior predictions can require much more.

Do not save every latent or replicated quantity merely because it can be generated.

### 69.4 Data volume: how many observations?

The number of observations $N$ matters through the cost of evaluating the likelihood and its gradient.

Let $C$ be the number of chains, $S_{\mathrm{warmup}}$ the warmup iterations per chain, $S_{\mathrm{sample}}$ the retained iterations per chain, and $L$ the average number of leapfrog steps per iteration. Let $T_{\nabla}(N,d)$ be the time for one evaluation of the log density and its gradient for $N$ observations and $d$ parameters. A rough serial-equivalent computational time is:

$$
T_{\mathrm{MCMC}}
\approx
C(S_{\mathrm{warmup}}+S_{\mathrm{sample}})L T_{\nabla}(N,d).
$$

The left side, $T_{\mathrm{MCMC}}$, denotes the approximate total compute time; running chains in parallel can reduce elapsed wall-clock time without reducing total work. The formula explains why doubling the data can nearly double the runtime in a simple full-data model, while a costly ODE, Gaussian process, or matrix factorization may scale much worse.

Indicative data regimes are:

| Data volume | Typical assessment |
|---:|---|
| $N<10^4$ | usually comfortable for vectorized regression and moderate hierarchical models |
| $10^4\le N<10^5$ | commonly feasible; profiling, vectorization, and parallel chains matter |
| $10^5\le N<10^6$ | model dependent; full-data NUTS may be expensive and careful benchmarking is required |
| $N\ge10^6$ | ordinary full-data MCMC is rarely the first production choice unless the model has exceptional structure or substantial compute |

Again, these are engineering ranges, not statistical laws. A Gaussian process with $N=20{,}000$ may be harder than a vectorized logistic regression with $N=500{,}000$.

The **potential energy** in HMC is the negative log target density, up to an additive constant. A **leapfrog step** is one reversible numerical update of parameters and artificial momentum along an HMC trajectory. Its **step size** controls the numerical distance covered by one such update. Ordinary HMC requires the full potential energy and gradient at every leapfrog step. Here **mini-batching** means evaluating only a subset of observations at a given update. Naive mini-batching generally breaks the Hamiltonian trajectory and does not produce exact standard HMC. Specialized subsampling algorithms exist, but they require their own theory and diagnostics.

### 69.5 Advantages

When it works, MCMC provides several important benefits.

1. **Asymptotic exactness for the specified posterior.** Under the required regularity and convergence conditions, Monte Carlo error can be made arbitrarily small by increasing effective sampling. MCMC does not automatically replace the posterior by a Gaussian distribution or by a **factorized approximation** that treats parameter blocks as independent.
2. **Joint uncertainty propagation.** Posterior correlations and nonlinear transformations are retained.
3. **Flexible summaries.** Any scientifically meaningful derived quantity can be computed draw by draw.
4. **Hierarchical modeling.** **Partial pooling** estimates group effects using both group-specific observations and information shared across groups. Latent, or unobserved, structures are natural in this framework.
5. **Predictive simulation.** Prior and posterior predictive distributions follow directly from the generative model.
6. **Transparent numerical diagnostics.** R-hat, ESS, MCSE, divergences, treedepth, and energy diagnostics expose several failure modes.
7. **Reusable draws.** One fitted posterior can answer many predeclared or exploratory probability questions without refitting.

The diagnostic terms have specific meanings:

- **R-hat:** a comparison of within-chain and between-chain variation; values materially above one indicate that chains are not behaving as draws from one common **stationary distribution**, the stable long-run distribution that should equal the posterior target;
- **effective sample size (ESS):** the number of independent draws that would contain approximately the same information as the autocorrelated MCMC draws;
- **bulk ESS:** effective sample size for central posterior summaries such as means and medians;
- **tail ESS:** effective sample size relevant to tail quantiles and interval endpoints;
- **Monte Carlo standard error (MCSE):** estimated numerical uncertainty in a posterior summary caused by using a finite MCMC sample;
- **divergence:** a numerical warning that the discrete HMC trajectory failed to follow the continuous Hamiltonian dynamics accurately in a region of difficult curvature;
- **treedepth:** the number of binary trajectory doublings used by NUTS; reaching its maximum indicates that the sampler stopped extending a trajectory because of the configured limit;
- **energy Bayesian fraction of missing information (E-BFMI):** a diagnostic comparing movement in HMC total energy—the sum of negative log target density and artificial-momentum energy—with its marginal variation; a low value signals poor energy exploration;
- **acceptance probability:** the probability used by the **Metropolis correction**, an accept-or-reject step comparing the target density before and after a numerical proposal, to retain that proposal; a value extremely close to one can accompany an unnecessarily small step size.

For a nonlinear estimand $g(\theta)$, where $g$ is a declared function mapping the parameter vector to a scientific quantity, draw-wise computation is especially convenient:

$$
g^{(s)}=g(\theta^{(s)}).
$$

The **empirical distribution** assigns equal weight to the computed values $g^{(1)},\ldots,g^{(S)}$. A 90% **credible interval**, for example, is an interval containing 90% posterior probability according to these draws.

### 69.6 Costs and disadvantages

MCMC also has real costs.

1. **Runtime.** Thousands of gradient evaluations may be required for each chain.
2. **Serial dependence.** Consecutive draws can be correlated, meaning that one draw partly predicts the next. Ten thousand stored draws therefore do not imply an ESS of ten thousand.
3. **Warmup cost.** During **warmup**, the sampler tunes numerical settings such as step size and mass matrix; this tuning is called **adaptation**. Warmup draws are not retained as posterior draws, and the cost must be paid for each independent fit.
4. **Memory and storage.** Large latent fields and posterior predictive arrays can dominate the workflow.
5. **Parameterization sensitivity.** A centered hierarchy samples group effects directly around a population mean; a non-centered hierarchy samples standardized offsets and then scales them. These mathematically equivalent forms can have dramatically different sampling geometry.
6. **Difficult automation.** A successful process exit is not evidence of trustworthy sampling.
7. **Multimodality.** The **typical set** is the region containing most posterior probability mass, which need not be near the single highest-density point. HMC explores one connected typical set efficiently but may not cross separated modes.
8. **Operational latency.** MCMC is often unsuitable for millisecond predictions or frequent online refits.
9. **Model risk remains.** Precise sampling from a misspecified posterior is still a precise answer to the wrong model.

### 69.7 When MCMC is a strong default

MCMC is a strong choice when most of these conditions hold:

- full posterior uncertainty matters;
- the model is hierarchical, nonlinear, latent, **censored**—some values are known only to lie above or below a threshold—or explicitly models measurement error;
- analytic integration is unavailable;
- the log density and preferably its gradients are computable;
- the data fit in a workflow in which every likelihood evaluation uses all observations, called full-batch computation;
- runtime of minutes to hours is acceptable;
- independent chains and diagnostics can be afforded;
- the posterior can be identified with defensible constraints and priors;
- decisions depend on tails, nonlinear contrasts, or joint uncertainty rather than only a point estimate.

### 69.8 When another method may be preferable

MCMC is not always the right first tool.

| Situation | Candidate alternative | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Conjugate or low-dimensional analytic posterior | exact calculation or quadrature | faster and more transparent |
| Nearly Gaussian, well-identified posterior | Laplace approximation or `quap()` | inexpensive approximation may be adequate |
| Very large data with repeated refits | variational inference, INLA for supported latent Gaussian models, or problem-specific approximations | lower latency and memory |
| Streaming or online decisions | sequential Monte Carlo, filtering, stochastic optimization | updates without a complete refit |
| Strongly multimodal posterior | tempered MCMC, sequential Monte Carlo, nested sampling, or model reformulation | ordinary HMC may stay within one mode |
| Discrete combinatorial state space | marginalization, dynamic programming, particle methods, or specialized discrete MCMC | HMC cannot move through discrete states |
| Only prediction is required and interpretation is secondary | validated predictive model | full posterior structure may not justify its cost |
| Repeated millisecond predictions | fit offline and deploy a compact predictive representation | MCMC belongs in training, not the request path |

The alternatives in the table solve different computational problems:

- a **conjugate model** has a prior–likelihood pair for which the posterior stays in a known distribution family;
- **quadrature** approximates an integral using a weighted sum of function evaluations and is most useful in low dimension;
- a **Laplace approximation** replaces the posterior near its mode by a Gaussian distribution based on local curvature;
- **variational inference** optimizes a tractable distribution to approximate the posterior, often gaining speed at the cost of approximation error;
- **INLA**, integrated nested Laplace approximation, is a specialized deterministic method for supported **latent Gaussian models**, whose unobserved variables have a joint Gaussian distribution;
- **sequential Monte Carlo (SMC)** evolves and reweights a population of particles through a sequence of distributions;
- **filtering** updates the distribution of a latent state sequentially as new time-ordered observations arrive;
- **tempered MCMC** introduces intermediate flattened target distributions to help chains move between separated modes;
- **particle methods** represent a distribution by weighted simulated states called particles;
- **nested sampling** explores nested likelihood regions and is designed especially for marginal-likelihood calculation and some multimodal targets.

An approximation should be validated against MCMC on a smaller representative problem when practical.

### 69.9 How MCMC fails

Failure should be divided into computational, statistical, and scientific categories.

#### Computational failure

| Symptom | Likely cause | Appropriate response |
|---|---|---|
| R-hat above 1.01 | chains have not mixed to the same distribution | inspect traces and ranks; run longer only after checking geometry |
| low bulk ESS | strong autocorrelation or slow exploration | rescale, reparameterize, improve metric, then reconsider draw count |
| low tail ESS | tail probabilities or intervals are unstable | improve geometry and increase effective sampling |
| high MCSE | reported digits are dominated by simulation error | obtain more effective draws or report less precision |
| divergences | funnel, boundary, curvature, or numerical instability | reparameterize; strengthen priors; inspect implicated parameters |
| maximum treedepth | long trajectories caused by difficult geometry | reparameterize and rescale before increasing the limit |
| low E-BFMI | poor momentum–energy exploration | inspect scaling and parameterization |
| chains occupy different modes | multimodality or non-identification | identify symmetry, impose defensible constraints, or use a multimodal method |
| saturated acceptance probability | step size may be unnecessarily small | inspect adaptation and efficiency, not acceptance alone |

More iterations are a reasonable remedy only when the chain is sampling the correct stationary distribution but Monte Carlo precision is insufficient.

More iterations do not repair:

- divergences;
- a missing mode;
- label switching;
- an improper posterior;
- structural non-identification;
- incorrect indexing;
- a wrong likelihood;
- a wrong causal estimand.

#### Statistical failure

The sampler may converge while the posterior remains scientifically weak.

Examples include:

- too few groups to estimate a population-level variance or group contrast;
- **complete or quasi-separation**, in which predictors perfectly or almost perfectly classify a binary outcome, under weak logistic priors;
- a likelihood–prior combination that leaves a **ridge**, meaning a long set of parameter combinations with nearly equal posterior density;
- latent scale and location not anchored;
- weak data that make conclusions almost entirely prior driven;
- a model with more flexibility than the data can identify.

These problems require more information, stronger justified priors, a simpler estimand, or a redesigned study.

#### Scientific failure

Perfect computation cannot correct:

- selection bias;
- unmeasured confounding;
- measurement error omitted from the model;
- inappropriate **exchangeability** assumptions, which treat units as governed by the same probability mechanism before their labels are observed;
- **outcome leakage**, in which information unavailable at prediction time enters the model during fitting;
- extrapolation outside **data support**, meaning prediction for combinations not represented by the observed data;
- a mismatch between the fitted contrast and the decision question.

Posterior predictive checks can detect some model–data discrepancies, but they cannot prove the causal graph or the scientific assumptions.

### 69.10 Geometries that commonly defeat naive MCMC

#### Funnel geometry

A hierarchical scale near zero compresses local effects into a narrow neck while larger scales create a wide region. A centered parameterization may force HMC to use one step size for incompatible curvature.

Typical repair: use a **non-centered parameterization**, representing each local effect as a standardized variable multiplied by its group scale rather than sampling the effect directly.

#### Strong ridges

Two parameters may compensate for one another:

$$
\theta_1+\theta_2\approx c.
$$

Typical repair: identify the meaningful combination, center predictors, or introduce justified information.

#### Separated modes

Mixtures, sign symmetries, and latent-label permutations can generate distant regions of high posterior mass.

Typical repair: remove non-scientific symmetry, marginalize labels, impose an interpretable ordering, or use a method designed for multimodality.

#### Hard boundaries

Scale parameters near zero, probabilities near zero or one, and ordered parameters can produce severe curvature.

Typical repair: use unconstrained transformations and priors that avoid implausible boundary concentration.

#### Weakly identified tails

Heavy-tailed priors combined with weak likelihoods can let trajectories wander into extreme regions.

Typical repair: use scientifically plausible **regularizing priors**, which assign low probability to implausibly extreme parameter values, and conduct prior predictive checks.

### 69.11 Examples

#### Example A — a good MCMC application

Suppose 50 hospitals contribute binary treatment outcomes, with varying patient mix and unequal sample sizes.

A hierarchical logistic model estimates:

- the population treatment association;
- hospital-specific deviations;
- the between-hospital scale;
- standardized risk differences.

The model may contain a few hundred continuous parameters and tens of thousands of observations. Vectorized HMC with regularizing priors and a non-centered hierarchy is often appropriate.

MCMC is valuable because partial pooling and nonlinear probability-scale contrasts must be propagated jointly.

#### Example B — A08 wine ratings

The A08 model has 180 observed ratings, 20 wines, and 9 judges. Its parameter count is moderate and full-data HMC is inexpensive.

The main risks are not volume or dimension. They are:

- sign and scale non-identification in the IRT response function;
- weak group-level information from only nine judges;
- sensitivity of the nationality contrast to individual judges;
- the possibility that Gaussian residual assumptions are inadequate.

Anchoring latent quality and enforcing positive discrimination repair the principal computational symmetry. They do not create additional nationality replication.

#### Example C — a large but favorable regression

A logistic regression with 500,000 rows and 30 coefficients is low dimensional but data heavy.

Full-data NUTS may still work if the likelihood is vectorized and hardware is adequate, but every leapfrog step scans the data. Benchmark a short warmup and estimate total gradient cost before committing to the final run.

If the model must be refitted hourly, a Laplace or variational approximation may be more appropriate operationally.

#### Example D — a small but difficult model

Neal's funnel may have only a handful of dimensions yet generate many divergences in a centered parameterization.

The small parameter count does not make it easy. Geometry dominates dimension.

#### Example E — mixture-model failure

A two-component mixture combines two component distributions. Let $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ denote their location parameters. The labels are **exchangeable** when swapping component 1 and component 2 leaves the likelihood unchanged:

$$
(\mu_1,\mu_2)
\quad\text{and}\quad
(\mu_2,\mu_1)
$$

describe the same likelihood.

Separate chains may settle in different labelings, yielding poor R-hat and meaningless component-wise averages. More draws inside each mode do not solve the labeling symmetry.

#### Example F — a poor first use of full MCMC

Consider 100 million clickstream events, millions of user-specific latent effects, and a requirement to refit every ten minutes.

Naive full-batch NUTS would require repeated scans of the entire dataset at every leapfrog step and enormous draw storage. An aggregated model, stochastic approximation, variational method, filtering approach, or two-stage workflow is a more plausible starting point.

MCMC may still be used on representative subsets to validate the approximation.

### 69.12 A practical decision gate

Use the following sequence before launching a final MCMC fit.

#### Gate 1 — scientific target

- Is the estimand written mathematically?
- Does the data-generating story connect observations to that estimand?
- Are causal claims supported by a design and a **directed acyclic graph (DAG)** whose arrows encode the assumed causal relations, rather than by MCMC alone?

#### Gate 2 — identification and priors

- Are location, scale, sign, and label symmetries resolved?
- Is the posterior expected to be proper?
- Do **prior predictive simulations**—data generated from parameters drawn from the prior before conditioning on observed data—produce plausible outcomes?

#### Gate 3 — computational budget

- How long does one gradient evaluation take?
- How many leapfrog steps occur in a pilot run?
- What are the expected draw-storage and posterior-predictive costs?
- Can at least four final chains be run?

#### Gate 4 — pilot recovery

- Can simulated parameters and, more importantly, the target estimands be recovered when synthetic data are generated from known values and the model is refitted?
- Does the implementation pass on known limiting cases?
- Do independent implementations agree within Monte Carlo error when cross-language parity is required?

#### Gate 5 — final diagnostics

- R-hat below 1.01 for reported quantities;
- adequate bulk and tail ESS;
- MCSE small relative to reported precision;
- zero unexplained divergences;
- no unexplained treedepth saturation;
- acceptable energy behavior;
- consistent **trace plots**, which show sampled values by iteration, and **rank plots**, which compare the distribution of pooled draw ranks across chains.

#### Gate 6 — model validation

- **posterior predictive checks**—comparisons between observed data and replicated data simulated from the fitted model—target the scientific failure modes;
- sensitivity to priors and influential groups is reported;
- validation on **held-out data**, excluded during fitting, or genuinely external data matches the intended unit to which predictions will be generalized;
- limitations distinguish computational certainty from scientific certainty.

### 69.13 Compact recommendation matrix

| Condition | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Moderate $N$, continuous identified hierarchy, full uncertainty required | use HMC/NUTS as a strong default |
| High $d$ but sparse and well-scaled structure | benchmark HMC; control stored quantities and use appropriate parameterization |
| Very large $N$ with a small regular parameter vector | benchmark full-data HMC against Laplace or variational methods |
| Millions of local latent variables | reformulate, marginalize, aggregate, or use a scalable approximation |
| Discrete latent states | marginalize or use specialized discrete/particle methods |
| Strong multimodality | ordinary NUTS alone is insufficient |
| Divergent hierarchical model | non-center or redesign before collecting more draws |
| Weak scientific identification | obtain more information or narrow the question; sampling longer is not a solution |
| Real-time prediction requirement | fit offline and deploy a cheaper predictive representation |
| High-stakes tail probability | prefer validated MCMC when feasible and verify tail ESS and MCSE explicitly |

### 69.14 Final rule

MCMC should be used when its extra fidelity changes the quality of the scientific answer enough to justify its computational and diagnostic cost.

The decision is not:

$$
\text{small model} \Rightarrow \text{MCMC},
\qquad
\text{large model} \Rightarrow \text{no MCMC}.
$$

It is:

$$
\text{scientific estimand}
+
\text{identification}
+
\text{posterior geometry}
+
\text{gradient cost}
+
\text{required accuracy}
+
\text{operational budget}.
$$

The most dangerous MCMC failure is not always a visible divergence. It is a cleanly converged computation for a model that cannot identify, predict, or causally answer the question being reported.
