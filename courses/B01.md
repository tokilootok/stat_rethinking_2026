## 1. Main thesis

The central argument is:

> **A multilevel model estimates group-specific effects and the population distribution of those effects simultaneously.**

This produces **partial pooling**.

Each group is informed by:

1. its own observations;
2. the observations from other groups;
3. the inferred amount of variation among groups.

The workflow is:

$$
\text{clustered generative process}
\rightarrow
\text{group-specific parameters}
\rightarrow
\text{population distribution of parameters}
\rightarrow
\text{adaptive regularization}
\rightarrow
\text{partial pooling}
\rightarrow
\text{predictions for observed and new groups}
$$

A multilevel model does not force every group to be identical.

It also does not treat every group as unrelated.

Instead:

$$
\text{complete pooling}
\longleftrightarrow
\text{partial pooling}
\longleftrightarrow
\text{no pooling}
$$

The data learn where the model should lie between the two extremes.

## 3. Why clustered data require memory

Many datasets contain repeated observations within units.

Examples include:

- tadpoles within tanks;
- responses within participants;
- judgments within stories;
- transactions within customers;
- customers within stores;
- products within categories;
- repeated measurements within patients;
- students within schools.

Observations from the same cluster often share unmeasured causes.

If observations $i$ and $k$ belong to group $j$, then generally:

$$
Y_{ij}
\not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp
Y_{kj}
$$

after conditioning only on global predictors.

A group-specific effect can represent the shared environment:

$$
Y_{ij}
\sim
p
\left(
Y_{ij}
\mid
\alpha_j,
X_{ij},
\theta
\right)
$$

The model has “memory” because learning about group $j$ is retained and can also inform the population of groups.

## 4. Three pooling strategies

Suppose each group $j$ has its own baseline parameter $\alpha_j$.

### 4.1 Complete pooling

One parameter is shared by all groups:

$$
\alpha_j
=
\alpha
$$

for every $j$.

The model assumes no residual group heterogeneity.

Advantages:

- low variance;
- simple;
- stable for sparse groups.

Disadvantages:

- underfits genuine between-group variation;
- can produce overconfident individual-group predictions;
- may mask important cluster structure.

### 4.2 No pooling

Every group receives an unrelated parameter:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
p_0(\alpha_j)
$$

independently under a fixed broad prior.

Advantages:

- allows arbitrary group differences;
- can reproduce group-level sample proportions closely.

Disadvantages:

- overfits sparse groups;
- does not share information;
- estimates extreme values from small samples;
- cannot learn a coherent population distribution adaptively.

### 4.3 Partial pooling

Group parameters share a learned population distribution:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

The hyperparameters are also estimated:

$$
\bar{\alpha}
\sim
p
\left(
\bar{\alpha}
\right)
$$

$$
\sigma_\alpha
\sim
p
\left(
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

This model learns how similar the groups are.

## 5. Varying effects

McElreath generally prefers **varying effects** over “random effects.”

A varying intercept model gives every group its own intercept:

$$
\alpha_j
$$

while assuming those intercepts arise from a common population.

The word “varying” emphasizes what the model does:

$$
\text{intercept varies by group}
$$

It avoids the misleading impression that:

- some parameters are random while others are not;
- varying effects are merely nuisance terms;
- population-level parameters are fixed constants known without uncertainty.

In Bayesian modeling, every unknown parameter has a posterior distribution.

---

## 6. The multilevel generative model

A generic two-level Gaussian model is:

### Observation level

$$
Y_{ij}
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\alpha_j,
\sigma_Y
\right)
$$

### Group level

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

### Hyperpriors

$$
\bar{\alpha}
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
m_\alpha,
s_\alpha
\right)
$$

$$
\sigma_\alpha
\sim
\operatorname{Exponential}
\left(
\lambda_\alpha
\right)
$$

The joint posterior is:

$$
p
\left(
\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_J,
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha,
\sigma_Y
\mid
D
\right)
$$

All levels are estimated simultaneously.

## 7. Adaptive priors

For a no-pooling model, the prior on each group effect is fixed before fitting:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(0,1.5)
$$

In a multilevel model:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

The prior itself depends on unknown parameters learned from all groups.

It is therefore an **adaptive prior**.

The posterior for group $j$ is shaped by:

$$
p(\alpha_j \mid D)
\propto
p(D_j \mid \alpha_j)
p
\left(
\alpha_j
\mid
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

But $\bar{\alpha}$ and $\sigma_\alpha$ are themselves learned using all groups:

$$
D_1,\ldots,D_J
$$

This is the mechanism by which the model remembers across groups.

---

## 8. Partial pooling and shrinkage

Partial pooling commonly moves group estimates toward the population mean.

This is called **shrinkage**.

For an approximately Gaussian group estimate:

$$
\widehat{\alpha}_j
$$

with sampling variance:

$$
V_j
$$

and a population model:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha^2
\right)
$$

the posterior mean has the schematic form:

$$
\mathbb{E}
\left[
\alpha_j
\mid D
\right]
\approx
w_j
\widehat{\alpha}_j
+
\left(
1-w_j
\right)
\bar{\alpha}
$$

where:

$$
w_j
=
\frac{
\sigma_\alpha^2
}{
\sigma_\alpha^2+V_j
}
$$

This formula is exact only in certain Gaussian settings, but it captures the logic.

### More data in group $j$

When $V_j$ is small:

$$
w_j
\rightarrow
1
$$

The group estimate remains near its own data.

### Less data in group $j$

When $V_j$ is large:

$$
w_j
\rightarrow
0
$$

The estimate moves more strongly toward the population mean.

### More genuine group heterogeneity

When $\sigma_\alpha$ is large, pooling weakens.

### More homogeneous groups

When $\sigma_\alpha$ is small, pooling strengthens.

Shrinkage is therefore adaptive to both sample size and estimated population variation.

---

## 9. Shrinkage is not bias correction

Partial pooling deliberately introduces bias toward the population mean for individual groups.

This can reduce total expected error.

The bias–variance trade-off is:

$$
\operatorname{MSE}
=
\operatorname{Bias}^2
+
\operatorname{Variance}
$$

No-pooling estimates have little pooling bias but can have high variance.

Complete-pooling estimates have low variance but can have large bias for unusual groups.

Partial pooling aims to improve average out-of-sample accuracy by accepting modest shrinkage bias in exchange for a larger reduction in variance.

This is regularization, not an attempt to make every group look average.

## 10. The café golem

The official B01 script introduces a population of cafés.

Each café has a latent waiting-time intensity.

Visits produce observed waiting-time counts.

A simplified model is:

$$
Y_i
\sim
\operatorname{Poisson}
\left(
\lambda_i
\right)
$$

$$
\log(\lambda_i)
=
\alpha_{C_i}
$$

where $C_i$ identifies the café.

The café effects arise from a population:

$$
\alpha_j
=
\bar{\alpha}
+
\sigma_\alpha z_j
$$

$$
z_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(0,1)
$$

with:

$$
\bar{\alpha}
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(2,0.5)
$$

$$
\sigma_\alpha
\sim
\operatorname{Exponential}(10)
$$

The script uses a non-centered representation directly.

## 11. Sequential learning in the café example

The animation begins with no café observations.

At that point, every café prediction is drawn from the same prior population.

As visits occur:

- the visited café's posterior changes directly;
- the population mean and variance may change;
- unvisited cafés can change indirectly because their prior population has changed;
- cafés with little information remain close to the population distribution;
- cafés with much information become more individually determined.

This is the key multilevel phenomenon:

> An observation from one group can change predictions for another group through the learned population distribution.

In a no-pooling model, a café observation would affect only that café.

In a multilevel model, the information propagates.

---

## 12. Population of cafés versus observed cafés

The model estimates two kinds of quantities.

### Café-specific effects

$$
\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_J
$$

These describe cafés observed in the sample.

### Population distribution

$$
\alpha_{\mathrm{new}}
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

This describes a new café drawn from the modeled population.

The distinction becomes important for prediction.

For an observed café $j$:

$$
p
\left(
Y_{\mathrm{new}}
\mid
C=j,D
\right)
$$

uses the posterior of $\alpha_j$.

For a new café:

$$
p
\left(
Y_{\mathrm{new}}
\mid
C=\mathrm{new},D
\right)
$$

must integrate over a newly drawn group effect.

## 13. The reed-frog data

The official script uses 48 tadpole tanks.

For tank $j$:

- $S_j$ is the number of survivors;
- $D_j$ is the initial number of tadpoles;
- $T_j$ identifies the tank.

The observation model is:

$$
S_j
\sim
\operatorname{Binomial}
\left(
D_j,
p_j
\right)
$$

The varying-intercept model is:

$$
\operatorname{logit}(p_j)
=
\alpha_{T_j}
$$

The group distribution is:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

with priors:

$$
\bar{\alpha}
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(0,1.5)
$$

$$
\sigma_\alpha
\sim
\operatorname{Exponential}(1)
$$

## 14. No-pooling tadpole model

A no-pooling version gives every tank a separate parameter under a fixed prior:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(0,1.5)
$$

This model does not learn how much tank intercepts vary.

Each tank is estimated independently, except for sharing the same fixed prior specification.

Observed survival proportions are:

$$
\widehat{p}_j
=
\frac{S_j}{D_j}
$$

Small tanks can produce extreme proportions:

$$
0
$$

or:

$$
1
$$

from only a few outcomes.

No pooling tends to preserve these extremes too strongly.

---

## 15. Partial-pooling tadpole model

The multilevel model estimates:

$$
\bar{\alpha}
$$

and:

$$
\sigma_\alpha
$$

from the 48 tanks.

Posterior tank probabilities are:

$$
p_j^{(s)}
=
\operatorname{logit}^{-1}
\left(
\alpha_j^{(s)}
\right)
$$

The posterior mean estimate is:

$$
\widehat{p}_j
=
\frac{1}{S}
\sum_{s=1}^{S}
p_j^{(s)}
$$

The estimates move toward the population center, especially for tanks with fewer tadpoles.

The model does not shrink equally on the probability scale because the hierarchy is Gaussian on the log-odds scale.

---

## 16. Pooling and tank size

The tanks have different initial counts.

A tank with:

$$
D_j=10
$$

contains less information about its survival probability than a tank with:

$$
D_j=35
$$

all else equal.

Therefore, smaller tanks are pooled more strongly.

This is visible in the difference between:

- raw survival proportion;
- partially pooled posterior probability.

The model automatically accounts for uncertainty from the binomial likelihood.

No manual sample-size weighting is required.

---

## 17. Extreme groups shrink most visibly

Groups with extreme raw outcomes often move substantially toward the population mean.

This is not because the model forbids extreme true groups.

It is because extreme sample proportions from small groups are often sampling noise.

For example:

$$
S_j=0
$$

out of:

$$
D_j=10
$$

does not imply:

$$
p_j=0
$$

Likewise:

$$
S_j=D_j
$$

does not imply:

$$
p_j=1
$$

A multilevel posterior combines the group likelihood with evidence about the population of tanks.

## 18. Adaptive regularization

The script compares a model that estimates $\sigma_\alpha$ with one that fixes it.

Estimated hierarchy:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

Fixed hierarchy:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
1
\right)
$$

The estimated model can learn the appropriate pooling strength.

This is adaptive regularization.

The hyperparameter $\sigma_\alpha$ controls flexibility:

### Small $\sigma_\alpha$

$$
\alpha_j
\approx
\bar{\alpha}
$$

Strong pooling and low effective complexity.

### Large $\sigma_\alpha$

Group parameters can differ widely.

Weak pooling and greater effective complexity.

---

## 19. Effective number of parameters

A varying-intercept model may contain many nominal parameters:

$$
\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_J
$$

But strong shrinkage means they do not behave like $J$ fully independent parameters.

Predictive criteria estimate an effective complexity such as:

$$
p_{\mathrm{WAIC}}
$$

or its PSIS-related analogues.

If $\sigma_\alpha$ is near zero, many group effects contribute little effective flexibility.

Thus:

$$
\text{nominal parameter count}
\neq
\text{effective parameter count}
$$

Multilevel models can include many group effects without incurring the full overfitting cost of no pooling.

---

## 20. Partial pooling as an overfitting solution

The official script varies a fixed hierarchy scale across a sequence.

As $\sigma_\alpha$ increases:

- group estimates move closer to raw proportions;
- in-sample fit improves;
- model flexibility increases;
- out-of-sample predictive performance can eventually worsen.

As $\sigma_\alpha$ decreases:

- estimates collapse toward the common mean;
- variance decreases;
- underfitting can increase.

The learned posterior for $\sigma_\alpha$ allows the data and prior to negotiate this trade-off.

The model searches continuously between complete and no pooling instead of choosing one extreme.

---

## 21. Simulation evidence for partial pooling

Chapter 13 simulates ponds with known survival probabilities and compares:

- no-pooling estimates;
- partial-pooling estimates.

For pond $j$, absolute error is:

$$
E_j
=
\left|
\widehat{p}_j-p_j^{\mathrm{true}}
\right|
$$

Across repeated or many simulated groups, partial pooling tends to reduce average error:

$$
\frac{1}{J}
\sum_{j=1}^{J}
E_j^{\mathrm{partial}}
<
\frac{1}{J}
\sum_{j=1}^{J}
E_j^{\mathrm{no\ pool}}
$$

especially for small groups.

Partial pooling is not guaranteed to improve every individual group estimate.

Its advantage is average performance over the population of groups.

## 22. Adding a group-level predictor

The official script adds predator presence:

$$
P_j
=
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{predator present} \\
0 & \text{predator absent}
\end{cases}
$$

The model is:

$$
S_j
\sim
\operatorname{Binomial}
\left(
D_j,p_j
\right)
$$

$$
\operatorname{logit}(p_j)
=
\alpha_j
+
\beta_P P_j
$$

with:

$$
\beta_P
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(0,0.5)
$$

and:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

The global predictor explains systematic variation.

The remaining $\alpha_j$ values represent residual tank heterogeneity after accounting for predators.

## 23. Explained and residual group variation

The posterior of $\sigma_\alpha$ can change after adding predictors.

Without predators:

$$
\sigma_\alpha
$$

captures all modeled tank-to-tank variation.

With predators:

$$
\sigma_\alpha
$$

captures variation not explained by $P_j$.

A reduction in $\sigma_\alpha$ suggests that predator status explains part of the between-tank variation.

It does not prove that predator status is causal.

The causal interpretation still requires:

- treatment assignment knowledge;
- no relevant confounding;
- correct timing;
- no selection bias.

Multilevel structure and causal identification are separate issues.

---

## 24. Exchangeability

Partial pooling assumes groups are exchangeable conditional on modeled predictors.

Informally, before observing the group outcomes, group labels carry no additional information beyond their predictors.

Mathematically, the prior is symmetric:

$$
p
\left(
\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_J
\mid
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
=
\prod_{j=1}^{J}
p
\left(
\alpha_j
\mid
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

The labels can be permuted without changing the prior.

Exchangeability does not mean groups are identical.

It means their differences are modeled as draws from a common population after conditioning on known structure.

### 24.1 When exchangeability is implausible

A single shared population may be inappropriate when groups differ structurally by:

- country;
- product class;
- treatment regime;
- measurement system;
- historical period;
- business unit;
- eligibility process.

These differences should be modeled with predictors, separate populations, or richer covariance structures.

---

## 25. Hierarchy is a scientific assumption

The statement:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

asserts that groups are generated by a population distribution.

It is not merely a computational trick.

The normal population model may be wrong when group effects are:

- multimodal;
- strongly skewed;
- heavy-tailed;
- divided into latent classes;
- spatially or temporally correlated.

A multilevel model can still be useful as an approximation, but the population assumption should be checked.

---

## 26. Prior predictive checks for a hierarchy

The official script examines priors on:

$$
\bar{\alpha}
$$

and:

$$
\sigma_\alpha
$$

Then it generates group effects:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

For a logistic model:

$$
p_j
=
\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\alpha_j)
$$

A proper prior predictive check should inspect:

- population-average survival;
- spread of survival probabilities across tanks;
- probability of near-zero or near-one groups;
- number of extreme groups among 48 tanks;
- replicated survival counts by tank size.

Priors on hierarchy scales can create unexpectedly extreme group populations.

---

## 27. Hyperpriors and near-zero variation

A hierarchy-scale prior might be:

$$
\sigma_\alpha
\sim
\operatorname{Exponential}(1)
$$

This places greatest density near zero while allowing larger values.

Near-zero group variation means:

$$
\alpha_j
\approx
\bar{\alpha}
$$

The model approaches complete pooling.

A scale prior should be chosen on the parameter scale used in the hierarchy.

For logistic intercepts, $\sigma_\alpha$ is measured in log-odds units, not probability units.

Prior predictive simulation is therefore more interpretable than the coefficient prior alone.

---

## 28. Centered parameterization

The centered hierarchy is:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

The sampler works directly with:

$$
\alpha_j
$$

When data are weak and $\sigma_\alpha$ can approach zero, the posterior can form a funnel:

- group effects must lie very close to $\bar{\alpha}$ when $\sigma_\alpha$ is small;
- they can spread widely when $\sigma_\alpha$ is large.

This geometry can produce:

- divergences;
- high autocorrelation;
- low effective sample size.

---

## 29. Non-centered parameterization

The same hierarchy can be written:

$$
z_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(0,1)
$$

$$
\alpha_j
=
\bar{\alpha}
+
\sigma_\alpha z_j
$$

The scientific model is unchanged.

The sampled geometry changes.

The standardized effects $z_j$ remain on a stable scale even when $\sigma_\alpha$ is near zero.

This often improves HMC performance for:

- many groups;
- little information per group;
- small between-group variation.

The café model and the final trolley model in the official script use non-centered forms.

---

## 30. Centered versus non-centered is computational

The two parameterizations imply the same prior:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

Therefore, when both sample correctly:

$$
p
\left(
\alpha,
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\mid D
\right)
$$

should be the same.

The choice affects:

- posterior geometry;
- divergences;
- effective sample size;
- computation time.

It does not alter the estimand or causal assumptions.

Neither parameterization is universally best.

---

## 31. Multiple clustering dimensions

A dataset can contain more than one grouping structure.

In the trolley data:

- each response belongs to a participant;
- each response also belongs to a story.

The cluster dimensions are crossed:

$$
\text{participant}
\times
\text{story}
$$

A varying-intercept model can include:

$$
\alpha^{\mathrm{person}}_{U_i}
$$

and:

$$
\alpha^{\mathrm{story}}_{S_i}
$$

in the same linear predictor.

This avoids attributing persistent participant differences to story effects or vice versa.

## 32. Trolley model with varying intercepts

The official script fits an ordered-logistic model.

Let:

- $R_i$ be the ordered response;
- $U_i$ identify the participant;
- $S_i$ identify the story;
- $A_i$, $I_i$, and $C_i$ encode experimental manipulations;
- $E_i$ encode education;
- $Y_i$ be standardized age;
- $G_i$ encode gender category.

The latent ordered-logistic predictor includes:

$$
\phi_i
=
\text{population predictors}
+
\alpha^{\mathrm{story}}_{S_i}
+
\alpha^{\mathrm{person}}_{U_i}
$$

The varying intercepts are:

$$
\alpha^{\mathrm{story}}_s
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
0,
\sigma_S
\right)
$$

$$
\alpha^{\mathrm{person}}_u
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
0,
\sigma_U
\right)
$$

with:

$$
\sigma_S
\sim
\operatorname{Exponential}(1)
$$

$$
\sigma_U
\sim
\operatorname{Exponential}(1)
$$

The script also provides a non-centered version.

## 33. What the two trolley hierarchies mean

$\sigma_S$ measures residual variation among stories after accounting for the modeled experimental features.

$\sigma_U$ measures persistent variation among participants after accounting for observed participant predictors.

If:

$$
\sigma_U
>
\sigma_S
$$

the model suggests greater unexplained heterogeneity among participants than stories on the latent response scale.

This is not a simple variance decomposition on the observed ordinal scale.

The ordered-logistic link transforms the latent predictor into category probabilities.

## 34. Repeated measures and pseudoreplication

Ignoring participant effects treats repeated responses from one participant as independent observations.

This can create:

- underestimated uncertainty;
- overconfident treatment coefficients;
- incorrect weighting of highly observed participants;
- poor predictions for individual participants.

A varying participant intercept represents stable participant-specific propensity.

It does not automatically handle every repeated-measure issue.

Time trends, carryover, treatment history, and within-person autocorrelation may require richer models.

---

## 35. Varying intercepts versus fixed indicators

A model with one indicator parameter per group under unrelated priors can fit group means.

A multilevel varying-intercept model adds:

$$
\bar{\alpha}
$$

and:

$$
\sigma_\alpha
$$

and makes the group priors adaptive.

The key difference is not whether there is one coefficient per group.

Both approaches have that.

The difference is:

$$
\text{independent fixed priors}
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\text{learned common population}
$$

The multilevel model shares information and supports coherent prediction for new groups.

---

## 36. Full Bayes versus empirical Bayes

An empirical-Bayes procedure estimates hyperparameters first:

$$
\widehat{\bar{\alpha}},
\widehat{\sigma}_\alpha
$$

and then treats them as fixed while estimating group effects.

A fully Bayesian model retains uncertainty:

$$
p
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\mid D
\right)
$$

and propagates it into:

$$
p
\left(
\alpha_j
\mid D
\right)
$$

and new-group predictions.

When the number of groups is small, hyperparameter uncertainty can be substantial.

Ignoring it can produce intervals that are too narrow.

---

## 37. Prediction for observed groups

For an existing group $j$, posterior predictions condition on its group effect.

For a binomial tank:

$$
S_{\mathrm{new},j}^{(s)}
\sim
\operatorname{Binomial}
\left(
D_{\mathrm{new},j},
p_j^{(s)}
\right)
$$

with:

$$
p_j^{(s)}
=
\operatorname{logit}^{-1}
\left(
\alpha_j^{(s)}
\right)
$$

This prediction benefits from both:

- the group's own data;
- partial pooling.

It answers:

> What should happen in another observation from this known group?

---

## 38. Prediction for new groups

For a new group, no group-specific posterior exists.

For each posterior draw:

$$
\alpha_{\mathrm{new}}^{(s)}
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha}^{(s)},
\sigma_\alpha^{(s)}
\right)
$$

Then:

$$
p_{\mathrm{new}}^{(s)}
=
\operatorname{logit}^{-1}
\left(
\alpha_{\mathrm{new}}^{(s)}
\right)
$$

and:

$$
S_{\mathrm{new}}^{(s)}
\sim
\operatorname{Binomial}
\left(
D_{\mathrm{new}},
p_{\mathrm{new}}^{(s)}
\right)
$$

New-group uncertainty is wider because it includes variation among groups.

---

## 39. Average-group prediction is not new-group prediction

A prediction that sets the varying effect to zero:

$$
\alpha_j
=
\bar{\alpha}
$$

describes a hypothetical average group on the hierarchy scale.

It does not describe a randomly selected new group.

A new group requires drawing:

$$
\alpha_{\mathrm{new}}
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

The difference matters whenever $\sigma_\alpha$ is non-negligible.

For nonlinear links:

$$
\operatorname{logit}^{-1}
\left(
\bar{\alpha}
\right)
\neq
\mathbb{E}
\left[
\operatorname{logit}^{-1}
\left(
\alpha_{\mathrm{new}}
\right)
\right]
$$

in general.

---

## 40. Population-average versus cluster-specific effects

In nonlinear multilevel models, a conditional effect inside a group can differ from a population-averaged effect.

Suppose:

$$
\operatorname{logit}(p_{ij})
=
\alpha_j
+
\beta X_{ij}
$$

The cluster-specific probability contrast is:

$$
\Delta_j
=
\operatorname{logit}^{-1}
\left(
\alpha_j+\beta
\right)
-
\operatorname{logit}^{-1}
\left(
\alpha_j
\right)
$$

A population-average contrast is:

$$
\Delta_{\mathrm{pop}}
=
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}
\left[
\operatorname{logit}^{-1}
\left(
\alpha+\beta
\right)
-
\operatorname{logit}^{-1}
\left(
\alpha
\right)
\right]
$$

These are not generally equal.

The target estimand must specify whether it is conditional on observed groups, averaged over them, or generalized to new groups.

---

## 41. Multilevel models and causality

Multilevel structure does not automatically identify causal effects.

A varying intercept can represent unobserved group heterogeneity, but it does not guarantee control of group-level confounding.

Suppose:

$$
U_j
\rightarrow
X_{ij}
$$

and:

$$
U_j
\rightarrow
Y_{ij}
$$

A group intercept may absorb some stable $U_j$ variation.

But causal identification depends on:

- how treatment varies within and between groups;
- whether group effects correlate with treatment;
- whether the hierarchy assumes independence incorrectly;
- whether post-treatment grouping or selection is present.

The multilevel prior:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

often implies prior independence between $\alpha_j$ and observed group predictors unless modeled otherwise.

That assumption can be inappropriate in causal panel or clustered data.

---

## 42. Varying effects are not fixed effects

In panel-data terminology, a unit fixed-effects model estimates one unrestricted unit parameter and uses only within-unit variation for treatment coefficients.

A standard multilevel varying-intercept model treats unit effects as draws from a population distribution.

This can gain precision and support prediction for new units.

But if unit effects are correlated with treatment exposure, a naïve random-intercept model can mix biased between-unit comparisons into the treatment estimate.

The choice is not merely statistical efficiency.

It depends on the causal structure.

Later course material, including Mundlak-style adjustments, addresses this issue more directly.

---

## 43. Exchangeability and causal transport

A new-group prediction assumes the new group is exchangeable with the observed group population after conditioning on modeled predictors.

For a deployment group with covariates $Z_{\mathrm{new}}$, a richer population model may be:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\gamma_0
+
Z_j^{\mathsf T}\gamma,
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

Transport to a new store, hospital, country, or customer segment is credible only if the hierarchy contains the features that explain systematic population differences.

Partial pooling cannot repair an invalid target-population assumption.

---

## 44. CRM application: customers with repeated events

Let:

- $i$ index events;
- $j$ index customers;
- $Y_{ij}$ indicate purchase;
- $A_{ij}$ indicate campaign exposure.

A varying-intercept model is:

$$
Y_{ij}
\sim
\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_{ij})
$$

$$
\operatorname{logit}(p_{ij})
=
\alpha_j
+
\beta_A A_{ij}
$$

with:

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

$\alpha_j$ represents stable customer purchase propensity.

Customers with few events are pooled more strongly.

This improves prediction but does not automatically make $\beta_A$ causal if campaign assignment depends on customer affinity.

---

## 45. CRM application: partially pooled campaign effects

Campaign effects may vary by customer segment, product, store, or campaign.

For campaign $k$:

$$
\beta_k
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\beta},
\sigma_\beta
\right)
$$

and:

$$
\operatorname{logit}(p_i)
=
\alpha
+
\beta_{K_i}A_i
+
X_i^{\mathsf T}\gamma
$$

Sparse campaigns are shrunk toward the campaign population effect.

Well-observed campaigns retain more individual evidence.

This is preferable to:

- one common effect for every campaign;
- unrelated estimates for hundreds of campaigns.

However, a treatment-effect hierarchy must be combined with a valid assignment model or randomized design.

---

## 46. CRM application: stores and customers

A transaction may belong to both:

- one customer;
- one store.

A cross-classified model can include:

$$
\alpha^{\mathrm{customer}}_{C_i}
$$

and:

$$
\alpha^{\mathrm{store}}_{S_i}
$$

so:

$$
\log(\lambda_i)
=
\mu
+
\alpha^{\mathrm{customer}}_{C_i}
+
\alpha^{\mathrm{store}}_{S_i}
+
X_i^{\mathsf T}\beta
$$

This separates:

- persistent customer intensity;
- persistent store intensity;
- observed transaction predictors.

Prediction for a new customer in an existing store differs from prediction for an existing customer in a new store.

---

## 47. CRM application: churn across products

Suppose customers are subscribed to products $p$ and observed over months $t$.

A multilevel churn model may contain:

$$
\alpha^{\mathrm{customer}}_i
$$

$$
\alpha^{\mathrm{product}}_p
$$

$$
\alpha^{\mathrm{month}}_t
$$

The model can share information across sparse products and periods.

But month effects may be temporally correlated, and product effects may depend on product family.

Independent Gaussian varying intercepts are only a starting point.

The hierarchy should reflect known structure rather than treat every label as exchangeable automatically.

## 49. Validating partial pooling

A simulation study should generate:

$$
\alpha_j^{\mathrm{true}}
\sim
p_{\mathrm{true}}(\alpha)
$$

then outcomes:

$$
Y_{ij}
\sim
p
\left(
Y
\mid
\alpha_j^{\mathrm{true}},
X_{ij}
\right)
$$

Compare:

1. complete pooling;
2. no pooling;
3. partial pooling.

Useful metrics include:

### Group-level error

$$
\operatorname{RMSE}_{\mathrm{group}}
=
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{J}
\sum_{j=1}^{J}
\left(
\widehat{\alpha}_j
-
\alpha_j^{\mathrm{true}}
\right)^2
}
$$

### Coverage

$$
\widehat{C}
=
\frac{1}{J}
\sum_{j=1}^{J}
\mathbb{I}
\left[
L_j
\leq
\alpha_j^{\mathrm{true}}
\leq
U_j
\right]
$$

### New-group prediction

Evaluate predictions for groups not used in fitting.

### Robustness

Simulate non-normal group populations, unequal group sizes, and omitted group predictors.

## 50. Common errors exposed by B01

### Error 1: Treating repeated observations as independent

This understates uncertainty and overweights highly observed groups.

### Error 2: Using one common parameter for heterogeneous groups

Complete pooling can underfit and produce misleading group predictions.

### Error 3: Estimating every group independently

No pooling overfits sparse groups and forgets the population.

### Error 4: Calling shrinkage a defect

Shrinkage deliberately trades some group-level bias for lower expected error.

### Error 5: Assuming every group shrinks equally

Pooling depends on group information and inferred population variation.

### Error 6: Interpreting the hierarchy scale on the outcome scale

In a logit model, $\sigma_\alpha$ is measured in log odds.

### Error 7: Ignoring uncertainty in hyperparameters

Plug-in empirical-Bayes estimates can understate uncertainty.

### Error 8: Treating exchangeability as automatic

Groups may require predictors, subpopulations, or correlated structures.

### Error 9: Predicting a new group by setting its effect to zero

A new group requires a draw from the population distribution.

### Error 10: Expecting pooled estimates to reproduce raw group proportions

Partial pooling intentionally smooths sample noise.

### Error 11: Assuming many nominal parameters imply severe overfitting

Strong shrinkage reduces effective complexity.

### Error 12: Ignoring centered-parameterization divergences

Hierarchy geometry can make MCMC unreliable.

### Error 13: Treating varying intercepts as causal adjustment automatically

Group effects may remain correlated with treatment.

### Error 14: Comparing observed-group and new-group predictions as though they were the same estimand

They condition on different uncertainty structures.

## 51. Lecture takeaways

1. Multilevel models estimate group effects and their population distribution jointly.
2. Complete pooling assumes no residual group heterogeneity.
3. No pooling treats groups as unrelated.
4. Partial pooling shares information adaptively.
5. Varying effects are group-specific parameters with learned population priors.
6. Shrinkage is stronger for weakly informed groups.
7. Shrinkage is weaker when the population is genuinely heterogeneous.
8. Adaptive priors learn the regularization strength from all groups.
9. Partial pooling usually improves average group prediction, especially for small groups.
10. It need not improve every individual group estimate.
11. The café example shows information propagating between groups through hyperparameters.
12. The tadpole model uses binomial observations and varying log-odds intercepts.
13. Adding predictors can explain part of the group-level variance.
14. Nominal parameter count differs from effective complexity.
15. Exchangeability is a scientific population assumption.
16. A centered hierarchy can create funnel geometry.
17. Non-centered parameterization often improves MCMC efficiency.
18. Multiple cluster types can be modeled simultaneously.
19. Participant and story effects in the trolley model are cross-classified.
20. Predictions for observed groups differ from predictions for new groups.
21. An average-group prediction is not a new-group prediction.
22. Nonlinear links make population-average and cluster-specific effects differ.
23. Multilevel structure improves modeling but does not itself identify causal effects.
24. The official B01 material maps to Chapter 13 of the uploaded second edition, despite the schedule's Chapter 12 label.

## 54. Official code guide

### `12_intro_multilevel_models.r`

The script contains four principal blocks.

#### Trolley heterogeneity preview

It plots response distributions:

- across stories;
- across participants.

This motivates multiple cluster dimensions.

#### Café golem

It:

- simulates café waiting-time counts;
- defines a non-centered Poisson hierarchy;
- adds café visits sequentially;
- updates café-specific and population distributions;
- illustrates information sharing.

Core hierarchy:

$$
\alpha_j
=
\bar{\alpha}
+
\sigma_\alpha z_j
$$

$$
z_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}(0,1)
$$

#### Reed-frog tanks

It:

- displays raw survival proportions;
- checks priors on population mean and scale;
- fits a multilevel binomial model;
- compares learned and fixed pooling strength;
- plots partially pooled estimates;
- adds predator presence;
- compares residual hierarchy scales;
- animates predictive performance across fixed values of $\sigma_\alpha$.

Core model:

$$
S_j
\sim
\operatorname{Binomial}
\left(
D_j,p_j
\right)
$$

$$
\operatorname{logit}(p_j)
=
\alpha_j
$$

$$
\alpha_j
\sim
\operatorname{Normal}
\left(
\bar{\alpha},
\sigma_\alpha
\right)
$$

It fits an ordered-logistic model with:

- experimental predictors;
- gender-specific coefficients;
- education and age terms;
- varying story intercepts;
- varying participant intercepts.

It then rewrites the two hierarchies in non-centered form.

The script introduces varying intercepts and multiple cluster types.

Correlated varying slopes and more elaborate multilevel causal structures are developed in later B lectures and should not be read back into B01 automatically.
