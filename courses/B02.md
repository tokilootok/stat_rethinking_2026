## 1. Main thesis

The central argument is:

> **A multilevel model can be expanded by assigning adaptive priors to any scientifically meaningful collection of related parameters, not only to intercepts.**

B01 introduced:

$$ \alpha_j \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{\alpha}, \sigma_\alpha \right) $$

B02 generalizes the same logic to:

- several group types;
- category-specific contrasts;
- treatment effects that vary by group;
- interaction cells;
- ordered-response models;
- target-population simulations;
- new-group causal predictions.

The workflow is:

$$ \text{simple hierarchy} \rightarrow \text{identify repeated parameter families} \rightarrow \text{assign one population model per family} \rightarrow \text{preserve group structure} \rightarrow \text{non-center when needed} \rightarrow \text{simulate estimands at the relevant population level} $$

The core design rule is:

$$ \text{parameters that answer the same kind of question} \rightarrow \text{one shared population model} $$

The hierarchy should respect the scientific structure rather than pool every coefficient merely because it is numerically available.

## 3. What it means to expand a hierarchy

A basic model contains one varying parameter:

$$ \alpha_j $$

An expanded multilevel model may contain several parameter families:

$$ \alpha_j $$

$$ \beta_j $$

$$ \gamma_k $$

$$ \delta_{jk} $$

Each family requires a population model.

For example:

$$ \alpha_j \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{\alpha}, \sigma_\alpha \right) $$

$$ \beta_j \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{\beta}, \sigma_\beta \right) $$

$$ \gamma_k \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( 0, \sigma_\gamma \right) $$

The hyperparameters answer population questions:

- What is the average baseline?
- What is the average treatment contrast?
- How heterogeneous are baselines?
- How heterogeneous are treatment effects?
- How much variation remains across a second cluster type?

The expansion is scientific, not merely syntactic.

---

## 4. Parameter families should not be pooled indiscriminately

Suppose admissions are observed for two gender categories across six departments.

There are twelve cell logits:

$$ \alpha_{g,d} $$

One possible hierarchy is:

$$ \alpha_{g,d} \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{\alpha}, \sigma \right) $$

This pools all twelve cells toward one mean.

But the twelve values contain at least two kinds of structure:

1. department baseline selectivity;
2. within-department gender contrast.

A more structured model is:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{g,d}) = \alpha_d + \beta_d G_g $$

with separate hierarchies:

$$ \alpha_d \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{\alpha}, \sigma_\alpha \right) $$

$$ \beta_d \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{\beta}, \sigma_\beta \right) $$

Pooling now respects the meanings of the parameters.

## 5. The three `UCBadmit` models

The official script begins with three models.

Let:

- $A_{g,d}$ be admitted applicants;
- $N_{g,d}$ be total applications;
- $G$ index gender category;
- $D$ index department.

The likelihood is:

$$ A_{g,d} \sim \operatorname{Binomial} \left( N_{g,d}, p_{g,d} \right) $$

### Model 0: unrelated cell parameters

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{g,d}) = a_{g,d} $$

$$ a_{g,d} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,1.5) $$

This is a no-pooling model across the twelve cells.

### Model 1: pool every cell toward one population

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{g,d}) = a_{g,d} $$

$$ a_{g,d} \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{a}, \sigma \right) $$

$$ \bar{a} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,1) $$

$$ \sigma \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(1) $$

This pools all gender-department cells without distinguishing their roles.

### Model 2: pool while preserving department structure

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{g,d}) = a_d+b_dG_{1g} $$

where $G_{1g}$ indicates one gender category.

The adaptive priors are:

$$ a_d \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{a}, \sigma \right) $$

$$ b_d \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( 0, \tau \right) $$

with:

$$ \bar{a} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,1) $$

$$ \sigma \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(1) $$

$$ \tau \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(1) $$

---

## 6. Why Model 1 can pool the wrong things

Model 1 treats:

$$ a_{1,A} $$

and:

$$ a_{2,F} $$

as members of one exchangeable population.

But department A and department F differ systematically in selectivity.

Likewise, the two gender cells within one department are related through a contrast.

Pooling every cell toward one mean ignores this structure.

The model may still regularize predictions effectively.

But the hierarchy is scientifically coarse.

A better hierarchy distinguishes:

$$ \text{department baseline} $$

from:

$$ \text{within-department category contrast} $$

The lesson is:

> Exchangeability should be declared for parameters with comparable meanings.

---

## 7. Department-varying contrasts

In Model 2, $a_d$ is a department baseline and $b_d$ is the category contrast in department $d$.

For the reference category:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{0,d}) = a_d $$

For the indicated category:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{1,d}) = a_d+b_d $$

The within-department log-odds contrast is:

$$ b_d $$

The probability contrast is:

$$ \Delta_d = \operatorname{logit}^{-1} \left( a_d+b_d \right) - \operatorname{logit}^{-1} \left( a_d \right) $$

Because of the logistic link, identical $b_d$ values can imply different probability differences in departments with different baselines.

The final estimand should therefore be computed on the desired scale.

---

## 8. Adaptive pooling of contrasts

The prior:

$$ b_d \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,\tau) $$

shares information across department-specific contrasts.

If the posterior for $\tau$ is small:

$$ b_d \approx 0 $$

and the model infers little heterogeneity in the contrast.

If $\tau$ is larger, department contrasts remain more distinct.

Departments with little information are pooled more strongly.

Departments with many applications contribute more directly to their own $b_d$ estimates.

This is the same adaptive regularization introduced for intercepts, now applied to slopes or contrasts.

---

## 9. Zero-centered varying slopes

The official Model 2 uses:

$$ b_d \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,\tau) $$

rather than:

$$ b_d \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{b}, \tau \right) $$

This asserts that the population-average contrast is centered at zero a priori and is not estimated through a separate $\bar{b}$ parameter.

That choice may be deliberate regularization.

It differs from a model that estimates an overall gender contrast.

A more general formulation is:

$$ b_d \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{b}, \tau \right) $$

with:

$$ \bar{b} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,s_b) $$

The official script supports the zero-centered form. The broader form is a model extension, not a claim about the exact code.

---

## 10. Uncorrelated varying intercepts and slopes

Model 2 gives separate independent population models:

$$ a_d \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{a}, \sigma \right) $$

$$ b_d \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,\tau) $$

Conditional on the hyperparameters:

$$ a_d \perp\!\!\!\perp b_d $$

This says department selectivity and department-specific gender contrast are unrelated in the population model.

That may be a useful first approximation.

It may also be scientifically unrealistic.

For example, highly selective departments might systematically have larger or smaller contrasts.

Modeling that covariance requires a multivariate population distribution, developed in B03 and Chapter 14.

---

## 11. Varying slope is a broad concept

A varying slope need not multiply a continuous predictor.

In the admissions model:

$$ b_dG_{1g} $$

is a department-varying contrast between two categories.

The same structure applies to:

- treatment versus control;
- campaign versus no campaign;
- male versus female indicator;
- before versus after;
- exposed versus unexposed.

A varying slope means:

$$ \text{the effect of a predictor varies across groups} $$

The predictor may be binary, categorical, continuous, or transformed.

## 12. Chimpanzee data structure

The official script next uses the chimpanzee experiment.

Let:

- $P_i$ indicate whether the left option was selected;
- $A_i$ index actor;
- $B_i$ index experimental block;
- $T_i$ index one of four treatments.

The treatment index is constructed as:

$$ T_i = 1 + \text{prosoc\_left}_i + 2\times \text{condition}_i $$

This produces four combinations conventionally labeled in the script:

- `R/N`;
- `L/N`;
- `R/P`;
- `L/P`.

The observations are cross-classified by actor and block.

Actors appear in multiple blocks.

Blocks contain observations from multiple actors.

---

## 13. Chimpanzee likelihood

The response model is:

$$ P_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_i) $$

The official linear predictor is:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_i) = b_{T_i,B_i} + a_{A_i} $$

This contains:

- an actor-specific baseline $a_a$;
- a treatment-by-block effect $b_{t,b}$.

The model is richer than a simple actor-plus-block intercept model.

It allows the treatment pattern itself to vary across blocks.

---

## 14. Actor hierarchy

Actor effects follow:

$$ a_a \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{a}, \sigma_A \right) $$

with:

$$ \bar{a} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,1.5) $$

$$ \sigma_A \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(1) $$

$\bar{a}$ is the population-average actor log-odds baseline.

$\sigma_A$ measures residual actor heterogeneity.

Actor-specific baseline probabilities are:

$$ p_a = \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(a_a) $$

The official plotting code displays posterior intervals and means for these probabilities.

---

## 15. Block-treatment hierarchy

The matrix:

$$ b_{t,b} $$

contains four treatment effects for each of six blocks.

The official prior is:

$$ b_{t,b} \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( 0, \sigma_B \right) $$

with:

$$ \sigma_B \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(1) $$

This pools all treatment-by-block deviations toward zero.

The model asks:

- How much do block-specific treatment patterns vary?
- Which cells contain enough information to depart from zero?
- Is block-to-block heterogeneity substantial?

The hierarchy is over interaction cells, not merely block intercepts.

---

## 16. Why there is only one global intercept

The linear predictor adds:

$$ a_{A_i} + b_{T_i,B_i} $$

If both hierarchies had unrestricted nonzero means:

$$ a_a \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{a}, \sigma_A \right) $$

and:

$$ b_{t,b} \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{b}, \sigma_B \right) $$

then only the sum:

$$ \bar{a}+\bar{b} $$

would be strongly identified.

The model could shift one mean upward and the other downward without changing predictions.

The script avoids this by:

- estimating $\bar{a}$;
- centering the block-treatment population at zero.

This is the multilevel analogue of redundant intercepts in ordinary regression.

---

## 17. Cross-classification

A model is cross-classified when observations belong to multiple cluster systems that are not nested.

For the chimpanzees:

$$ \text{observation} \rightarrow \text{actor} $$

and:

$$ \text{observation} \rightarrow \text{block} $$

Actors are not unique to one block.

Blocks are not unique to one actor.

The likelihood can nevertheless include both cluster effects additively.

MCMC does not require the cluster systems to form a strict tree.

---

## 18. Cross-classification versus nesting

A nested design might be:

$$ \text{student} \subset \text{classroom} \subset \text{school} $$

A cross-classified design might be:

$$ \text{student response} \rightarrow \text{student} $$

and:

$$ \text{student response} \rightarrow \text{teacher} $$

when students encounter several teachers.

The model syntax can be similar:

$$ \eta_i = \alpha^{\mathrm{student}}_{S_i} + \alpha^{\mathrm{teacher}}_{T_i} + X_i^{\mathsf T}\beta $$

The key issue is the membership mapping, not the informal label “hierarchical.”

---

## 19. Multiple hierarchy scales

The chimpanzee model estimates:

$$ \sigma_A $$

for actor heterogeneity and:

$$ \sigma_B $$

for treatment-by-block heterogeneity.

These scales are not directly comparable as simple observed variances because they describe different parameter families.

Still, they indicate where residual variability resides on the log-odds scale.

A small $\sigma_B$ suggests treatment patterns are similar across blocks.

A large $\sigma_A$ suggests persistent actor differences.

Posterior predictive checks should determine whether those hierarchies reproduce relevant response patterns.

---

## 20. Centered chimpanzee model

The centered form samples:

$$ a_a \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{a}, \sigma_A \right) $$

and:

$$ b_{t,b} \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( 0, \sigma_B \right) $$

directly.

This representation is intuitive because the sampled parameters are the scientific group effects.

But when hierarchy scales approach zero, the posterior can form narrow funnels.

The model may suffer:

- low effective sample size;
- divergences;
- slow exploration.

---

## 21. Non-centered chimpanzee model

The official non-centered model samples standardized effects:

$$ z_{a,a} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,1) $$

$$ z_{b,t,b} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,1) $$

and constructs:

$$ a_a = \bar{a} + \sigma_A z_{a,a} $$

$$ b_{t,b} = \sigma_B z_{b,t,b} $$

The scientific model is unchanged.

The posterior geometry can improve because the standardized parameters remain on stable scales.

The script reconstructs $a$ and $b$ in generated quantities.

---

## 22. Effective-sample-size comparison

The official script compares centered and non-centered effective sample sizes for:

- $\bar{a}$;
- $\sigma_A$;
- $\sigma_B$;
- actor effects;
- block-treatment effects.

The comparison is empirical.

Non-centering is not guaranteed to improve every parameter.

The plot colors points according to which parameterization has larger effective sample size.

The lesson is:

> Parameterization should be evaluated with diagnostics, not chosen by slogan alone.

Relevant diagnostics include:

- divergences;
- $\widehat{R}$;
- bulk ESS;
- tail ESS;
- rank plots;
- computation time.

---

## 23. Strong data can favor centered models

Non-centering often helps when group effects are weakly informed.

When each group contains abundant information, the group effects are tightly identified by their own likelihoods.

In such settings, centered parameterization may mix as well or better.

A practical strategy is:

1. fit a scientifically correct hierarchy;
2. inspect diagnostics;
3. compare centered and non-centered forms if needed;
4. preserve the form with better geometry and stable inference.

The parameterization is computational, not scientific.

---

## 24. Trolley model with two cluster types

The official script next models ordered trolley judgments.

Let:

- $R_i$ be ordered response;
- $A_i$ be action;
- $I_i$ be intention;
- $C_i$ be contact;
- $S_i$ identify story;
- $U_i$ identify participant.

The model is:

$$ R_i \sim \operatorname{OrderedLogistic} \left( \phi_i, \kappa \right) $$

with:

$$ \phi_i = \beta_A A_i + \beta_I I_i + \beta_C C_i + a^{S}_{S_i} + a^{U}_{U_i} $$

The varying intercepts are:

$$ a^S_s \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma_S) $$

$$ a^U_u \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma_U) $$

---

## 25. Why stories and participants both matter

Stories differ in baseline moral acceptability beyond the coded experimental manipulations.

Participants differ in general willingness to endorse the actions.

If story effects are omitted:

- unusual stories can distort treatment coefficients;
- repeated responses to one story are treated as more independent than they are.

If participant effects are omitted:

- participants with many responses are over-weighted;
- stable individual response tendencies inflate apparent information.

The two hierarchy types separate these persistent sources of variation.

---

## 26. Partial non-centering

The script includes a model in which the story effects are non-centered:

$$ a^S_s = \sigma_S z^S_s $$

$$ z^S_s \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,1) $$

while participant effects remain centered:

$$ a^U_u \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma_U) $$

This demonstrates that parameterization can differ by hierarchy.

One cluster type may be weakly informed and benefit from non-centering.

Another may be well informed and work adequately in centered form.

There is no requirement that every hierarchy use the same computational parameterization.

---

## 27. The full trolley expansion

The script then fits a much richer ordered-logistic model containing:

- gender-specific action coefficients;
- gender-specific intention coefficients;
- gender-specific contact coefficients;
- gender-specific age coefficients;
- gender-specific education effects;
- story varying intercepts;
- participant varying intercepts;
- ordered category thresholds.

The latent predictor has the schematic form:

$$ \phi_i = \text{gender-specific education term} + \beta_{A,G_i}A_i + \beta_{I,G_i}I_i + \beta_{C,G_i}C_i + \beta_{Y,G_i}Y_i + a^S_{S_i} + a^U_{U_i} $$

This is a model expansion in several dimensions at once.

---

## 28. Ordered education effects

The script reorders education levels and represents monotonic increments using simplex parameters.

For one gender group:

$$ \delta^{F} \sim \operatorname{Dirichlet}(2,\ldots,2) $$

For another:

$$ \delta^{M} \sim \operatorname{Dirichlet}(2,\ldots,2) $$

Cumulative education effects are formed by summing increments up to level $E_i$.

This guarantees a monotonic ordered predictor effect while allowing unequal spacing between levels.

The hierarchy coexists with a specialized ordered-predictor model.

Multilevel expansion does not require every component to use the same distributional form.

---

## 29. Model expansion should remain modular

The full trolley model can be decomposed into modules:

### Observation module

$$ R_i \sim \operatorname{OrderedLogistic} \left( \phi_i, \kappa \right) $$

### Experimental-effect module

$$ \beta_{A,G}, \beta_{I,G}, \beta_{C,G} $$

### Demographic module

$$ \beta_{Y,G} $$

and ordered education effects.

### Story hierarchy

$$ a^S_s \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma_S) $$

### Participant hierarchy

$$ a^U_u \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma_U) $$

This modular view helps with:

- prior predictive checking;
- simulation validation;
- debugging;
- sensitivity analysis;
- CausalOps artifact design.

---

## 30. Expansion does not imply adding every available term

A model can be expanded structurally without becoming an indiscriminate maximal model.

Each added component should answer a question:

- Why should this coefficient vary?
- Which groups share a population?
- Which interaction is scientifically meaningful?
- What prediction requires this hierarchy?
- Which omitted dependence would otherwise distort uncertainty?

A multilevel model is not automatically improved by adding every possible varying slope and interaction.

Unnecessary complexity can create:

- weak identification;
- difficult geometry;
- prior sensitivity;
- opaque estimands.

---

## 31. Reed-frog prediction model

The official script returns to reed-frog survival.

Let:

- $S_j$ be survivors;
- $D_j$ be initial density;
- $T_j$ identify tank;
- $P_j$ indicate predation category;
- $G_j$ indicate size category.

The model is:

$$ S_j \sim \operatorname{Binomial} \left( D_j,p_j \right) $$

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_j) = a_{T_j} + b_{P_j,G_j} $$

with:

$$ a_t \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma) $$

$$ b_{p,g} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,1) $$

$$ \sigma \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(1) $$

The tank intercepts represent residual group variation.

The $b_{p,g}$ matrix represents predation-by-size combinations.

---

## 32. Population intervention simulation

The script uses posterior simulation to ask how survival changes under a different target-population composition.

The common setup is:

- tank density fixed at 35;
- predation probability fixed at 50%;
- status quo with 25% large tanks;
- intervention with 75% large tanks.

For each simulated new tank:

1. draw a new tank effect:

$$ a_{\mathrm{new}}^{(s)} \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( 0, \sigma^{(s)} \right) $$

2. sample predation status;
3. sample size status from the target distribution;
4. compute survival probability;
5. simulate survivors.

This is new-cluster prediction combined with post-stratification or population intervention.

---

## 33. A source inconsistency in the prediction comments

The script's main comment states:

> status quo 25% large; intervention 75% large.

The code implements:

$$ \Pr(G=\mathrm{large})=0.25 $$

under the first simulation and:

$$ \Pr(G=\mathrm{large})=0.75 $$

under the second.

An intermediate comment says “intervention - 50% large,” but the code still uses 75%.

The implemented comparison is therefore 25% versus 75% large.

This guide follows the executable probabilities and records the comment inconsistency rather than silently replacing one with the other.

---

## 34. Why a new tank effect must be drawn

The intervention concerns a target population of new tanks.

Setting:

$$ a_{\mathrm{new}}=0 $$

would simulate only an average tank on the log-odds scale.

The script instead draws:

$$ a_{\mathrm{new}} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma) $$

This includes tank-to-tank heterogeneity in the intervention outcome.

The resulting distribution of survivors contains:

1. posterior parameter uncertainty;
2. new-tank heterogeneity;
3. predation and size-composition uncertainty;
4. binomial survival variation.

This is a full population predictive estimand.

---

## 35. Expected intervention outcome versus replicated outcome

An expected-survival estimand could average:

$$ D \times p_j $$

without drawing binomial outcomes.

The official code draws:

$$ S_j \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(35,p_j) $$

Therefore, its distribution represents realized survival counts in simulated populations, not only expected survival.

The estimand should distinguish:

$$ \mathbb{E}[S \mid \text{policy}] $$

from:

$$ p(S_{\mathrm{future}} \mid \text{policy},D) $$

Both can be useful.

The predictive distribution is wider.

---

## 36. Composition intervention versus unit-level treatment

Changing the proportion of large tanks from 25% to 75% is a population-composition intervention.

It is not the same as changing one existing tank from small to large.

The target is:

$$ \mathbb{E}_{G\sim p_1} \left[ S(G) \right] - \mathbb{E}_{G\sim p_0} \left[ S(G) \right] $$

where:

$$ p_0(G) $$

and:

$$ p_1(G) $$

are two target distributions.

This is analogous to:

- changing customer-segment composition;
- changing product allocation;
- changing store mix;
- changing campaign-policy frequencies.

The intervention acts on a distribution, not merely a coefficient.

---

## 37. Post-stratification in a multilevel model

Suppose a target population contains cells $c$ with weights:

$$ w_c $$

A multilevel model estimates:

$$ \mu_c^{(s)} $$

for each cell, including sparse cells through partial pooling.

The standardized target mean is:

$$ \mu_{\mathrm{target}}^{(s)} = \sum_c w_c \mu_c^{(s)} $$

Multilevel regression and post-stratification are complementary:

- the hierarchy stabilizes cell estimates;
- post-stratification supplies the target population distribution.

The target weights are part of the estimand.

---

## 38. Devil's funnel

The official script includes the hierarchy:

$$ v \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,3) $$

$$ x \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( 0, \exp(v) \right) $$

When $v$ is very negative:

$$ \exp(v) \approx 0 $$

and $x$ must lie in an extremely narrow region near zero.

When $v$ is positive, $x$ can vary widely.

The joint density has a funnel shape.

This is an abstract representation of the geometry that arises when a hierarchy scale approaches zero.

---

## 39. Non-centered funnel

The non-centered version is:

$$ v \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,3) $$

$$ z \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,1) $$

$$ x = z\exp(v) $$

The sampled variables:

$$ (v,z) $$

have much simpler geometry.

The scientific quantity $x$ is reconstructed afterward.

This demonstration makes clear that non-centering is a coordinate transformation for computation.

It does not change the implied distribution of $x$.

---

## 40. Why expanded models create geometry problems

As a model gains:

- more hierarchy scales;
- more sparse group effects;
- interactions;
- ordered predictors;
- latent parameters,

the posterior gains more opportunities for:

- funnels;
- ridges;
- near-redundant intercepts;
- strong parameter correlations;
- weakly identified variance components.

MCMC diagnostics become more important as the model becomes more scientifically expressive.

A rich formula with poor geometry is not a successful analysis.

---

## 41. Prior predictive checks for expanded hierarchies

Each parameter family should be checked through the predictions it generates.

For the admissions model, inspect prior distributions of:

- department baseline probabilities;
- department-specific category contrasts;
- between-department variation;
- extreme cell probabilities.

For chimpanzees, inspect:

- actor baseline probabilities;
- treatment patterns across blocks;
- block-to-block treatment heterogeneity.

For trolley data, inspect:

- response-category probabilities;
- story and participant variation;
- monotonic education trajectories;
- gender-specific predictor effects.

The number of coefficients makes coefficient-by-coefficient prior inspection inadequate.

---

## 42. Hierarchy-scale priors should be family-specific

A common scale prior for every hierarchy may be inappropriate.

Actor intercepts, block-treatment deviations, story effects, and participant effects live on different scientific scales.

Possible priors include:

$$ \sigma_A \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(1) $$

$$ \sigma_B \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(2) $$

or half-normal alternatives.

The correct scale should be evaluated through prior predictive distributions.

A hierarchy scale near one log-odds unit can imply large probability variation near the middle of the logistic curve.

---

## 43. Varying effects and interactions

A conventional interaction model might be:

$$ \eta_i = \alpha + \beta_T T_i + \beta_B B_i + \beta_{TB}T_iB_i $$

A multilevel interaction model assigns adaptive priors to many interaction cells:

$$ \delta_{t,b} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma_\delta) $$

This is especially useful when:

- there are many blocks;
- each block-treatment cell is sparse;
- treatment heterogeneity is expected but uncertain.

The hierarchy regularizes the interaction surface.

---

## 44. Separate hierarchies versus one giant hierarchy

Suppose a model contains actor intercepts and block-treatment effects.

One could pool every parameter under one distribution.

That would imply exchangeability between:

- an actor's baseline;
- a treatment deviation in a block.

These parameters answer different questions and have different meanings.

The official model uses:

$$ a_a \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{a}, \sigma_A \right) $$

and:

$$ b_{t,b} \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( 0, \sigma_B \right) $$

This is the correct modular principle:

> One adaptive prior for each coherent family of effects.

---

## 45. Model comparison is not enough

Predictive comparison can help decide whether a hierarchy improves generalization.

But it cannot determine whether the hierarchy represents the correct causal structure.

For example, a block-treatment interaction may improve prediction because blocks capture hidden temporal or procedural changes.

That does not prove that block modifies the causal treatment effect.

Likewise, a participant intercept can improve prediction without solving treatment assignment bias.

The model should be assessed along separate dimensions:

- predictive adequacy;
- computational adequacy;
- causal identification;
- transportability.

---

## 46. Causal interpretation of varying treatment effects

Suppose treatment $A$ varies across groups $j$:

$$ Y_{ij} \sim p \left( Y_{ij} \mid \alpha_j, \beta_j, A_{ij}, X_{ij} \right) $$

with:

$$ \beta_j \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{\beta}, \sigma_\beta \right) $$

$\beta_j$ is causally interpretable only if treatment is identified within group $j$ or through a valid pooled design.

A hierarchy can shrink confounded group estimates toward a confounded population mean.

Partial pooling reduces variance.

It does not remove confounding.

---

## 47. Treatment heterogeneity estimands

Several targets are possible.

### Mean group-specific effect

$$ \bar{\tau} = \mathbb{E}_j \left[ \tau_j \right] $$

### Variance of effects

$$ \operatorname{Var}_j \left( \tau_j \right) $$

### Effect in an observed group

$$ \tau_j $$

### Effect in a new group

$$ \tau_{\mathrm{new}} \sim p \left( \tau \mid \bar{\tau}, \sigma_\tau \right) $$

### Target-population effect

$$ \tau_{\mathrm{target}} = \sum_j w_j\tau_j $$

The model formula does not determine which estimand should be reported.

---

## 48. CRM application: department-varying campaign effects

Let:

- $d$ index business department or channel;
- $A$ be campaign exposure;
- $Y$ be conversion.

A model is:

$$ Y_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_i) $$

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_i) = \alpha_{D_i} + \beta_{D_i}A_i $$

with:

$$ \alpha_d \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{\alpha}, \sigma_\alpha \right) $$

$$ \beta_d \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{\beta}, \sigma_\beta \right) $$

Sparse departments receive stronger pooling.

The final business estimand might be:

$$ \tau_{\mathrm{target}} = \sum_d w_d \left[ p_d(1)-p_d(0) \right] $$

with deployment weights $w_d$.

---

## 49. CRM application: customer and campaign cross-classification

Each response belongs to:

- one customer;
- one campaign.

A cross-classified logistic model is:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_i) = \alpha^{C}_{C_i} + \alpha^{K}_{K_i} + X_i^{\mathsf T}\beta $$

with separate hierarchies:

$$ \alpha^{C}_c \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{\alpha}_C, \sigma_C \right) $$

$$ \alpha^{K}_k \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{\alpha}_K, \sigma_K \right) $$

To avoid redundant global means, one family may be centered at zero or the model can use a single global intercept plus zero-centered deviations.

The hierarchy distinguishes persistent customer affinity from campaign popularity.

---

## 50. CRM application: campaign effects varying by channel

Let:

$$ \beta_{a,k} $$

be the effect of offer $a$ in channel $k$.

A multilevel interaction is:

$$ \beta_{a,k} \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{\beta}_a, \sigma_a \right) $$

This pools channel-specific versions of the same offer.

It can support:

- email;
- SMS;
- push;
- web;
- call-center channels.

The hierarchy should not pool fundamentally different treatments under one population unless exchangeability is defensible.

---

## 51. CRM application: changing portfolio composition

Suppose expected margin depends on customer segment and offer.

A policy changes the segment composition receiving an offer.

For posterior draw $s$:

$$ V^{(s)}(\pi) = \sum_c w_c^{\pi} \mu_c^{(s)} $$

where:

$$ w_c^{\pi} $$

are the policy-induced cell weights.

A policy contrast is:

$$ \Delta_V^{(s)} = V^{(s)}(\pi_1) - V^{(s)}(\pi_0) $$

New-customer or new-store heterogeneity should be simulated when the policy targets clusters not present in training.

This is the CRM analogue of the reed-frog population-composition simulation.

## 53. Validation strategy

A simulation should generate:

1. department baselines;
2. department-specific treatment contrasts;
3. actor effects;
4. block-treatment deviations;
5. observations under known links.

Compare candidate models:

- one global effect;
- unrelated group effects;
- one coarse hierarchy over all cells;
- separate intercept and slope hierarchies;
- correlated hierarchy when appropriate.

Metrics should include:

### Group effect recovery

$$ \operatorname{RMSE}_\beta = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{J} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \left( \widehat{\beta}_j- \beta_j^{\mathrm{true}} \right)^2 } $$

### Population-scale recovery

$$ \left| \widehat{\sigma}_\beta - \sigma_\beta^{\mathrm{true}} \right| $$

### Coverage

$$ \frac{1}{J} \sum_j \mathbb{I} \left[ \beta_j^{\mathrm{true}} \in I_j \right] $$

### New-group prediction

Evaluate groups omitted from fitting.

### Target-population estimand

Compare the estimated policy contrast with the known simulated contrast.

## 54. Common errors exposed by B02

### Error 1: Pooling parameters with different meanings

Actor baselines and block-treatment deviations should not share one hierarchy.

### Error 2: Treating all category cells as one exchangeable population

Structured baselines and contrasts may need separate adaptive priors.

### Error 3: Calling every group-varying contrast a continuous slope

Binary and categorical contrasts can vary by group too.

### Error 4: Assuming zero-centered slopes estimate an overall mean effect

They shrink toward zero unless a separate population mean is modeled.

### Error 5: Giving every additive hierarchy its own free global mean

The means can become redundant and weakly identified.

### Error 6: Confusing cross-classification with nesting

The membership structure must be represented correctly.

### Error 7: Believing non-centering is always superior

Its benefit depends on group information and posterior geometry.

### Error 8: Comparing centered and non-centered fits only by posterior means

Use ESS, divergences, rank plots, and computation time.

### Error 9: Predicting new clusters by reusing observed cluster effects

A new cluster requires a draw from the population hierarchy.

### Error 10: Setting a new-cluster effect to zero

This gives an average latent cluster, not a random new cluster.

### Error 11: Ignoring target-population composition

Population interventions require explicit cell weights.

### Error 12: Reading a script comment without checking the implemented probabilities

The reed-frog code implements 25% versus 75% large despite one inconsistent comment.

### Error 13: Treating a hierarchical treatment effect as causal automatically

Partial pooling cannot remove assignment bias.

### Error 14: Expanding a model without modular prior predictive checks

Rich models can imply implausible response distributions.

### Error 15: Treating interaction heterogeneity as evidence of mechanism

A varying interaction can be predictive without being causal.

## 55. Lecture takeaways

1. Multilevel expansion applies adaptive priors to coherent families of related parameters.
2. Parameter families should be defined by scientific meaning.
3. Pooling every category cell under one population can ignore important structure.
4. Department baselines and department-specific contrasts can have separate hierarchies.
5. A varying slope can be a binary or categorical contrast.
6. The official admissions slope hierarchy is independent of the intercept hierarchy.
7. Correlated intercepts and slopes are the next conceptual step, not the main B02 structure.
8. Cross-classified observations belong to several non-nested cluster systems.
9. The chimpanzee model combines actor effects with treatment-by-block effects.
10. One global intercept is sufficient when additive cluster deviations are centered at zero.
11. Centered and non-centered parameterizations represent the same scientific model.
12. Non-centering can improve ESS and reduce divergences for weakly informed effects.
13. Different hierarchies in one model can use different parameterizations.
14. The trolley model combines an ordered likelihood, several predictor modules, and two cluster types.
15. Ordered predictors can coexist with multilevel effects.
16. Expanded models should remain modular and scientifically motivated.
17. Multilevel predictions must specify observed versus new clusters.
18. New-cluster simulation requires drawing a new varying effect.
19. Population-composition interventions require explicit target distributions.
20. The reed-frog code implements a 25% to 75% large-tank shift.
21. Predictive outcomes include more uncertainty than expected outcomes.
22. Funnel geometry explains why hierarchy scale parameters can be computationally difficult.
23. Partial pooling reduces variance but does not establish causal identification.
24. CausalOps should store one hierarchy artifact per effect family and one explicit target-population artifact per policy estimand.

## 58. Official code guide

### `13_GLMM2.r`

The script contains the following B02-relevant blocks.

#### `UCBadmit` hierarchy comparison

It fits:

1. unrelated gender-department cell logits;
2. one hierarchy over all cell logits;
3. department baselines plus department-varying gender contrasts.

Core structured model:

$$ A \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(N,p) $$

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p) = a_D+b_DG_1 $$

$$ a_D \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar{a}, \sigma \right) $$

$$ b_D \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,\tau) $$

#### Chimpanzee block interactions

It fits:

$$ P_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_i) $$

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_i) = b_{T_i,B_i}+a_{A_i} $$

with separate actor and block-treatment hierarchies.

It then fits a non-centered equivalent and compares effective sample sizes.

#### Trolley story and participant effects

It fits an ordered-logistic model with:

- action;
- intention;
- contact;
- story varying intercepts;
- participant varying intercepts.

It then partially non-centers the hierarchy.

#### Full trolley model

It adds:

- gender-specific coefficients;
- age;
- monotonic education effects;
- story and participant effects;
- a fully non-centered hierarchy version.

#### Reed-frog population simulation

It fits:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_j) = a_{T_j}+b_{P_j,G_j} $$

and simulates new tanks under two size distributions.

The code implements:

$$ 25\% \rightarrow 75\% $$

large tanks.

#### Devil's funnel

It compares:

$$ x \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( 0, \exp(v) \right) $$

with:

$$ z \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,1) $$

$$ x=z\exp(v) $$

to demonstrate why non-centering can improve posterior geometry.

### Scope limitation

The file uses an uncorrelated department intercept-and-slope hierarchy and a block-treatment matrix hierarchy.

It does not make correlated varying intercepts and slopes the central model. That material is developed in B03.
