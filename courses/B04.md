## 1. Main thesis

The lecture joins two problems that initially look different:

1. observations from the same group share causes;
2. observations from the same dyad share a relationship.

Both problems require the model to represent dependence at the level where it arises.

The central causal warning is:

> **Partial pooling is not automatically deconfounding. If group effects are associated with an exposure, an ordinary varying-intercept model can mix within-group and between-group information.**

The central network insight is:

> **A directed interaction is generated jointly by a sender, a receiver, and their dyad. Correlated varying effects let the model learn each source of dependence.**

The workflow is:

$$ \text{causal structure} \rightarrow \text{level of variation} \rightarrow \text{within-between decomposition} \rightarrow \text{correlated group or dyad effects} \rightarrow \text{posterior intervention or network summary} $$

## 3. Why ordinary regression fails with grouped data

Let individual $i$ belong to group $j$.

Suppose:

- $X_{ij}$ is an individual exposure;
- $Y_{ij}$ is an outcome;
- $U_j$ is an unobserved group cause.

A simple causal structure is:

$$ U_j \rightarrow X_{ij} $$

$$ U_j \rightarrow Y_{ij} $$

and possibly:

$$ X_{ij} \rightarrow Y_{ij} $$

The backdoor path is:

$$ X_{ij} \leftarrow U_j \rightarrow Y_{ij} $$

The naive model:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{ij}) = \alpha+\beta_X X_{ij} $$

cannot distinguish:

- differences among people inside the same group;
- differences between groups with different average exposure.

It therefore combines two associations that need not have the same causal meaning.

## 4. The official simulation

The script generates group membership:

```r
g <- sample(1:N_groups, size=N_id, replace=TRUE)
```

and an unobserved group feature:

```r
Ug <- rnorm(N_groups, 1.5)
```

The individual exposure depends on that group feature:

```r
X <- rnorm(N_id, Ug[g])
```

The binary outcome also depends on it:

```r
Y <- rbern(
  N_id,
  p = inv_logit(a0 + bXY*X + Ug[g] + bZY*Z[g])
)
```

In mathematical form:

$$ X_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(U_j,1) $$

$$ Y_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_{ij}) $$

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{ij}) = \alpha_0 + \beta_{XY}X_{ij} + U_j + \beta_{ZY}Z_j $$

The lecture sets the true individual effect to zero in the main demonstration:

$$ \beta_{XY}=0 $$

Yet $X$ and $Y$ remain associated because both inherit variation from $U_j$.

This is a calibration exercise: because the data-generating truth is known, competing estimators can be judged against the correct intervention.

---

## 5. Three levels of variables

The simulation contains variables at different levels.

| Variable | Level | Varies within group? | Varies between groups? |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| $Y_{ij}$ | Individual | Yes | Yes |
| $X_{ij}$ | Individual | Yes | Yes |
| $Z_j$ | Group | No | Yes |
| $U_j$ | Group, unobserved | No | Yes |
| $g_i$ | Membership index | No | Identifies group |

This classification is not bookkeeping trivia. It determines what the data can identify.

A group-level predictor such as $Z_j$ cannot be distinguished from a complete set of unrestricted group intercepts using only cross-sectional variation.

An individual predictor such as $X_{ij}$ can be compared within a group, provided there is sufficient within-group variation.

---

## 6. The within-between decomposition

For each group, define the exposure mean:

$$ \bar X_j = \frac{1}{n_j} \sum_{i:g_i=j}X_{ij} $$

Then decompose every exposure value:

$$ X_{ij} = \bar X_j + \left(X_{ij}-\bar X_j\right) $$

The two components answer different questions.

### Within-group component

$$ X_{ij}-\bar X_j $$

compares people to others in the same group.

### Between-group component

$$ \bar X_j $$

compares groups with different average exposure.

If $U_j$ causes both $\bar X_j$ and $Y_{ij}$, the between-group association is confounded.

The within-group contrast can still be useful because $U_j$ is constant inside group $j$.

---

## 7. The naive model

The official naive model is:

$$ Y_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_i) $$

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_i) = \alpha + \beta_X X_i + \beta_Z Z_{g_i} $$

```r
m0 <- ulam(
  alist(
    Y ~ bernoulli(p),
    logit(p) <- a + bxy*X + bzy*Z_g,
    a ~ dnorm(0, 10),
    c(bxy, bzy) ~ dnorm(0, 1)
  ), data=dat, chains=4, cores=4
)
```

The coefficient $\beta_X$ is informed by both:

$$ \operatorname{Cov} \left( X_{ij}-\bar X_j, Y_{ij} \right) $$

and:

$$ \operatorname{Cov} \left( \bar X_j, \bar Y_j \right) $$

The latter contains the open path through $U_j$.

Adding $Z_j$ does not solve confounding by $U_j$ unless $Z_j$ is a sufficient adjustment for it.

---

## 8. Fixed group effects

A fixed-effects model assigns each group its own intercept:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{ij}) = \alpha_j + \beta_W X_{ij} $$

where the $\alpha_j$ are estimated without a shared population distribution.

Within a group, $U_j$ is constant and absorbed into $\alpha_j$.

The slope is consequently learned from within-group comparisons.

The official form is:

```r
mf <- ulam(
  alist(
    Y ~ bernoulli(p),
    logit(p) <- a[g] + bxy*X + bzy*Z_g,
    a[g] ~ dnorm(0, 10),
    c(bxy, bzy) ~ dnorm(0, 1)
  ), data=dat, chains=4, cores=4
)
```

### What fixed effects accomplish

- They block all time-constant group-level backdoor paths for the within-group exposure contrast.
- They do not require the group intercepts to be independent of $X$.
- They preserve a common within-group slope.

### What fixed effects cost

- There is no partial pooling of group intercepts.
- Sparse groups can have unstable intercept estimates.
- New-group prediction is not naturally defined.
- A purely group-level predictor is collinear with the group indicators.

---

## 9. Why a group-level coefficient becomes unidentified

Suppose $Z_j$ is constant for everyone in group $j$.

The linear predictor is:

$$ \alpha_j+\beta_ZZ_j $$

Define a new intercept:

$$ \alpha_j^* = \alpha_j+\beta_ZZ_j $$

Every value of $\beta_Z$ can be offset by a corresponding change in $\alpha_j$.

The likelihood cannot distinguish them.

This is not a software defect. It is a lack of independent information.

Fixed effects can identify a within-group exposure effect while making a group-level effect unidentified.

---

## 10. Demeaning as a fixed-effects transformation

The script also fits:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{ij}) = \alpha + \beta_W \left(X_{ij}-\bar X_j\right) + \beta_Z Z_j $$

```r
mf2 <- ulam(
  alist(
    Y ~ bernoulli(p),
    logit(p) <- a + bxy*(X-Xbar_g) + bzy*Z_g,
    a ~ dnorm(0, 10),
    c(bxy, bzy) ~ dnorm(0, 1)
  ), data=dat, chains=4, cores=4
)
```

The centered exposure has zero mean in each group:

$$ \sum_{i:g_i=j} \left(X_{ij}-\bar X_j\right) =0 $$

Therefore its coefficient is learned from within-group deviations.

For nonlinear models, explicit fixed intercepts and a demeaning transformation need not be exactly equivalent in every respect. They nevertheless express the same core strategy: remove between-group exposure variation from the slope estimate.

---

## 11. The ordinary varying-intercept model

The multilevel model is:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{ij}) = \alpha_j + \beta_X X_{ij} + \beta_Z Z_j $$

$$ \alpha_j \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar\alpha, \tau \right) $$

Its non-centered form is:

$$ z_j\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,1) $$

$$ \alpha_j=\bar\alpha+\tau z_j $$

```r
mr <- ulam(
  alist(
    Y ~ bernoulli(p),
    logit(p) <- a[g] + bxy*X + bzy*Z_g,
    transpars> vector[Ng]:a <<- abar + z*tau,
    z[g] ~ dnorm(0, 1),
    c(bxy, bzy) ~ dnorm(0, 1),
    abar ~ dnorm(0, 1),
    tau ~ dexp(1)
  ), data=dat, chains=4, cores=4
)
```

This model pools noisy group intercepts and is often better for prediction.

But its population prior implies:

$$ \alpha_j \perp X_{ij} \mid \bar\alpha,\tau,\text{included predictors} $$

That independence is false in the simulation: the unobserved group intercept $U_j$ also shifts the distribution of $X$.

Partial pooling then draws intercept variation and exposure variation together in a way that can contaminate $\beta_X$.

---

## 12. The false dichotomy of fixed versus random effects

The words *fixed* and *random* often conceal the important assumptions.

The practical contrast here is:

| Model | Group intercepts pooled? | Allows intercept–exposure association? | New-group prediction? |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Fixed group effects | No | Yes, implicitly | Poorly defined |
| Ordinary varying effects | Yes | No, conditionally | Yes |
| Mundlak varying effects | Yes | Yes, through group exposure mean | Yes |

The choice is not whether groups are metaphysically fixed or random.

The choice is which population assumptions are scientifically defensible for the estimand.

---

## 13. The Mundlak model

The Mundlak strategy adds the group exposure mean to the varying-intercept model:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{ij}) = \alpha_j + \beta_W X_{ij} + \beta_M\bar X_j + \beta_ZZ_j $$

$$ \alpha_j \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar\alpha, \tau \right) $$

The official script calls this **the Mundlak Machine**:

```r
mrx <- ulam(
  alist(
    Y ~ bernoulli(p),
    logit(p) <- a[g] + bxy*X + buy*Xbar_g + bzy*Z_g,
    transpars> vector[Ng]:a <<- abar + z*tau,
    z[g] ~ dnorm(0, 1),
    c(bxy, buy, bzy) ~ dnorm(0, 1),
    abar ~ dnorm(0, 1),
    tau ~ dexp(1)
  ), data=dat, chains=4, cores=4
)
```

The group mean acts as an observed proxy for the group context associated with the exposure.

Conditional on $\bar X_j$, the residual varying intercept is less associated with individual $X_{ij}$.

---

## 14. Interpreting Mundlak coefficients

Start with:

$$ \eta_{ij} = \alpha_j + \beta_WX_{ij} + \beta_M\bar X_j $$

Substitute:

$$ X_{ij} = \left(X_{ij}-\bar X_j\right) + \bar X_j $$

Then:

$$ \eta_{ij} = \alpha_j + \beta_W \left(X_{ij}-\bar X_j\right) + \left(\beta_W+\beta_M\right)\bar X_j $$

Therefore:

- $\beta_W$ is the within-group association;
- $\beta_W+\beta_M$ is the between-group association;
- $\beta_M$ measures the difference between the between- and within-group slopes.

If $\beta_M$ is far from zero, the pooled association is not well represented by one common coefficient.

This difference may arise from group confounding, contextual effects, nonlinearities, measurement differences, or model misspecification. It is not by itself proof of a particular causal mechanism.

---

## 15. Equivalent centered Mundlak form

The model can be written more transparently as:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{ij}) = \alpha_j + \beta_W \left(X_{ij}-\bar X_j\right) + \beta_B\bar X_j $$

where:

$$ \beta_B = \beta_W+\beta_M $$

This form names the coefficients directly:

- $\beta_W$: within-group slope;
- $\beta_B$: between-group slope.

The raw-$X$ form in the official script and the centered form encode the same linear predictor after coefficient transformation.

---

## 16. Why Mundlak can keep pooling

Fixed effects protect the within-group contrast by allowing arbitrary group intercepts.

Ordinary varying effects pool, but impose conditional independence between the group effect and exposure.

Mundlak decomposes the group intercept conceptually:

$$ \text{group intercept} = \text{part associated with }\bar X_j + \text{residual group variation} $$

One representation is:

$$ \alpha_j^{\text{total}} = \bar\alpha + \beta_M\bar X_j + u_j $$

$$ u_j\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,\tau) $$

The residual $u_j$ can be pooled without demanding that the total group intercept be unrelated to exposure composition.

---

## 17. What Mundlak does not guarantee

The Mundlak adjustment is not a universal causal repair.

It does not solve:

- unmeasured individual-level confounding inside groups;
- time-varying group confounding in longitudinal data;
- exposure measurement error;
- interference between individuals;
- exposure-dependent selection into groups;
- positivity failures within groups;
- incorrect link functions or omitted nonlinearities;
- varying treatment effects that correlate with exposure;
- reverse causation.

Its causal value depends on the assumption that relevant unobserved group causes are time-constant or adequately represented by the group exposure distribution.

---

## 18. Positivity and within-group information

Fixed-effects and Mundlak estimates rely on exposure variation within groups.

If every person in group $j$ has the same exposure:

$$ X_{ij}-\bar X_j=0 $$

for all $i$ in that group.

That group provides no information about the within-group slope.

More generally, identification weakens when:

- groups are small;
- within-group exposure variance is low;
- outcomes are rare;
- binary groups have complete separation;
- only a few groups contain both exposure states.

Partial pooling stabilizes estimation, but it cannot manufacture missing within-group contrasts.

---

## 19. Nonlinear links and effect scales

In a logistic model:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{ij}) = \eta_{ij} $$

$\beta_W$ is a log-odds contrast, not a probability difference.

For an intervention changing $X$ from $x_0$ to $x_1$:

$$ p_{ij}(x) = \operatorname{logit}^{-1} \left( \alpha_j + \beta_Wx + \beta_M\bar X_j + \beta_ZZ_j \right) $$

The group-specific probability contrast is:

$$ \Delta_{ij} = p_{ij}(x_1)-p_{ij}(x_0) $$

Even with one common log-odds coefficient, $\Delta_{ij}$ varies across groups because the inverse-logit transformation depends on the baseline.

---

## 20. The real estimand is an intervention

The script explicitly evaluates a one-unit intervention on $X$.

It copies the observed data and changes:

```r
dat1$X <- dat1$X + 1
```

Then it compares posterior expected outcomes before and after the intervention.

For posterior draw $s$:

$$ \Delta_i^{(s)} = p_i^{(s)}(X_i+1) - p_i^{(s)}(X_i) $$

An average intervention contrast is:

$$ \operatorname{ATE}^{(s)} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \Delta_i^{(s)} $$

This is more meaningful than asking only whether $\beta_X$ is near its true simulated value.

On nonlinear outcome scales, coefficient error and intervention error are not interchangeable.

---

## 21. Conditional versus compositional interventions

The official intervention increases individual $X$ while leaving the observed group mean covariate fixed.

That corresponds to a short-run individual intervention conditional on group context.

A different intervention would change the exposure of everyone in a group. Then:

$$ X_{ij}\mapsto X_{ij}+1 $$

and:

$$ \bar X_j\mapsto\bar X_j+1 $$

Both the within-exposure and group-composition terms would change.

These interventions answer different questions. An estiplan must state which variables are recomputed after intervention.

---

## 22. The latent Mundlak model

The script also represents an unobserved group factor directly.

Outcome model:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{ij}) = \alpha_j + \beta_X X_{ij} + \beta_ZZ_j + \beta_{UY}u_j $$

Exposure model:

$$ X_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_{ij},\sigma_X) $$

$$ \mu_{ij} = \alpha_X + \beta_{UX}u_j $$

Latent group distribution:

$$ u_j\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,1) $$

The relevant script is:

```r
mru <- ulam(
  alist(
    Y ~ bernoulli(p),
    logit(p) <- a[g] + bxy*X + bzy*Z_g + buy*u[g],
    transpars> vector[Ng]:a <<- abar + z*tau,
    X ~ normal(mu, sigma),
    mu <- aX + bux*u[g],
    vector[Ng]:u ~ normal(0, 1),
    z[g] ~ dnorm(0, 1),
    c(aX, bxy, buy, bzy) ~ dnorm(0, 1),
    bux ~ dexp(1),
    abar ~ dnorm(0, 1),
    tau ~ dexp(1),
    sigma ~ dexp(1)
  ), data=dat, chains=4, cores=4
)
```

This is a joint generative model of exposure and outcome.

It makes the confounding pathway explicit, but it also makes stronger distributional and identification assumptions.

---

## 23. Ordinary Mundlak versus latent Mundlak

| Feature | Observed-mean Mundlak | Latent Mundlak |
|---|---|---|
| Group context | $\bar X_j$ | Latent $u_j$ |
| Exposure model | Not required | Explicitly modeled |
| Distributional burden | Lower | Higher |
| Handles noisy group means | Only indirectly | Can model latent context |
| Identification | Within-between variation | Joint exposure-outcome assumptions |
| Interpretability | Direct decomposition | Structural latent-factor interpretation |

The latent model should not be preferred merely because it is more elaborate.

The scientific story and calibration behavior determine whether the additional assumptions are useful.

---

## 24. Intercept accuracy and slope accuracy

The script compares fixed and varying intercept estimates against the known simulated truth:

$$ \alpha_0+U_j $$

Partial pooling often improves group-intercept accuracy by shrinking noisy estimates toward the population.

But a model can estimate intercepts well while estimating the causal slope poorly.

Conversely, fixed effects may protect the within-group slope while yielding noisy group intercepts.

Model performance is estimand-specific:

$$ \text{good prediction of group baselines} \not\Rightarrow \text{good causal intervention estimate} $$

---

## 25. Group-specific treatment effects on the probability scale

For group $j$, the script computes:

$$ \Delta_j = \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\alpha_j+\beta_X) - \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\alpha_j) $$

when comparing $X=1$ with $X=0$.

Although $\beta_X$ is constant across groups, $\Delta_j$ is not.

This distinction prevents a common error:

> A common coefficient on the link scale does not imply a common treatment effect on the outcome scale.

The fixed-effects and varying-effects models can therefore differ in their group-specific probability contrasts even if their slope posteriors appear similar.

## 26. A simulation comparison protocol

For repeated simulated data sets:

1. generate groups, $U_j$, $X_{ij}$, and $Y_{ij}$;
2. fit each candidate model;
3. draw posterior interventions;
4. compare posterior estimates with the known interventional truth;
5. repeat under different group sizes and confounding strengths.

Useful metrics include:

### Bias

$$ \operatorname{Bias} = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^R \left( \widehat\Delta_r-\Delta_r^{\text{true}} \right) $$

### Root mean squared error

$$ \operatorname{RMSE} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^R \left( \widehat\Delta_r-\Delta_r^{\text{true}} \right)^2 } $$

### Interval coverage

$$ \operatorname{Coverage} = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^R \mathbb{I} \left( \Delta_r^{\text{true}} \in \operatorname{CI}_r \right) $$

No single simulation setting establishes a universal winner.

## 27. Homework B04: the Bangladesh estimand

The homework asks for the influence of surviving children on contraceptive use while considering unmeasured district-level confounding.

The outcome is binary:

$$ C_i = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{contraception is used}\\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} $$

Let:

- $K_i$ denote surviving or living children;
- $D_i$ denote district;
- $\bar K_d$ denote the district mean of $K$.

The causal estimand must be stated before fitting models.

A possible conditional contrast is:

$$ \Delta(k_1,k_0) = \mathbb{E} \left[ C_i(k_1)-C_i(k_0) \right] $$

for a specified change in number or category of surviving children, holding district context fixed.

Because surviving children is not obviously manipulable in isolation, the causal interpretation requires a scientific intervention story and careful consideration of parental age, fertility history, child mortality, urban residence, and selection.

---

## 28. Homework model 1: district fixed effects

One version is:

$$ C_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_i) $$

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_i) = \alpha_{D_i} + f(K_i) $$

The function $f$ may be:

- linear, if a constant log-odds increment is scientifically defensible;
- indexed by child-count category, if `living.children` is coded categorically;
- monotonic, if increasing order is credible but equal spacing is not;
- nonlinear, if prior predictive checks support a flexible curve.

The fixed-effects estimate derives from within-district differences in children.

---

## 29. Homework model 2: ordinary varying effects

The varying-intercept model is:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_i) = \alpha_{D_i} + f(K_i) $$

$$ \alpha_d \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar\alpha, \sigma_D \right) $$

This improves district estimates through pooling.

But it assumes the residual district effect is independent of the district exposure composition.

If districts with high average numbers of surviving children also have unmeasured causes of contraception, the ordinary hierarchy can mix confounded between-district information into $f(K)$.

---

## 30. Homework model 3: the Mundlak machine

For a linear exposure representation:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_i) = \alpha_{D_i} + \beta_WK_i + \beta_M\bar K_{D_i} $$

$$ \alpha_d \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar\alpha, \sigma_D \right) $$

Equivalently:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_i) = \alpha_{D_i} + \beta_W \left(K_i-\bar K_{D_i}\right) + \beta_B\bar K_{D_i} $$

For a categorical exposure, the decomposition should be performed with district proportions for the relevant categories rather than treating arbitrary category numbers as metric without justification.

The key comparison is whether the ordinary varying-effects slope shifts toward the fixed-effects estimate once district exposure composition is included.

---

## 31. How to compare the homework models

Do not choose a model only by information criteria.

Compare:

1. the implied DAG and identifying assumptions;
2. the posterior within-district contrast;
3. the difference between within- and between-district associations;
4. district intercept shrinkage;
5. posterior predictive behavior by district;
6. intervention contrasts on the probability scale;
7. sensitivity to exposure coding and relevant individual covariates;
8. MCMC diagnostics and prior sensitivity.

Expected conceptual pattern:

- the naive or ordinary varying-effects estimate may use confounded between-district information;
- fixed effects isolate within-district variation but do not pool districts;
- Mundlak aims to preserve the fixed-effects protection for the within contrast while retaining partial pooling.

The best estimate is the one supported by the causal assumptions and calibrated behavior, not automatically the narrowest posterior.

## 32. Transition from groups to networks

A grouped data set maps each observation to one group.

A dyadic data set maps each observation to two actors:

$$ i\rightarrow j $$

The response may depend on:

- the general tendency of $i$ to send;
- the general tendency of $j$ to receive;
- the particular relationship between $i$ and $j$;
- measured sender, receiver, or dyad features.

This creates overlapping, cross-classified dependence.

Standard regression that treats directed dyad observations as independent cannot represent this structure.

---

## 33. The Koster–Leckie gift data

The script loads:

```r
data(KosterLeckie)
d1 <- kl_dyads
d2 <- kl_households
```

For each unordered household pair $d=(A,B)$, the data contain two directed counts:

$$ G_{AB} = \text{gifts from household A to household B} $$

$$ G_{BA} = \text{gifts from household B to household A} $$

The pair is one dyad with two directions.

A scatterplot of $G_{AB}$ against $G_{BA}$ is descriptive, but raw correlation mixes:

- generalized generosity;
- generalized need or receiving;
- dyad-specific reciprocity;
- household wealth;
- productive activity;
- count noise.

The model separates these sources.

## 34. Ordered and unordered dyads

An unordered dyad is the pair:

$$ \{A,B\} $$

There are:

$$ \binom{N}{2} = \frac{N(N-1)}{2} $$

unordered dyads among $N$ actors.

Each unordered dyad contains two ordered directions:

$$ A\rightarrow B $$

and:

$$ B\rightarrow A $$

The official script builds dyads with:

```r
dyads <- t(combn(N, 2))
```

and models the two outcomes together.

---

## 35. A synthetic latent friendship process

The script first simulates a shared dyad state:

$$ F_d \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.1) $$

If $F_d=1$, ties in both directions are highly likely in the teaching simulation.

Otherwise each directed tie follows a low base rate:

$$ Y_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli} \left( \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\alpha) \right) $$

The resulting directed ties generate gift counts with different Poisson rates for tie and no-tie states.

This simulation shows how a shared relationship can induce positive association between two directions without forcing them to be identical.

---

## 36. The dyad-only social-relations model

For dyad $d=(A,B)$:

$$ G_{AB,d} \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_{AB,d}) $$

$$ G_{BA,d} \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_{BA,d}) $$

$$ \log\lambda_{AB,d} = \alpha+T_{d,AB} $$

$$ \log\lambda_{BA,d} = \alpha+T_{d,BA} $$

The directed dyad effects are jointly distributed:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} T_{d,AB}\\ T_{d,BA} \end{bmatrix} \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}, \mathbf S_T \right) $$

with:

$$ \mathbf S_T = \mathbf D_T \mathbf R_T \mathbf D_T $$

---

## 37. Reciprocity as dyad correlation

The off-diagonal element:

$$ \rho_T = \operatorname{Cor} \left( T_{d,AB},T_{d,BA} \right) $$

describes balance within dyads after other model components are accounted for.

### Positive $\rho_T$

Dyads with above-average giving in one direction tend also to have above-average giving in the other.

### Near-zero $\rho_T$

Knowing one directed deviation provides little information about the reverse direction.

### Negative $\rho_T$

Dyads with above-average flow in one direction tend to have below-average reverse flow.

The term **reciprocity** is a substantive interpretation. The parameter itself is conditional covariance, and may also reflect kinship, shared opportunity, mutual obligation, or omitted dyad features.

---

## 38. Dyad scale and dyad correlation

For a two-direction dyad model:

$$ \mathbf D_T = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{T,AB}&0\\ 0&\sigma_{T,BA} \end{bmatrix} $$

and:

$$ \mathbf R_T = \begin{bmatrix} 1&\rho_T\\ \rho_T&1 \end{bmatrix} $$

In the symmetric official model, the same scale is used in each direction:

$$ \sigma_{T,AB} = \sigma_{T,BA} = \sigma_T $$

Then:

$$ \operatorname{Cov} \left(T_{d,AB},T_{d,BA}\right) = \rho_T\sigma_T^2 $$

A large correlation with negligible $\sigma_T$ has little practical importance.

---

## 39. Non-centered dyad effects

The script constructs effects using independent standardized scores:

$$ \mathbf z_d \sim \operatorname{MVNormal}(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I) $$

$$ \mathbf T_d = \mathbf D_T \mathbf L_T \mathbf z_d $$

where $\mathbf L_T$ is the Cholesky factor of the correlation matrix.

In `ulam`:

```r
transpars> matrix[N_dyads,2]:T <-
  compose_noncentered(rep_vector(sigma_T, 2), L_Rho_T, Z)
matrix[2,N_dyads]:Z ~ normal(0, 1)
cholesky_factor_corr[2]:L_Rho_T ~ lkj_corr_cholesky(2)
sigma_T ~ exponential(1)
```

This is the same covariance machinery introduced in B03, now applied to paired directions of a relation.

---

## 40. From dyads to generalized giving and receiving

The dyad-only model attributes all stable heterogeneity to pairs.

But some households may generally give more to everyone, while others generally receive more from everyone.

For household $h$, define:

$$ g_h = \text{generalized giving effect} $$

$$ r_h = \text{generalized receiving effect} $$

Model them jointly:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} g_h\\ r_h \end{bmatrix} \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}, \mathbf S_{gr} \right) $$

This creates a second correlated varying-effects population.

---

## 41. The full social-relations linear predictor

For dyad $d=(A,B)$:

$$ \log\lambda_{AB,d} = \alpha + T_{d,AB} + g_A + r_B $$

$$ \log\lambda_{BA,d} = \alpha + T_{d,BA} + g_B + r_A $$

Each direction receives:

1. a global baseline;
2. a direction-specific dyad effect;
3. the sender's giving effect;
4. the receiver's receiving effect.

The symmetry of the construction matters. When direction reverses, the same household changes from sender to receiver.

---

## 42. The complete varying-effects structure

The model contains two populations:

### Dyad population

$$ \mathbf T_d \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \mathbf 0, \mathbf S_T \right) $$

### Household population

$$ \mathbf{gr}_h \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \mathbf 0, \mathbf S_{gr} \right) $$

where:

$$ \mathbf{gr}_h = \begin{bmatrix} g_h\\ r_h \end{bmatrix} $$

The two correlation parameters answer distinct questions:

$$ \rho_T = \text{association between directions within a dyad} $$

$$ \rho_{gr} = \text{association between a household's giving and receiving tendencies} $$

They must not be conflated.

---

## 43. Interpreting generalized giving–receiving correlation

The household correlation is:

$$ \rho_{gr} = \operatorname{Cor}(g_h,r_h) $$

### Positive correlation

Households that give more than average also tend to receive more than average.

Possible mechanisms include high activity, centrality, or greater opportunity for exchange.

### Negative correlation

Households that give more tend to receive less, and households that give less tend to receive more.

Possible mechanisms include need-based transfers or resource inequality.

### Near-zero correlation

General giving tendency contains little information about general receiving tendency.

These are conditional population patterns, not automatically causal mechanisms.

---

## 44. Cross-classification in a network

Each household appears in many dyads and in both roles.

For example, household $A$ may appear in:

$$ A\rightarrow B, \quad A\rightarrow C, \quad D\rightarrow A $$

Observations are therefore not nested in a single simple tree.

The data are cross-classified by:

- sender household;
- receiver household;
- unordered dyad.

The model uses shared varying effects to represent the repeated appearance of each unit.

---

## 45. Adding household wealth

Let $W_h$ be standardized household wealth.

The official feature model defines:

$$ G_A = g_A+\beta_{WG}W_A $$

for giving and:

$$ R_B = r_B+\beta_{WR}W_B $$

for receiving.

Then:

$$ \log\lambda_{AB,d} = \alpha + T_{AB,d} + G_A + R_B $$

The two wealth coefficients need not be equal:

$$ \beta_{WG} \neq \beta_{WR} $$

Wealth may raise giving while lowering receiving, or have another pattern supported by the posterior.

---

## 46. Adding a dyad-level production feature

The script includes standardized dyad production, denoted $A_d$ in the code.

It enters both directions:

$$ T_{AB,d}^{*} = T_{AB,d}+\beta_AA_d $$

$$ T_{BA,d}^{*} = T_{BA,d}+\beta_AA_d $$

This is appropriate only if the predictor is genuinely shared by the unordered dyad and its hypothesized influence is symmetric.

If a covariate is directional, the two directions need separate values or coefficients.

---

## 47. Explained and residual network variation

After adding predictors, compare the posterior scales:

$$ \sigma_T^{\text{before}} $$

and:

$$ \sigma_T^{\text{after}} $$

A reduction suggests that measured dyad features explain some residual relationship heterogeneity.

Likewise, changes in:

$$ \sigma_g, \quad \sigma_r, \quad \rho_T, \quad \rho_{gr} $$

show how predictors reorganize unexplained variation.

But variance reduction is descriptive. It does not prove that the added feature causally generates the network.

---

## 48. Posterior network reconstruction

For each posterior draw, the dyad effects can be placed into an adjacency-like matrix:

$$ \mathbf T^{(s)} = \left[T_{ij}^{(s)}\right] $$

The script plots a network from posterior mean effects and animates networks across posterior draws.

This distinguishes:

- raw gift counts;
- posterior mean latent relationship effects;
- uncertainty in the network representation.

A single posterior mean graph hides uncertainty. An animation or edge-wise interval summary reveals that many apparent edges are weakly determined.

---

## 49. A crucial network-scale caution

In the fitted Poisson model, $T_{ij}$ is a continuous log-rate deviation.

It is not itself:

- a binary friendship indicator;
- a posterior probability that an edge exists;
- a causal effect of one household on another.

Exponentiation gives a multiplicative rate factor:

$$ \exp(T_{ij}) $$

relative to other components of the predictor.

A binary latent-edge interpretation would require an explicit mixture or latent-state model.

---

## 50. Posterior expected rates

For posterior draw $s$:

$$ \lambda_{AB,d}^{(s)} = \exp \left( \alpha^{(s)} + T_{AB,d}^{(s)} + g_A^{(s)} + r_B^{(s)} \right) $$

Posterior expected generalized giving for household $h$ can be summarized as:

$$ \mathbb E \left[ \exp(\alpha+g_h) \mid D \right] $$

and generalized receiving as:

$$ \mathbb E \left[ \exp(\alpha+r_h) \mid D \right] $$

Because:

$$ \mathbb E[\exp(X)] \neq \exp(\mathbb E[X]) $$

the transformation should be performed within each posterior draw before averaging.

---

## 51. Posterior ellipses for household roles

For household $h$, posterior draws give pairs:

$$ \left( \alpha+g_h, \alpha+r_h \right)^{(s)} $$

A posterior ellipse represents uncertainty and covariance in that household's generalized roles.

The population pattern across household posterior means reflects $\rho_{gr}$.

These are different covariances:

- within-household posterior covariance expresses estimation uncertainty;
- across-household population covariance expresses modeled heterogeneity.

Plotting both is useful, but their interpretations must remain separate.

---

## 52. Prior predictive checks for the network model

The log link can generate implausibly large counts under diffuse priors.

Prior predictive simulation should inspect:

- distribution of total gifts;
- proportion of zero dyads;
- maximum dyad count;
- concentration of gifts among households;
- plausible multiplicative effects $\exp(g_h)$ and $\exp(r_h)$;
- plausible reciprocity patterns;
- degree and strength distributions in simulated networks.

A prior such as:

$$ \alpha\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,1) $$

should be interpreted on the rate scale:

$$ \exp(\alpha) $$

Likewise, exponential priors on varying-effect scales should be tested by simulating entire networks, not inspected only as one-dimensional densities.

---

## 53. Posterior predictive checks for relational data

Node-wise histograms are not enough.

Useful replicated statistics include:

### Total flow

$$ T_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i\neq j}G_{ij} $$

### Household outflow

$$ O_i = \sum_{j\neq i}G_{ij} $$

### Household inflow

$$ I_i = \sum_{j\neq i}G_{ji} $$

### Dyadic balance

$$ B_{ij} = |G_{ij}-G_{ji}| $$

### Zero proportion

$$ Z = \frac{1}{N(N-1)} \sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb I(G_{ij}=0) $$

Also inspect whether the model reproduces hubs, isolates, reciprocity, and heavy tails.

---

## 54. Dependence not represented by the model

The social-relations model represents sender, receiver, and dyad dependence.

It does not automatically model:

- transitivity;
- triadic closure;
- community or block structure;
- temporal network evolution;
- strategic response;
- diffusion or contagion;
- missing-not-at-random ties;
- observation effort differences;
- zero inflation beyond the Poisson model.

For example, if $A$ exchanges with $B$ and $B$ exchanges with $C$, the model does not thereby make $A$–$C$ exchange more likely.

The book identifies transitivity as an important omitted structure and points toward stochastic block models.

---

## 55. Confounding inside networks

Social ties are rarely randomized.

An observed association between a tie and an outcome may reflect:

- homophily;
- shared environment;
- common shocks;
- selection into the relationship;
- latent traits;
- reverse causation;
- interference.

A sophisticated network likelihood does not by itself identify peer influence.

The same lesson from the Mundlak section returns:

$$ \text{better dependence model} \not\Rightarrow \text{causal identification} $$

---

## 56. Linking the two halves of B04

The Mundlak and network models share a deeper structure.

| Group-confounding model | Social-relations model |
|---|---|
| $U_j$ shifts members of a group | $T_{ij}$ shifts both directions of a dyad |
| $\bar X_j$ carries group composition | dyad and household features carry relational context |
| group intercepts induce dependence | sender, receiver, and dyad effects induce dependence |
| within-group contrast isolates individual variation | role decomposition isolates sender, receiver, and pair variation |
| partial pooling stabilizes groups | partial pooling stabilizes nodes and dyads |

In both cases, a flat regression combines signals arising at different levels.

The remedy begins by decomposing the data according to the generative structure.

---

## 57. Same-group and new-group predictions

For an observed group $j$, predictions condition on its posterior intercept:

$$ \alpha_j^{(s)} $$

For a new group:

$$ \alpha_{\text{new}}^{(s)} \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \bar\alpha^{(s)}, \tau^{(s)} \right) $$

In a Mundlak model, prediction for a new group additionally requires a value or model for:

$$ \bar X_{\text{new}} $$

For a network, distinguish:

- a new interaction among known households;
- a new dyad of known households;
- a known household paired with a new household;
- two entirely new households.

Each target requires different varying effects to be conditioned on or simulated.

---

## 58. New-dyad prediction

For a new dyad between known households $A$ and $B$:

$$ \mathbf T_{d,\text{new}}^{(s)} \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \mathbf 0, \mathbf S_T^{(s)} \right) $$

while $g_A$, $r_A$, $g_B$, and $r_B$ come from their existing posteriors.

For two new households, draw jointly:

$$ \begin{bmatrix}g_A\\r_A\end{bmatrix}^{(s)}, \begin{bmatrix}g_B\\r_B\end{bmatrix}^{(s)} \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \mathbf0, \mathbf S_{gr}^{(s)} \right) $$

and draw the dyad effects as well.

Setting all varying effects to zero predicts an average latent unit, not a randomly selected new unit.

---

## 59. LKJ priors in B04

The model uses:

$$ \mathbf R \sim \operatorname{LKJCorr}(\eta) $$

through its Cholesky representation.

With:

$$ \eta=2 $$

the prior mildly favors correlation matrices nearer the identity.

This regularizes both:

- dyad-direction correlation $\rho_T$;
- household giving-receiving correlation $\rho_{gr}$.

The prior does not state that reciprocity is absent. It states that extreme correlation needs evidence.

Prior sensitivity matters when there are few households or few informative dyads.

---

## 60. MCMC diagnostics

The multilevel network model is high-dimensional.

Check:

- divergent transitions;
- maximum tree depth;
- rank-normalized $\widehat R$;
- bulk effective sample size;
- tail effective sample size;
- trace and rank plots;
- energy diagnostics;
- posterior geometry of scale and correlation parameters.

Pay special attention to:

$$ \sigma_T, \quad \sigma_g, \quad \sigma_r, \quad \rho_T, \quad \rho_{gr} $$

Non-centering often improves sampling when group-level scales are small or group information is weak, but centered and non-centered forms should be judged empirically.

---

## 61. Model comparison without causal confusion

Predictive criteria answer questions about expected predictive accuracy under a specified prediction target.

They do not determine whether:

- a backdoor path is closed;
- a group effect is independent of exposure;
- an intervention is well defined;
- a tie causes an outcome;
- a wealth coefficient is causally identified.

For the homework, an ordinary varying-effects model might predict well by exploiting between-district association while biasing the within-district causal estimand.

Model comparison must therefore be paired with causal design.

---

## 62. A recommended B04 workflow

### Step 1: draw the causal graph

Mark individual, group, household, and dyad variables.

### Step 2: define the estimand

State the intervention, target population, contrast, and scale.

### Step 3: identify sources of dependence

Decide which observations share groups, actors, receivers, or dyads.

### Step 4: decompose predictors

Separate within-group deviations from group means when exposure and group effects may be associated.

### Step 5: write the generative model

Include each role and direction explicitly.

### Step 6: simulate priors

Inspect outcomes and network summaries, not only parameters.

### Step 7: fit and diagnose

Use non-centering and Cholesky factors where appropriate.

### Step 8: validate by simulation

Recover known interventions and latent structures under realistic scenarios.

### Step 9: compute posterior estimands

Transform every draw to probability, rate, or network-summary scale.

### Step 10: report assumptions and residual dependence

Separate descriptive covariance from causal conclusions.

## 63. Common errors exposed by B04

### Error 1: Treating clustering as only a standard-error problem

Group dependence can bias a causal coefficient, not merely narrow its interval.

### Error 2: Assuming varying intercepts automatically deconfound

Ordinary varying effects impose a conditional independence that may fail.

### Error 3: Calling partial pooling equivalent to fixed effects

Pooling can reintroduce between-group information into a slope.

### Error 4: Ignoring the group mean of the exposure

This conceals the within-between difference.

### Error 5: Interpreting $\beta_M$ as the between slope

In the raw-$X$ Mundlak form, the between slope is $\beta_W+\beta_M$.

### Error 6: Using category codes as a metric exposure without justification

Ordered labels do not guarantee equal causal spacing.

### Error 7: Expecting fixed effects to identify group-level predictors

Time-constant group predictors are collinear with unrestricted group intercepts.

### Error 8: Comparing only coefficient posteriors

The estimand may be a probability-scale intervention.

### Error 9: Recomputing or freezing group means without defining the intervention

These choices represent different interventions.

### Error 10: Treating a dyad as two independent rows

The directions share a relationship and actors.

### Error 11: Calling raw directional correlation reciprocity

Raw association mixes household and dyad heterogeneity.

### Error 12: Confusing $\rho_T$ with $\rho_{gr}$

One is within dyads; the other is across household roles.

### Error 13: Treating a posterior dyad effect as an edge probability

It is a continuous log-rate deviation in this model.

### Error 14: Plotting only the posterior mean network

This hides edge uncertainty.

### Error 15: Claiming peer influence from a network varying-effects model

Homophily, shared causes, and selection remain possible.

### Error 16: Ignoring transitivity

Sender, receiver, and dyad effects do not model triadic closure.

### Error 17: Using diffuse Gaussian priors under a log link

They can imply enormous prior predictive counts.

### Error 18: Setting new-unit effects to zero

That predicts an average unit rather than integrating over population heterogeneity.

## 66. Compact model templates

### 66.1 Fixed-effects template

$$ Y_{ij}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_{ij}) $$

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{ij}) = \alpha_j+\beta_WX_{ij} $$

### 66.2 Ordinary varying-effects template

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{ij}) = \alpha_j+\beta_X X_{ij} $$

$$ \alpha_j\sim\operatorname{Normal}(\bar\alpha,\tau) $$

### 66.3 Mundlak template

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{ij}) = \alpha_j + \beta_W(X_{ij}-\bar X_j) + \beta_B\bar X_j $$

$$ \alpha_j\sim\operatorname{Normal}(\bar\alpha,\tau) $$

### 66.4 Dyad-only template

$$ G_{ij,d}\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_{ij,d}) $$

$$ \log\lambda_{ij,d}=\alpha+T_{d,ij} $$

$$ \begin{bmatrix}T_{d,ij}\\T_{d,ji}\end{bmatrix} \sim \operatorname{MVNormal}(\mathbf0,\mathbf S_T) $$

### 66.5 Full social-relations template

$$ \log\lambda_{ij,d} = \alpha+T_{d,ij}+g_i+r_j $$

$$ \begin{bmatrix}g_h\\r_h\end{bmatrix} \sim \operatorname{MVNormal}(\mathbf0,\mathbf S_{gr}) $$

## 67. Glossary

**Between-group association:** Association among group averages or contexts.

**Contextual effect:** Difference between a between-group association and a within-group association under a stated parameterization.

**Correlated random effects:** Econometric name often used for a varying-effects model that permits association between group effects and predictors, commonly through group means.

**Dyad:** A pair of actors.

**Fixed effects:** Here, separate unpooled group intercepts; the term has other meanings elsewhere.

**Generalized giving:** A household's tendency to send gifts across partners, after other modeled terms.

**Generalized receiving:** A household's tendency to receive gifts across partners, after other modeled terms.

**Group-mean centering:** Replacing $X_{ij}$ with $X_{ij}-\bar X_j$.

**Group-level confounding:** Confounding generated by a cause shared by members of a group that is also associated with exposure.

**Mundlak adjustment:** Adding group means of time- or individual-varying predictors to a varying-effects model.

**Ordered dyad:** A directed pair, such as $i\to j$.

**Reciprocity:** Conditional association between the two directions of a dyad; its substantive meaning depends on the model.

**Relational data:** Data whose observations concern pairs or larger sets of interacting units.

**Sender effect:** An actor's general tendency to produce outgoing outcomes.

**Receiver effect:** An actor's general tendency to attract incoming outcomes.

**Unordered dyad:** The pair $\{i,j\}$ without choosing a direction.

**Within-group association:** Association based on differences among observations in the same group.
