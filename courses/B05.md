## 1. Main thesis

B04 introduced the social-relations decomposition:

$$ \text{directed outcome} = \text{sender tendency} + \text{receiver tendency} + \text{dyad relation} + \text{noise} $$

B05 asks what happens when the data-collection design fixes one of those components.

The central lesson is:

> **A network model must represent both the social process and the observation design. When actors nominate under a fixed quota, unrestricted generalized giving is conditioned away or severely censored, while generalized receiving and dyadic reciprocity remain inferential targets.**

The second lesson is:

> **Reciprocity is not the raw correlation between two observed directions. It is the posterior correlation of residual dyad effects after accounting for stable actor roles and the likelihood that generated the observations.**

The workflow is:

$$ \text{nomination design} \rightarrow \text{directed dyad representation} \rightarrow \text{receiver and dyad effects} \rightarrow \text{correlated dyad population} \rightarrow \text{posterior popularity and reciprocity} \rightarrow \text{network-level validation} $$

## 3. The monks data

The data describe 18 monks living in one monastery.

Each monk reported which other monks he liked and disliked over three observation periods.

The homework focuses only on likes.

For an unordered dyad $d=(A,B)$, the variables are:

| Variable | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `A` | Index of the first monk in the stored dyad |
| `B` | Index of the second monk |
| `like_AB` | Number of periods in which A nominated B as liked |
| `like_BA` | Number of periods in which B nominated A as liked |
| `dislike_AB` | Number of periods in which A nominated B as disliked |
| `dislike_BA` | Number of periods in which B nominated A as disliked |
| `A_name` | Name corresponding to A |
| `B_name` | Name corresponding to B |

Each directional count lies in:

$$ \{0,1,2,3\} $$

because there are three observation periods.

---

## 4. Number of dyads and directed relations

With:

$$ N=18 $$

monks, the number of unordered dyads is:

$$ D = \binom{18}{2} = \frac{18\times17}{2} =153 $$

Each dyad contains two directions, so the number of directed monk pairs is:

$$ 2D =306 $$

There are no self-ties:

$$ i\neq j $$

The dyad table is an efficient representation of the off-diagonal cells of an $18\times18$ adjacency matrix.

---

## 5. Why A and B are only storage labels

For dyad $d$, the data assign one monk to position A and the other to position B.

These labels are arbitrary.

The scientific relation:

$$ i\leftrightarrow j $$

must not change if the row is relabeled:

$$ A\leftrightarrow B $$

Therefore a valid model must be invariant to exchanging A and B while also exchanging the two directional outcomes.

This is why the dyad effects in both directions share the same marginal standard deviation.

---

## 6. Three levels of variation

The like data contain at least three potential sources of variation.

### Observation variation

The same monk may nominate a peer in some periods and not others.

### Actor variation

Some monks may generally attract more nominations from all peers.

### Dyad variation

Some particular pairs may have unusually strong or weak affinity after accounting for popularity.

A flat model combines all three.

The social-relations model assigns a distinct mathematical component to each source.

---

## 7. What is fixed by design?

The homework states that each monk named only three likes, and describes three observation periods.

Let:

$$ q = \text{allowed nominations in a completed wave} $$

and let $T_i$ be the number of completed waves represented for sender $i$.

At each observation period $t$, for sender $i$:

$$ \sum_{j\neq i}Y_{ijt} =q $$

Under a complete three-wave design with $q=3$:

$$ \sum_{j\neq i}Y_{ij+} =qT_i $$

where:

$$ Y_{ij+} = \sum_{t=1}^{3}Y_{ijt} $$

The design intends to constrain the amount of outgoing nomination, so generalized giving is not the homework target.

### 7.1 Repository data audit

The local `week08_Monks.csv` should not be assumed to have a literal aggregate row total of 9. A direct audit gives outgoing like totals of 6 for most monks and 7 or 8 for two monks.

That discrepancy may reflect incomplete waves, preprocessing, or details of the original nomination coding that are not documented in the homework page.

Therefore:

- preserve the homework's substantive conclusion that giving is fixed or conditioned by design;
- use the observed directional counts with their stated three-trial bound;
- do not hard-code an aggregate total of 9 without reconciling the raw-data construction;
- use sender-specific observed totals $m_i$ in any conditional allocation model.

The outgoing margin is a survey constraint, not a discovered social property.

---

## 8. Why generalized giving is not estimated

Let $g_i$ denote monk $i$'s generalized tendency to nominate others.

In an unrestricted interaction process, variation in $g_i$ could be learned from variation in total outgoing nominations.

The analysis conditions on each sender's realized outgoing margin:

$$ O_i = \sum_{j\neq i}Y_{ij+} =m_i $$

The small observed differences in $m_i$ documented above should not automatically be interpreted as social giving heterogeneity. They may arise from completeness or preprocessing under the quota-based protocol.

There is no unrestricted outdegree variation from which to cleanly estimate a generalized giving effect.

Including $g_i$ anyway asks the model to infer a latent dimension that the design has conditioned away.

The appropriate B05 model therefore omits $g_i$ or conditions it out.

---

## 9. Fixed does not mean scientifically equal

The survey design caps and largely equalizes observed outgoing counts.

It does not prove that monks have equal latent sociability or generosity.

A monk who would naturally name ten peers and a monk who would naturally name only three both appear with exactly three names under a three-nomination limit.

Thus:

$$ \text{equal observed outdegree} \not\Rightarrow \text{equal latent propensity to like} $$

The data cannot recover unrestricted generalized giving without additional information or a richer elicitation design.

---

## 10. What remains identifiable?

The design still allows inference about:

### Generalized receiving

Some monks are selected by many different peers.

### Dyad-specific affinity

A particular sender may repeatedly select a particular receiver beyond what the receiver's general popularity predicts.

### Reciprocity

The two directional dyad deviations may be positively or negatively associated.

These quantities draw on variation in *who* is nominated, not how many nominations each sender makes.

---

## 11. The raw adjacency matrix

Define:

$$ \mathbf Y = \left[Y_{ij}\right] $$

where:

$$ Y_{ij} = \text{number of periods in which monk }i\text{ liked monk }j $$

The diagonal is structurally absent:

$$ Y_{ii}=0 $$

Rows represent senders.

Columns represent receivers.

Because row totals are fixed, variation in column totals is the most direct raw indication of popularity.

But column totals alone do not separate popularity from clusters of strong dyadic relations.

---

## 12. Raw popularity

The raw incoming total for monk $j$ is:

$$ I_j = \sum_{i\neq j}Y_{ij} $$

A high $I_j$ means monk $j$ received many observed like nominations.

This is a useful descriptive statistic.

It is not yet the generalized receiving effect because:

- repeated nominations from one peer may dominate;
- dyad affinity is not separated;
- the count likelihood and shrinkage are ignored;
- uncertainty differs across patterns with the same total;
- nomination constraints induce dependence across possible receivers.

---

## 13. Raw reciprocity

A naive analysis might correlate:

$$ Y_{AB} $$

with:

$$ Y_{BA} $$

across dyads.

This raw correlation can be misleading because it mixes:

- popularity of A;
- popularity of B;
- stable dyad affinity;
- bounded count noise;
- the nomination quota;
- arbitrary A/B ordering.

B05 seeks residual reciprocity after the actor-level receiving effects have been accounted for.

---

## 14. The minimal B05 social-relations model

For dyad $d=(A,B)$, define:

$$ Y_{AB,d} = \text{like count from A to B} $$

$$ Y_{BA,d} = \text{like count from B to A} $$

A receiver-only social-relations predictor is:

$$ \eta_{AB,d} = \alpha + r_B + T_{d,AB} $$

$$ \eta_{BA,d} = \alpha + r_A + T_{d,BA} $$

There is no generalized giving term.

---

## 15. Receiver effects

Assign each monk a partially pooled receiving effect:

$$ r_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma_r) $$

The zero population mean identifies $\alpha$ as the average baseline on the link scale.

The scale:

$$ \sigma_r $$

measures heterogeneity in popularity.

If $\sigma_r$ is near zero, the data support little persistent difference in receiving after dyad effects are included.

If it is large, monks differ substantially in generalized popularity.

---

## 16. Dyad effects

Each unordered dyad receives two directional deviations:

$$ \mathbf T_d = \begin{bmatrix} T_{d,AB}\\ T_{d,BA} \end{bmatrix} $$

with population model:

$$ \mathbf T_d \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}, \mathbf S_T \right) $$

Because A/B labels are arbitrary:

$$ \mathbf S_T = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_T^2&\rho_T\sigma_T^2\\ \rho_T\sigma_T^2&\sigma_T^2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Both directions share the same marginal scale $\sigma_T$.

---

## 17. Reciprocity parameter

The reciprocity parameter is:

$$ \rho_T = \operatorname{Cor} \left( T_{d,AB},T_{d,BA} \right) $$

It describes the population pattern of directional dyad effects.

### Positive reciprocity

When A likes B more than expected from B's popularity, B also tends to like A more than expected from A's popularity.

### Negative reciprocity

An unusually strong nomination in one direction tends to accompany an unusually weak reverse direction.

### Near-zero reciprocity

The residual affinity in one direction provides little information about the reverse direction.

---

## 18. Reciprocity is conditional

$\rho_T$ is not a correlation of raw nominations.

It is conditional on:

- the global intercept;
- generalized receiving effects;
- the likelihood;
- the shared dyad scale;
- any additional predictors;
- the prior model.

If receiver effects are omitted, the dyad covariance may absorb patterns caused by popularity.

If important faction or role variables are omitted, $\rho_T$ may summarize those as well.

---

## 19. Reciprocity is not necessarily mutual affection

A positive $\rho_T$ is compatible with several mechanisms:

- mutual liking;
- shared faction membership;
- common work assignments;
- kinship or prior acquaintance;
- common status;
- strategic alliance;
- correlated reporting behavior;
- unmeasured triadic structure.

The statistical parameter describes balance after modeled adjustments.

The word *reciprocity* is a scientific interpretation that requires contextual evidence.

---

## 20. Choosing the observation model

The counts are bounded:

$$ 0\le Y_{ij}\le3 $$

and outgoing nominations are quota-constrained.

Several likelihoods are possible.

| Likelihood | What it represents | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Poisson | Aggregate count intensity | Ignores upper bound and quota unless conditioned |
| Binomial | Three repeated binary nomination opportunities | Ignores competition among receivers |
| Multinomial | Fixed outgoing total allocated among receivers | Can permit more than one selection per receiver per wave after aggregation |
| Choice without replacement | Exactly three distinct nominees per wave | Requires wave-level choice-set data or a more complex aggregated likelihood |

The correct choice follows the observation process and available data resolution.

---

## 21. Binomial working model

If each wave is treated as one binary opportunity for $i$ to nominate $j$:

$$ Y_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(3,p_{ij}) $$

with:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{AB,d}) = \alpha+r_B+T_{d,AB} $$

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{BA,d}) = \alpha+r_A+T_{d,BA} $$

This respects the directional count bound.

However, it treats each candidate receiver as conditionally independent, even though a sender can choose only three monks in each wave.

It is therefore a useful working model, not an exact encoding of the quota.

---

## 22. Poisson working model

The direct adaptation of the Chapter 14 gift model is:

$$ Y_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_{ij}) $$

$$ \log\lambda_{AB,d} = \alpha+r_B+T_{d,AB} $$

$$ \log\lambda_{BA,d} = \alpha+r_A+T_{d,BA} $$

This preserves the lecture's social-relations structure and is convenient computationally.

But its prior and posterior predictive distributions can produce values above 3.

The frequency of such impossible replications is a direct model check.

---

## 23. Conditional Poisson and the quota

Suppose a sender's counts are independent Poisson variables:

$$ Y_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_{ij}) $$

Conditional on the sender-specific observed total:

$$ \sum_{j\neq i}Y_{ij}=m_i $$

the count vector follows a multinomial distribution with probabilities:

$$ \pi_{ij} = \frac{\lambda_{ij}} {\sum_{k\neq i}\lambda_{ik}} $$

This explains why sender intercepts cancel after conditioning on outgoing totals.

It also provides a bridge from the Poisson social-relations model to a design-aware choice allocation model.

---

## 24. Conditional multinomial model

For sender $i$, collect the aggregated counts:

$$ \mathbf Y_i = \left(Y_{i1},\ldots,Y_{i,i-1},Y_{i,i+1},\ldots,Y_{iN}\right) $$

Then:

$$ \mathbf Y_i \sim \operatorname{Multinomial} \left( m_i, \boldsymbol\pi_i \right) $$

with:

$$ \pi_{ij} = \frac{ \exp(r_j+T_{ij}) }{ \sum_{k\neq i} \exp(r_k+T_{ik}) } $$

Any sender-specific additive intercept cancels from the softmax.

That cancellation formally expresses why generalized giving is unidentified after conditioning on the quota.

---

## 25. Limitation of the aggregated multinomial

Across waves, sender $i$ contributes an aggregate total $m_i$.

But no receiver can be nominated more than once in the same wave.

An ordinary multinomial with total $m_i$ can allow a single receiver to receive more than three nominations, which is impossible in a three-wave data set.

The observed data never exceed 3 because they aggregate three repeated selections without replacement.

An exact model would represent each wave's set of three selected peers.

If wave-level nominations are unavailable, the aggregated likelihood necessarily approximates or sums over unknown wave configurations.

---

## 26. Choice-set model

At wave $t$, monk $i$ chooses a set:

$$ S_{it} \subset \{1,\ldots,N\}\setminus\{i\} $$

with:

$$ |S_{it}|=3 $$

A design-faithful model could assign utility:

$$ u_{ijt} = r_j+T_{ij}+\epsilon_{ijt} $$

and model sampling of three distinct receivers without replacement.

This resembles a Plackett–Luce or conditional-logit choice model.

It is conceptually attractive but more complex than the homework likely requires.

---

## 27. Likelihood choice as sensitivity analysis

A responsible workflow can fit more than one defensible observation model.

Compare:

- posterior $\rho_T$;
- posterior $\sigma_T$;
- posterior $\sigma_r$;
- rank ordering of receiver effects;
- posterior probability that each monk is above average;
- impossible posterior predictive counts;
- reproduction of fixed row totals.

If substantive conclusions change sharply between Poisson and binomial working models, the observation design is carrying important information and should not be treated as a technical detail.

---

## 28. Priors for the intercept

Under a binomial model, an exchangeable baseline probability might be near:

$$ \frac{q}{N-1} $$

per sender, receiver, and wave when each sender chooses $q$ of the other $N-1$ actors.

The corresponding logit is:

$$ \operatorname{logit} \left( \frac{q}{N-1} \right) $$

This provides a natural center for an intercept prior.

The prior should still allow heterogeneity generated by receiver and dyad effects.

Using $\alpha\sim\operatorname{Normal}(0,1)$ without checking would center the baseline probability near one half, implausibly high for a sparse nomination design.

Because the local aggregate margins do not equal the simplest $q=3$, three-wave expectation, document whether the prior center comes from the declared protocol, a reconstructed observation process, or a deliberately weak sensitivity analysis.

---

## 29. Priors for varying-effect scales

The scale priors control plausible heterogeneity:

$$ \sigma_r \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(\lambda_r) $$

$$ \sigma_T \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(\lambda_T) $$

On a logit scale, an effect of magnitude 1 multiplies odds by:

$$ e^1\approx2.72 $$

On a log-rate scale, it multiplies expected counts by the same factor.

Prior predictive simulation should determine whether the implied popularity concentration and repeated nomination patterns are plausible.

---

## 30. Prior for reciprocity

The correlation matrix is:

$$ \mathbf R_T = \begin{bmatrix} 1&\rho_T\\ \rho_T&1 \end{bmatrix} $$

A prior can be assigned through:

$$ \mathbf R_T \sim \operatorname{LKJCorr}(\eta) $$

With:

$$ \eta>1 $$

the prior favors weaker correlations.

The book's gift model uses stronger regularization for dyad correlation than a uniform correlation prior.

Because the monks network has only 153 dyads, prior sensitivity for $\rho_T$ should be inspected.

---

## 31. Non-centered receiver effects

Define standardized scores:

$$ z_{r,j} \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,1) $$

and construct:

$$ r_j = \sigma_rz_{r,j} $$

This non-centered form often improves sampling when $\sigma_r$ is small or individual receiver effects are weakly identified.

It also makes the population prior explicit:

$$ r_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma_r) $$

---

## 32. Non-centered correlated dyad effects

Let:

$$ \mathbf z_d \sim \operatorname{MVNormal}(\mathbf0,\mathbf I) $$

and let $\mathbf L_T$ be a Cholesky factor of $\mathbf R_T$.

Construct:

$$ \mathbf T_d = \sigma_T\mathbf L_T\mathbf z_d $$

Then:

$$ \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf T_d) = \sigma_T^2 \mathbf L_T\mathbf L_T^\top = \mathbf S_T $$

This is the same machinery used for reciprocal gift effects in the Chapter 14 model.

---

## 33. A binomial `ulam` template

The following is a structural template, not a claim that a dedicated B05 script is present:

```r
f_monks_binom <- alist(
  LAB ~ binomial(3, pAB),
  LBA ~ binomial(3, pBA),

  logit(pAB) <- a + r[B] + T[D,1],
  logit(pBA) <- a + r[A] + T[D,2],

  transpars> vector[N_monk]:r <- zr*sigma_r,
  vector[N_monk]:zr ~ normal(0, 1),
  sigma_r ~ exponential(1),

  transpars> matrix[N_dyad,2]:T <-
    compose_noncentered(rep_vector(sigma_T,2), L_Rho_T, Z),
  matrix[2,N_dyad]:Z ~ normal(0, 1),
  cholesky_factor_corr[2]:L_Rho_T ~ lkj_corr_cholesky(2),
  sigma_T ~ exponential(1),

  a ~ normal(a0, 0.7),
  gq> matrix[2,2]:Rho_T <<- Chol_to_Corr(L_Rho_T)
)
```

Exact `ulam` syntax can vary with package version, so compilation is part of verification.

## 36. Why indexing errors are dangerous

The A-to-B predictor must use:

$$ r_B $$

because B is receiving.

The B-to-A predictor must use:

$$ r_A $$

because A is receiving.

Likewise:

$$ T_{d,1} $$

must always correspond to A-to-B, and:

$$ T_{d,2} $$

to B-to-A.

A single swapped index can reverse substantive interpretations while leaving the sampler apparently healthy.

---

## 37. Label-swap test

A powerful unit test is to swap A and B in every row:

$$ A^*=B, \qquad B^*=A $$

and swap outcomes:

$$ Y_{AB}^*=Y_{BA}, \qquad Y_{BA}^*=Y_{AB} $$

After refitting, population summaries should be invariant up to Monte Carlo error:

$$ \sigma_r^*\approx\sigma_r $$

$$ \sigma_T^*\approx\sigma_T $$

$$ \rho_T^*\approx\rho_T $$

This catches asymmetric code that should be symmetric.

---

## 38. Partial pooling of popularity

Each receiver effect is estimated from nominations by 17 possible peers across three waves.

The hierarchy shrinks noisy raw popularity toward the population mean:

$$ r_j^{\text{posterior}} \longrightarrow 0 $$

with the amount of shrinkage determined by:

- the incoming nomination pattern;
- the dyad effects;
- the observation model;
- $\sigma_r$;
- prior information.

Partial pooling reduces overinterpretation of extreme raw column totals.

---

## 39. Popularity versus special relationships

Suppose monk $j$ receives many likes.

Two explanations are:

### General popularity

Many different monks nominate $j$.

### Concentrated dyad affinity

Only one or two monks repeatedly nominate $j$.

The receiver effect $r_j$ is supported most strongly by broad incoming evidence across dyads.

The dyad effects $T_{ij}$ absorb pair-specific departures.

The decomposition asks whether a monk is liked *independent of particular relationships*.

---

## 40. Identifying particularly liked monks

For each monk $j$, summarize:

$$ \Pr(r_j>0\mid D) $$

This is the posterior probability that the monk's receiving effect exceeds the population-average effect on the link scale.

Also report:

- posterior median or mean of $r_j$;
- compatibility interval for $r_j$;
- outcome-scale expected receiving probability or rate;
- rank uncertainty;
- sensitivity to likelihood and prior choices.

A monk should not be declared particularly liked from posterior mean rank alone.

---

## 41. Outcome-scale popularity under the binomial model

An average-dyad receiving probability for monk $j$ sets the dyad effect to zero:

$$ p_j^{\text{avg},(s)} = \operatorname{logit}^{-1} \left( \alpha^{(s)}+r_j^{(s)} \right) $$

The expected number of periods nominated by an average peer is:

$$ \mu_j^{(s)} = 3p_j^{\text{avg},(s)} $$

This is a standardized summary that excludes special dyad affinity.

It is not the observed incoming total and not a prediction for any specific dyad.

---

## 42. Outcome-scale popularity under the Poisson model

For an average dyad:

$$ \lambda_j^{\text{avg},(s)} = \exp \left( \alpha^{(s)}+r_j^{(s)} \right) $$

Because the exponential is nonlinear:

$$ \mathbb E \left[ \exp(\alpha+r_j) \mid D \right] \neq \exp \left( \mathbb E[\alpha+r_j\mid D] \right) $$

Transform each posterior draw before averaging.

---

## 43. Population-average versus random-dyad popularity

Setting:

$$ T_{ij}=0 $$

describes a dyad with average latent effect.

A prediction for a randomly selected new dyad should instead integrate:

$$ \begin{bmatrix}T_{ij}\\T_{ji}\end{bmatrix} \sim \operatorname{MVNormal}(\mathbf0,\mathbf S_T) $$

Under nonlinear links, these targets differ because:

$$ \mathbb E[f(T)] \neq f(\mathbb E[T]) $$

The reporting label should state whether dyad heterogeneity is set to its mean or integrated over.

---

## 44. Rank uncertainty

For each posterior draw $s$, compute monk ranks from $r_j^{(s)}$.

Then summarize:

$$ \Pr \left( \operatorname{rank}_j=1 \mid D \right) $$

or:

$$ \Pr \left( \operatorname{rank}_j\le3 \mid D \right) $$

Posterior mean ranks conceal uncertainty and can imply a sharp hierarchy unsupported by the data.

A rank-interval or rank-probability plot is preferable to a deterministic league table.

---

## 45. Pairwise popularity comparisons

For monks $j$ and $k$, compute:

$$ \Pr(r_j>r_k\mid D) $$

and the posterior difference:

$$ \delta_{jk}^{(s)} = r_j^{(s)}-r_k^{(s)} $$

Pairwise comparisons are often more stable and scientifically meaningful than global ranks.

They also preserve uncertainty about near-ties.

---

## 46. Dyad-level posterior summaries

For dyad $d=(A,B)$, relevant posterior quantities include:

$$ T_{d,AB} $$

$$ T_{d,BA} $$

their average:

$$ M_d = \frac{T_{d,AB}+T_{d,BA}}{2} $$

and their directional asymmetry:

$$ A_d = T_{d,AB}-T_{d,BA} $$

$M_d$ summarizes shared dyadic strength.

$A_d$ summarizes directionality.

Both should be reported with posterior uncertainty.

---

## 47. Mutual-like probabilities

Under a binomial working model, one-wave directional probabilities are:

$$ p_{ij}^{(s)} = \operatorname{logit}^{-1} \left( \alpha^{(s)}+r_j^{(s)}+T_{ij}^{(s)} \right) $$

and:

$$ p_{ji}^{(s)} = \operatorname{logit}^{-1} \left( \alpha^{(s)}+r_i^{(s)}+T_{ji}^{(s)} \right) $$

Conditional independence would give:

$$ \Pr(Y_{ij}=1,Y_{ji}=1\mid\theta^{(s)}) = p_{ij}^{(s)}p_{ji}^{(s)} $$

Across posterior and dyad-effect draws, correlation in $T$ generates excess mutuality at the population level.

---

## 48. Posterior network construction

For each draw $s$, construct an $18\times18$ matrix of expected nominations:

$$ \mathbf P^{(s)} = \left[p_{ij}^{(s)}\right] $$

or expected counts:

$$ \boldsymbol\Lambda^{(s)} = \left[\lambda_{ij}^{(s)}\right] $$

Then set the diagonal to missing rather than interpreting it as an observed zero.

Network graphics can map:

- edge width to posterior expected nominations;
- node size to generalized receiving;
- edge color to direction;
- opacity to certainty.

---

## 49. Why a posterior mean graph is insufficient

A graph based on:

$$ \mathbb E[T_{ij}\mid D] $$

compresses the posterior into one matrix.

It hides:

- uncertainty about each edge;
- covariance between opposite directions;
- uncertainty in receiver effects;
- uncertainty in rank;
- alternative network configurations.

Useful supplements include:

- animations across posterior draws;
- edge compatibility intervals;
- posterior probabilities above a meaningful threshold;
- distributions of network summaries.

---

## 50. Prior predictive checks

Before fitting, simulate complete nomination networks.

Check:

- counts remain in the permitted range;
- outgoing totals match the design if the likelihood conditions on them;
- plausible number of unique nominees per monk;
- plausible concentration of incoming nominations;
- plausible number of mutual nominations;
- plausible repeated nominations across waves;
- plausible receiver and dyad heterogeneity.

For a Poisson model, explicitly count replications with:

$$ Y_{ij}^{\text{rep}}>3 $$

These are impossible under the observed design and diagnose likelihood mismatch.

---

## 51. Posterior predictive checks: margins

For every replicated network, compute outgoing totals:

$$ O_i^{\text{rep}} = \sum_{j\neq i}Y_{ij}^{\text{rep}} $$

and incoming totals:

$$ I_j^{\text{rep}} = \sum_{i\neq j}Y_{ij}^{\text{rep}} $$

If the model is conditional on observed outgoing totals, then:

$$ O_i^{\text{rep}}=m_i $$

by construction.

The model must still reproduce the observed distribution of $I_j$.

---

## 52. Posterior predictive checks: reciprocity

Useful reciprocity summaries include:

### Mutual positive dyads

$$ M_+ = \sum_{d=1}^{D} \mathbb I(Y_{AB,d}>0) \mathbb I(Y_{BA,d}>0) $$

### Cross-product

$$ C = \sum_{d=1}^{D} Y_{AB,d}Y_{BA,d} $$

### Absolute imbalance

$$ B = \sum_{d=1}^{D} |Y_{AB,d}-Y_{BA,d}| $$

Compare observed summaries with their posterior predictive distributions.

Parameter recovery alone does not guarantee realistic network reciprocity.

---

## 53. Posterior predictive checks: concentration

Popularity can be assessed through the incoming distribution.

Useful summaries include:

### Maximum indegree strength

$$ I_{\max} = \max_j I_j $$

### Incoming variance

$$ V_I = \operatorname{Var}(I_1,\ldots,I_N) $$

### Share received by the top monks

$$ S_k = \frac{ \sum_{j\in\operatorname{Top}(k)}I_j }{ \sum_jI_j } $$

These checks test whether the receiver hierarchy produces realistic concentration.

---

## 54. MCMC diagnostics

The model contains:

- 18 receiver effects;
- 306 directional dyad effects;
- at least two scale parameters;
- one correlation parameter;
- an intercept.

Check:

- divergent transitions;
- tree depth;
- rank-normalized $\widehat R$;
- bulk effective sample size;
- tail effective sample size;
- energy diagnostics;
- trace and rank plots for $\sigma_r$, $\sigma_T$, and $\rho_T$.

The high nominal parameter count is manageable because the hierarchy regularizes the latent effects.

---

## 55. Weak scale and unstable correlation

Correlation is hard to interpret when the dyad scale is near zero.

The covariance is:

$$ \operatorname{Cov} \left(T_{AB},T_{BA}\right) = \rho_T\sigma_T^2 $$

If:

$$ \sigma_T\approx0 $$

then even an extreme $\rho_T$ implies little practical covariance.

Always interpret $\rho_T$ jointly with $\sigma_T$ and posterior predictive mutuality.

---

## 56. Receiver–dyad competition

The model can explain a nomination using either:

- a high receiver effect $r_j$;
- a high dyad effect $T_{ij}$.

With only three waves, these components may compete strongly.

The hierarchy distinguishes them through repeated structure:

- $r_j$ affects all 17 potential senders to $j$;
- $T_{ij}$ affects one ordered dyad direction.

Broad incoming support identifies popularity; isolated repetition identifies affinity.

---

## 57. Leave-one-sender-out validation

To test generalized receiving, hold out all nominations made by one sender $i$.

Fit the model to the remaining senders and predict how $i$ allocates nominations.

The receiver effects remain estimable from other monks.

This validation target asks whether inferred popularity generalizes to a new observer within the same monastery.

It is more diagnostic of receiver effects than randomly holding out individual dyad cells.

---

## 58. Leave-one-dyad-out validation

Holding out both directions of dyad $d=(i,j)$ tests prediction for a new relation among known monks.

For the held-out dyad, draw:

$$ \mathbf T_d^{(s)} \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \mathbf0, \mathbf S_T^{(s)} \right) $$

while using posterior receiver effects $r_i$ and $r_j$.

This tests whether the actor population and dyad population predict unseen relationships.

---

## 59. Predicting one direction from the other

If A-to-B is observed but B-to-A is held out, the correlated dyad prior permits information flow.

For a bivariate Gaussian population:

$$ \mathbb E \left[ T_{BA} \mid T_{AB}=t \right] = \rho_T t $$

when both directions have the same scale and zero mean.

Thus positive reciprocity shrinks the unknown reverse effect toward the observed direction.

This is a direct example of correlated partial pooling from B03.

---

## 60. New-monk prediction

For a new monk $n$:

$$ r_n^{(s)} \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( 0, \sigma_r^{(s)} \right) $$

Every dyad involving the new monk also needs new correlated dyad effects.

Because generalized giving was conditioned away by the original survey design, prediction assumes the new monk is also constrained to the same number of outgoing nominations.

Changing the nomination protocol changes the prediction target and may require a giving model that the original data cannot identify.

---

## 61. The dislike network

The homework suggests applying the same architecture to dislike nominations.

Define:

$$ Y_{ij}^{(-)} = \text{number of periods monk }i\text{ disliked monk }j $$

Possible quantities include:

- generalized receiving of dislike;
- reciprocal antagonism;
- directional dyad asymmetry;
- overlap between liking and disliking structures.

Before reusing the same likelihood, verify the number of dislike nominations allowed by the design.

---

## 62. Joint like–dislike modeling

A larger model could assign each monk correlated receiving effects:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} r_j^{(+)}\\ r_j^{(-)} \end{bmatrix} \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \mathbf0, \mathbf S_r \right) $$

The correlation asks whether monks who are generally liked are also generally disliked.

Each dyad could also have a four-feature vector:

$$ \mathbf T_d = \begin{bmatrix} T_{AB}^{(+)}\\ T_{BA}^{(+)}\\ T_{AB}^{(-)}\\ T_{BA}^{(-)} \end{bmatrix} $$

This is a direct high-dimensional extension of B03, but it requires stronger regularization and careful interpretation.

---

## 63. Time structure hidden by aggregation

The counts aggregate three periods:

$$ Y_{ij+} = Y_{ij1}+Y_{ij2}+Y_{ij3} $$

Aggregation removes information about:

- when a nomination occurred;
- whether reciprocity was simultaneous or delayed;
- changes in factions;
- entry or exit;
- temporal persistence;
- reactions to previous nominations.

A count of 2 can arise from several temporal patterns with different social meanings.

---

## 64. A longitudinal extension

With wave-level data:

$$ Y_{ijt} \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_{ijt}) $$

One might model:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{ijt}) = \alpha_t + r_{jt} + T_{ij,t} $$

and dynamic evolution:

$$ T_{ij,t} = \phi_TT_{ij,t-1} + \epsilon_{ij,t} $$

This separates stable affinity from temporal shocks.

It also allows lagged reciprocity hypotheses, though causal interpretation remains difficult.

---

## 65. Transitivity and factions

The receiver–dyad model does not represent triadic closure.

If:

$$ A\rightarrow B $$

and:

$$ B\rightarrow C $$

the model does not automatically increase:

$$ A\rightarrow C $$

Unmodeled factions can generate apparent dyad reciprocity and popularity patterns.

Potential extensions include:

- stochastic block models;
- latent-space models;
- eigenmodels;
- explicit triadic statistics;
- community membership predictors.

---

## 66. Causal interpretation

The B05 model is primarily descriptive and predictive.

It estimates latent social structure under a generative observation model.

It does not by itself establish that:

- being liked causes status;
- popularity causes influence;
- one nomination causes the reciprocal nomination;
- a faction causes a relationship;
- a named monk would remain popular under intervention.

Network ties are selected, interdependent, and often affected by shared causes.

---

## 67. Measurement interpretation

An observed nomination is an imperfect measurement of a relationship.

It may depend on:

- private affect;
- memory;
- willingness to report;
- social desirability;
- current conflict;
- survey rules;
- the forced nomination limit.

The latent dyad effect should therefore be described as a model-based affinity or nomination tendency, not as direct access to true friendship.

This prepares for B07's measurement-model perspective.

---

## 68. Recommended B05 workflow

### Step 1: audit the design

Confirm number of waves, nomination quotas, possible receivers, and missingness.

### Step 2: reshape and validate dyads

Ensure every pair occurs once and both directions are consistently indexed.

### Step 3: define targets

Separate reciprocity, receiving heterogeneity, individual popularity, and dyad affinity.

### Step 4: select an observation model

Choose Poisson, binomial, multinomial, or choice-set likelihood based on the design and data resolution.

### Step 5: write the hierarchy

Use receiver effects and correlated directional dyad effects; omit unidentifiable giving effects.

### Step 6: simulate priors

Inspect complete networks and design-constrained summaries.

### Step 7: fit and diagnose

Check the covariance parameters and all standard HMC diagnostics.

### Step 8: simulate posterior networks

Validate margins, concentration, repeated ties, and reciprocity.

### Step 9: report popularity with uncertainty

Use posterior contrasts and rank probabilities rather than a fixed ranking.

### Step 10: state limitations

Identify quota effects, aggregation, omitted transitivity, and non-causal interpretation.

## 69. Common errors exposed by B05

### Error 1: Estimating unrestricted generalized giving from quota-constrained outgoing totals

The survey conditioned away its variation.

### Error 2: Treating quota-limited outdegree as latent sociability

The equality is imposed by design.

### Error 3: Treating all directed counts as independent

They share senders, receivers, dyads, and quota constraints.

### Error 4: Calling raw directional correlation reciprocity

It mixes popularity and dyad structure.

### Error 5: Giving the two arbitrary dyad positions different variances

A/B storage labels have no scientific meaning.

### Error 6: Using the sender's receiver effect

For A-to-B, generalized receiving belongs to B.

### Error 7: Ignoring the upper count bound

A Poisson model can generate values above 3.

### Error 8: Assuming a binomial model fully represents the quota

Candidate receivers compete for three slots within each wave.

### Error 9: Treating an aggregated multinomial as exact sampling without replacement

It can allocate more than three nominations to one receiver.

### Error 10: Using a zero-centered logit intercept prior without prior simulation

It implies an implausibly dense nomination network.

### Error 11: Interpreting high $r_j$ as a particular friendship

$r_j$ is generalized receiving across dyads.

### Error 12: Interpreting high $T_{ij}$ as general popularity

$T_{ij}$ is pair-specific.

### Error 13: Reporting only receiver ranks

Ranks can be highly uncertain.

### Error 14: Reading $\rho_T$ without $\sigma_T$

Correlation has little practical meaning when dyad variation is negligible.

### Error 15: Using the posterior mean network as if observed without error

It hides posterior uncertainty.

### Error 16: Confusing mutual nomination with causal reciprocity

Shared faction membership and other common causes can generate the pattern.

### Error 17: Ignoring temporal aggregation

Counts do not reveal when nominations occurred.

### Error 18: Claiming the model captures factions or transitivity

Receiver and dyad effects do not encode triadic dependence.

## 72. Compact model templates

### 72.1 Receiver-only binomial SRM

$$ Y_{AB,d} \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(3,p_{AB,d}) $$

$$ Y_{BA,d} \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(3,p_{BA,d}) $$

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{AB,d}) = \alpha+r_B+T_{d,AB} $$

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_{BA,d}) = \alpha+r_A+T_{d,BA} $$

### 72.2 Receiver population

$$ r_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,\sigma_r) $$

### 72.3 Reciprocal dyad population

$$ \begin{bmatrix} T_{d,AB}\\ T_{d,BA} \end{bmatrix} \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_T^2&\rho_T\sigma_T^2\\ \rho_T\sigma_T^2&\sigma_T^2 \end{bmatrix} \right) $$

### 72.4 Conditional allocation model

$$ \mathbf Y_i \sim \operatorname{Multinomial} \left( m_i, \boldsymbol\pi_i \right) $$

$$ \pi_{ij} = \frac{\exp(r_j+T_{ij})} {\sum_{k\neq i}\exp(r_k+T_{ik})} $$

### 72.5 Average-dyad popularity

$$ p_j^{\text{avg}} = \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\alpha+r_j) $$

## 73. Glossary

**Actor:** A node capable of sending or receiving a directed relation.

**Adjacency matrix:** A matrix whose off-diagonal entry $Y_{ij}$ records the directed relation from $i$ to $j$.

**Choice set:** The collection of possible receivers available to a sender.

**Conditional multinomial:** The distribution of independent Poisson counts after conditioning on their sum.

**Dyad:** An unordered pair of actors.

**Dyad effect:** A pair-specific directional deviation after actor roles are accounted for.

**Generalized giving:** An actor's tendency to send relations across partners; not cleanly identified here because outgoing totals are quota-constrained.

**Generalized receiving:** An actor's tendency to attract relations across partners.

**Indegree strength:** Total incoming nomination count.

**Nomination quota:** A design rule fixing how many peers each sender can nominate.

**Outdegree strength:** Total outgoing nomination count.

**Popularity:** The substantive interpretation of generalized receiving for like nominations.

**Reciprocity:** Population covariance between the two residual directions of a dyad.

**Receiver effect:** A partially pooled actor parameter shared across all incoming dyads.

**Relabeling invariance:** The requirement that arbitrary A/B storage order not alter inference.

**Social-relations model:** A model decomposing directed outcomes into sender, receiver, and dyad components.

**Transitivity:** Dependence in which two relations through a third actor increase the probability of a third relation.
