## 1. Main thesis

A conventional varying-intercept model pools discrete groups:

$$ \alpha_j \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\bar\alpha,\sigma_\alpha) $$

but treats every pair of different groups as equally unrelated.

A Gaussian process replaces that assumption with graded similarity:

> **Cases that are close according to a scientifically chosen distance are expected to have more similar latent effects than cases that are far apart.**

The central construction is:

$$ \mathbf f \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \mathbf m, \mathbf K \right) $$

where each covariance entry is computed by a kernel:

$$ K_{ij} = k(x_i,x_j) $$

The workflow is:

$$ \text{scientific similarity} \rightarrow \text{distance matrix} \rightarrow \text{kernel} \rightarrow \text{covariance matrix} \rightarrow \text{joint latent effects} \rightarrow \text{posterior function and prediction} $$

| Earlier idea | Gaussian-process extension |

| B05 dyad covariance | Dependence is encoded before observing outcomes |
| Partial pooling | Nearby inputs pool more strongly |
| New-group prediction | New-input prediction follows conditional multivariate Gaussian algebra |

The progression is:

$$ \text{exchangeable groups} \rightarrow \text{correlated group features} \rightarrow \text{continuous similarity} $$

A Gaussian process is therefore not disconnected machinery. It is multilevel modeling over a continuous category.

---

## 3. From discrete categories to continuous categories

For ordinary varying effects:

$$ \operatorname{Cov}(\alpha_i,\alpha_j) = \begin{cases} \sigma_\alpha^2&i=j\\ 0&i\neq j \end{cases} $$

The labels are unordered.

For locations, ages, dates, body sizes, or phylogenetic positions, similarity has structure.

We instead define:

$$ \operatorname{Cov}(f_i,f_j) = k(d_{ij}) $$

where $d_{ij}$ is a meaningful distance.

As distance increases, covariance usually declines.

---

## 4. A Gaussian process is a distribution over functions

Write:

$$ f(x) \sim \operatorname{GP} \left( m(x), k(x,x') \right) $$

This means that for every finite set of inputs:

$$ \mathbf x = (x_1,\ldots,x_N) $$

the corresponding function values satisfy:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} f(x_1)\\ \vdots\\ f(x_N) \end{bmatrix} \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \begin{bmatrix} m(x_1)\\ \vdots\\ m(x_N) \end{bmatrix}, \mathbf K \right) $$

with:

$$ K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j) $$

The apparently infinite-dimensional process becomes ordinary multivariate Gaussian algebra at observed and prediction points.

---

## 5. The mean function

The mean function is:

$$ m(x) = \mathbb E[f(x)] $$

Common choices include:

### Zero mean

$$ m(x)=0 $$

The GP represents deviations around zero or another intercept.

### Parametric mean

$$ m(x) = \alpha+\beta x $$

The GP represents departures from a trend.

### Scientific mean model

$$ m(x) = g(x;\boldsymbol\theta) $$

The GP absorbs structured residual variation around a mechanistic expectation.

The mean and kernel divide explanatory labor, so both must be interpreted together.

---

## 6. Distance matrices

For inputs $x_1,\ldots,x_N$, define:

$$ D_{ij} = d(x_i,x_j) $$

A valid distance matrix normally has:

$$ D_{ii}=0 $$

$$ D_{ij}=D_{ji} $$

and:

$$ D_{ij}\ge0 $$

Examples include:

- Euclidean geographic distance;
- absolute age difference;
- absolute log-population difference;
- time separation;
- phylogenetic branch distance;
- periodic angular distance;
- network or semantic distance.

The scientific meaning of proximity comes from the distance definition, not from the phrase *Gaussian process*.

---

## 7. The squared-exponential kernel

The lecture animation uses the form:

$$ k(d) = \alpha^2 \exp \left( -\frac{d^2}{2\rho^2} \right) $$

The Oceanic tools model uses an equivalent parameterization:

$$ k(d) = \eta^2 \exp \left( -\rho^2d^2 \right) $$

The symbols and interpretation of the range parameter differ between forms.

Never compare numerical values of $\rho$ across parameterizations without translating the kernel.

---

## 8. Amplitude

In:

$$ k(d) = \alpha^2 \exp \left( -\frac{d^2}{2\rho^2} \right) $$

the maximum covariance is:

$$ k(0)=\alpha^2 $$

$\alpha$ is the marginal standard deviation of the latent function.

Large $\alpha$ permits large vertical variation.

Small $\alpha$ shrinks the function toward its mean.

In the `cov_GPL2` parameterization, `etasq` directly represents the maximum covariance rather than its square root.

---

## 9. Length scale

In the animation parameterization, $\rho$ controls the horizontal scale of similarity.

### Large $\rho$

Covariance decays slowly, producing smoother, longer-range functions.

### Small $\rho$

Covariance decays quickly, allowing rapid local changes.

In the book's form:

$$ k(d)=\eta^2\exp(-\rho^2d^2) $$

larger $\rho^2$ means *faster* decay.

This opposite numerical direction is a common source of mistakes.

---

## 10. The nugget and observation noise

A covariance matrix often includes a diagonal term:

$$ K_{ij} = k(d_{ij}) + \delta_{ij}\sigma^2 $$

where:

$$ \delta_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1&i=j\\ 0&i\neq j \end{cases} $$

This term may represent:

- observation noise;
- independent residual variation;
- a numerical jitter when extremely small;
- within-location variation when replicates exist.

Scientific noise and numerical jitter must be named separately even if both appear on a diagonal.

---

## 11. Covariance versus correlation

Covariance depends on amplitude.

Correlation is:

$$ R_{ij} = \frac{K_{ij}} {\sqrt{K_{ii}K_{jj}}} $$

With equal marginal variance and no nugget:

$$ R_{ij} = \exp(-\rho^2d_{ij}^2) $$

The script scales covariance matrices for visualization, but scaled line opacity is not itself a posterior probability of connection.

## 13. Conditional Gaussian-process prediction

Partition latent values into observed and new inputs:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf f_1\\ \mathbf f_2 \end{bmatrix} \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol\mu_1\\ \boldsymbol\mu_2 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf K_{11}&\mathbf K_{12}\\ \mathbf K_{21}&\mathbf K_{22} \end{bmatrix} \right) $$

Then:

$$ \mathbf f_2\mid\mathbf y_1 \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \boldsymbol\mu_{2\mid1}, \mathbf K_{2\mid1} \right) $$

where:

$$ \boldsymbol\mu_{2\mid1} = \boldsymbol\mu_2 + \mathbf K_{21} \left( \mathbf K_{11}+\sigma^2\mathbf I \right)^{-1} \left( \mathbf y_1-\boldsymbol\mu_1 \right) $$

and:

$$ \mathbf K_{2\mid1} = \mathbf K_{22} - \mathbf K_{21} \left( \mathbf K_{11}+\sigma^2\mathbf I \right)^{-1} \mathbf K_{12} $$

---

## 14. What the conditional equations mean

The posterior mean is the prior mean plus a covariance-weighted correction from observed residuals.

The posterior covariance is the prior covariance minus information supplied by observations.

Therefore:

- predictions near data are updated strongly;
- predictions far from data revert toward the mean function;
- uncertainty falls near informative observations;
- observation noise limits interpolation;
- all prediction points remain jointly correlated.

This is partial pooling in function space.

---

## 15. Latent function versus predictive observation

The Stan programs generate both:

$$ f_2 $$

and:

$$ y_2 \sim \operatorname{Normal}(f_2,\sigma) $$

The uncertainty in $f_2$ describes the latent function.

The uncertainty in $y_2$ additionally includes observation noise.

Consequently:

$$ \operatorname{Var}(y_2\mid D) = \operatorname{Var}(f_2\mid D) + \sigma^2 $$

Compatibility bands for the function and predictive intervals for observations answer different questions.

---

## 16. Learning the kernel

The second animation estimates:

- $\alpha$;
- $\rho$;
- $\sigma$.

The data inform not just a curve, but a posterior distribution over possible covariance functions.

Each posterior draw contains:

$$ \left( \alpha^{(s)}, \rho^{(s)}, \sigma^{(s)} \right) $$

which generates its own kernel and conditional function.

Uncertainty about kernel hyperparameters should be propagated into predictions.

---

## 17. Regularization in the animation

When nearby observations disagree sharply, the model can explain the conflict using:

- a shorter length scale;
- a larger noise scale;
- a larger function amplitude;
- posterior compromise among these mechanisms.

This creates posterior dependence among hyperparameters.

Inspecting only marginal means of $\alpha$, $\rho$, and $\sigma$ can therefore obscure the learned family of functions.

Plot joint posterior kernel draws.

---

## 18. Prior predictive simulation for kernels

A kernel prior is best understood through sampled functions.

For each prior draw:

1. draw hyperparameters;
2. build $\mathbf K$;
3. draw $\mathbf f\sim\operatorname{MVNormal}(\mathbf m,\mathbf K)$;
4. optionally draw observations;
5. plot the resulting function and summaries.

Ask whether the prior permits:

- implausibly large variation;
- implausibly rapid oscillation;
- nearly constant functions only;
- unrealistic long-range dependence;
- numerical degeneracy.

---

## 19. L2 kernel

The script illustrates:

$$ k_{L2}(d) = \eta^2\exp(-\rho^2d^2) $$

This squared-distance kernel produces very smooth functions.

It is appropriate when the latent relationship is expected to vary continuously with no abrupt changes at the modeled scale.

Its smoothness is an assumption, not an empirical fact guaranteed by Gaussian processes.

---

## 20. L1 kernel

The script also illustrates:

$$ k_{L1}(d) = \eta^2\exp(-\rho d) $$

Covariance decays exponentially in unsquared distance.

This kernel produces rougher functions than the L2 kernel.

In the phylogenetic example, it corresponds to an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck-style covariance model.

The choice between L1 and L2 is a scientific choice about expected regularity.

---

## 21. Periodic kernel

The script sketches a periodic form:

$$ k_{\text{per}}(d) = \eta^2 \exp \left[ -c\sin^2(d/2) \right] $$

Points separated by a full period become similar again.

Applications include:

- season of year;
- time of day;
- orientation angles;
- cyclic biological rhythms.

Ordinary Euclidean distance would incorrectly treat the two ends of a cycle as far apart.

---

## 22. Stationarity and isotropy

A stationary kernel depends on displacement rather than absolute location.

An isotropic kernel depends only on scalar distance:

$$ k(x_i,x_j)=k(d_{ij}) $$

The L1 and L2 distance kernels used here are stationary and isotropic.

They assume the same covariance-decay rule everywhere and in every direction.

This can fail when:

- coastlines channel contact;
- barriers block exchange;
- evolutionary rates differ among clades;
- smoothness varies across predictor space;
- distance has direction-specific meaning.

---

## 23. Positive semidefiniteness

A proposed kernel must generate a valid covariance matrix:

$$ \mathbf v^\top\mathbf K\mathbf v \ge0 $$

for every vector $\mathbf v$.

An arbitrary decreasing function of distance is not automatically a valid kernel.

Standard kernels are useful partly because their covariance validity is known.

Adding kernels and multiplying kernels in valid ways can construct richer covariance structures.

---

## 24. Cholesky computation

Instead of directly inverting $\mathbf K$, computation uses:

$$ \mathbf K = \mathbf L\mathbf L^\top $$

where $\mathbf L$ is lower triangular.

The Stan prediction function:

- builds the observed covariance matrix;
- adds squared noise to its diagonal;
- computes the Cholesky factor;
- uses triangular solves;
- constructs the conditional mean and covariance;
- samples the latent prediction jointly.

This is more stable and efficient than explicit matrix inversion.

---

## 25. Numerical jitter

The Stan scripts add:

$$ \delta\mathbf I $$

with very small:

$$ \delta=10^{-9} $$

to a prediction covariance matrix.

The `cov_GPL1` and `cov_GPL2` calls often use `0.01` on the diagonal in teaching models.

These values play different possible roles.

A tiny jitter stabilizes Cholesky decomposition.

A larger diagonal value may materially alter independent residual variance and must not be dismissed as purely numerical.

---

## 26. Computational scaling

Exact Gaussian-process fitting requires Cholesky decomposition of an $N\times N$ matrix.

Its approximate computational cost is:

$$ O(N^3) $$

and memory cost is:

$$ O(N^2) $$

For ten islands or 151 primates, exact computation is feasible.

For tens of thousands of points, sparse, inducing-point, nearest-neighbor, basis-function, or structured methods may be needed.

## 29. Oceanic tools data

The first applied case uses ten Oceanic societies from `Kline2`.

The outcome is:

$$ T_i = \text{total number of tools in society }i $$

Important variables include:

- population size;
- log population;
- latitude and longitude;
- a geographic distance matrix.

Nearby societies may share tools, ecology, history, or unmeasured causes.

The observations are therefore plausibly dependent even after population is modeled.

## 31. Distance-only tools model

The lecture first fits:

$$ T_i \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_i) $$

$$ \log\lambda_i = \bar\alpha+a_i $$

with:

$$ \mathbf a \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \mathbf0, \mathbf K_D \right) $$

and:

$$ K_{D,ij} = \eta_D^2 \exp \left( -\rho_D^2D_{ij}^2 \right) + \delta_{ij}\epsilon $$

This model asks whether spatial similarity alone organizes tool-count deviations.

---

## 32. Centered spatial model

The centered form is:

```r
vector[10]:a ~ multi_normal(0, K)
```

It maps the population model directly onto the latent effects.

This form is readable, but sampling may become inefficient when the covariance amplitude is small or latent effects are weakly informed.

Posterior geometry can contain a multivariate version of the hierarchical funnel.

---

## 33. Non-centered spatial model

The script also constructs:

$$ \mathbf z \sim \operatorname{MVNormal}(\mathbf0,\mathbf I) $$

$$ \mathbf a = \mathbf L_K\mathbf z $$

where:

$$ \mathbf L_K = \operatorname{Cholesky}(\mathbf K) $$

In `ulam`:

```r
transpars> vector[10]:a <<- L_K*z
vector[10]:z ~ normal(0, 1)
transpars> matrix[10,10]:L_K <<- cholesky_decompose(K)
```

The centered and non-centered forms encode the same prior but different posterior geometry.

---

## 34. Prior covariance functions

The spatial model uses:

$$ \eta_D^2 \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(2) $$

$$ \rho_D^2 \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(0.5) $$

The script draws both jointly and plots:

$$ k_D(d) = \eta_D^2\exp(-\rho_D^2d^2) $$

This is more informative than separate prior density plots because amplitude and decay interact to define the actual covariance.

---

## 35. Posterior covariance functions

For posterior draw $s$:

$$ k_D^{(s)}(d) = \eta_D^{2(s)} \exp \left( -\rho_D^{2(s)}d^2 \right) $$

Plot many joint draws.

Do not construct a pseudo-kernel from separate marginal posterior means if the hyperparameters are correlated.

The uncertainty of a posterior mean kernel is not the same as the posterior distribution of kernels.

---

## 36. Spatial-association map

The script computes a posterior mean covariance matrix and draws lines between societies.

Line opacity increases with modeled spatial covariance.

This visualization shows which observations pool most strongly.

It does not show:

- a causal exchange path;
- a probability of contact;
- a transportation network;
- direct evidence of cultural transmission.

The lines visualize covariance implied by distance and posterior hyperparameters.

---

## 37. Scientific population–tools model

The full lecture model uses:

$$ T_i \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_i) $$

$$ \lambda_i = \left( \frac{\bar aP_i^b}{g} \right) \exp(a_i) $$

where:

- $P_i$ is population;
- $b$ governs population scaling;
- $g$ is a loss or saturation parameter in the scientific model;
- $a_i$ is a spatial GP deviation.

The GP does not replace scientific structure. It models residual similarity around it.

---

## 38. Interpretation on the count scale

The latent spatial effect is additive on the log scale:

$$ \log\lambda_i = \log\lambda_i^{\text{science}} + a_i $$

Therefore it is multiplicative on the count scale:

$$ \lambda_i = \lambda_i^{\text{science}} \exp(a_i) $$

If:

$$ a_i=0.3 $$

then the spatial adjustment multiplies the expected tool count by:

$$ e^{0.3} $$

relative to the scientific mean component.

---

## 39. Spatial covariance is not a mechanism

Nearby societies may resemble one another because of:

- exchange;
- shared ancestry;
- similar ecology;
- common raw materials;
- measurement traditions;
- regional shocks;
- omitted predictors.

The GP describes residual covariance compatible with all these explanations.

It does not identify which mechanism generated the covariance.

---

## 40. Spatial confounding

Population itself may be spatially patterned.

A flexible spatial GP can compete with the population predictor for the same variation.

This may:

- appropriately adjust for spatially structured omitted causes;
- attenuate a confounded population association;
- absorb part of a true population signal;
- reveal weak identification between mean and covariance components.

The DAG and intervention target must determine how the population coefficient is interpreted.

Predictive performance alone cannot resolve spatial confounding.

---

## 41. Extrapolation

Inside the observed input range, nearby data guide the GP.

Far outside that range:

$$ k(x_*,x_i)\rightarrow0 $$

and predictions revert toward the mean function.

A zero-mean GP therefore tends toward zero latent deviation outside supported regions.

This is not learned scientific asymptotic behavior.

If extrapolation matters, encode plausible structure in the mean function rather than relying on the kernel alone.

---

## 42. B06 homework estimand

The homework asks what relationship a Gaussian process finds between log population and tool counts.

Let:

$$ x_i = \log P_i $$

The target is a flexible latent function:

$$ f_P(x) $$

that contributes to the expected tool count without imposing:

$$ f_P(x)=\beta x $$

The comparison is functional:

$$ \text{GP population relationship} \quad\text{versus}\quad \text{log-linear population relationship} $$

---

## 43. Constructing the log-population distance matrix

For societies $i$ and $j$:

$$ D^P_{ij} = |x_i-x_j| = |\log P_i-\log P_j| $$

In R:

```r
lp <- log(d$population)
DP <- abs(outer(lp, lp, "-"))
```

Check:

$$ D^P_{ii}=0 $$

and:

$$ D^P_{ij}=D^P_{ji} $$

The row and column order must match the outcome data exactly.

---

## 44. Normalizing population distance

The homework permits:

$$ \widetilde D^P = \frac{D^P}{\max(D^P)} $$

Then:

$$ 0\le\widetilde D^P_{ij}\le1 $$

Normalization does not add information.

It changes the numerical interpretation of the decay prior and can make prior calibration easier.

Every prediction distance must use the same scaling constant learned from the training design.

---

## 45. Homework model with two Gaussian processes

One natural additive model is:

$$ T_i \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_i) $$

$$ \log\lambda_i = \bar\alpha + a_i^{D} + a_i^{P} $$

Spatial effects:

$$ \mathbf a^D \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \mathbf0, \mathbf K_D \right) $$

Population-similarity effects:

$$ \mathbf a^P \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \mathbf0, \mathbf K_P \right) $$

---

## 46. Two kernels

For geographic distance:

$$ K_{D,ij} = \eta_D^2 \exp \left( -\rho_D^2(D^D_{ij})^2 \right) $$

For log-population distance:

$$ K_{P,ij} = \eta_P^2 \exp \left( -\rho_P^2(D^P_{ij})^2 \right) $$

Each GP needs its own amplitude and decay parameters.

Sharing them would assert that spatial and population similarity have identical scales after numerical standardization.

---

## 47. Why population must not appear directly

The homework explicitly requires that population appear only through $\mathbf D^P$.

Do not include:

$$ \beta_P\log P_i $$

in the same homework model.

Otherwise the GP would describe deviations around an imposed linear trend rather than discover the full flexible relationship requested.

The log-population values are still needed for:

- building distances;
- ordering predictions;
- plotting the recovered function.

They are not a separate linear predictor term.

---

## 48. Equivalent summed-kernel view

If:

$$ \mathbf a^D \perp \mathbf a^P $$

and both are Gaussian, then:

$$ \mathbf a = \mathbf a^D+\mathbf a^P $$

has covariance:

$$ \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf a) = \mathbf K_D+\mathbf K_P $$

A model can therefore be expressed as:

$$ \mathbf a \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \mathbf0, \mathbf K_D+\mathbf K_P \right) $$

Separate latent vectors are more convenient when the homework requires plotting the population component alone.

---


## 50. Identifiability of additive GPs

With only ten societies, two flexible latent functions can compete.

Problems arise when geographic similarity and population similarity are themselves correlated.

Then:

$$ \mathbf K_D $$

and:

$$ \mathbf K_P $$

may explain similar outcome patterns.

Consequences include:

- strong posterior tradeoffs in amplitudes;
- weakly identified length scales;
- broad component functions;
- stable total predictions but unstable decomposition.

Regularizing priors and joint posterior plots are essential.

---

## 51. Centering additive components

The intercept and GP components can compete for a constant shift.

The zero-mean GP prior provides population-level centering, but a finite posterior draw may still have a nonzero sample mean across the ten inputs.

For plotting a population component, one may summarize contrasts such as:

$$ f_P(x)-f_P(x_{\text{ref}}) $$

or center each draw over observed inputs:

$$ \widetilde a_i^{P(s)} = a_i^{P(s)} - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N a_j^{P(s)} $$

State the centering convention.

---

## 52. Reconstructing the population relationship

At observed societies, order posterior population effects by:

$$ x_i=\log P_i $$

For posterior draw $s$, a partial expected count curve is:

$$ \lambda_P^{(s)}(x_i) = \exp \left( \bar\alpha^{(s)}+a_i^{P(s)} \right) $$

with spatial effect held at zero.

Alternatively plot $a_i^P$ on the log-count scale.

Do not connect posterior means without uncertainty and call the result the posterior function.

---

## 53. Predicting the population function on a grid

Create new log-population values:

$$ x_1^*,\ldots,x_M^* $$

Compute:

$$ D_{*o,mi}^P = |x_m^*-x_i| $$

and:

$$ D_{**,mn}^P = |x_m^*-x_n^*| $$

using the same normalization.

Then apply the conditional GP formulas draw by draw using posterior $\eta_P^2$ and $\rho_P^2$.

This yields a jointly coherent posterior curve rather than independent pointwise smoothing.

---

## 54. Compare with the log-linear trend

The earlier model implies:

$$ \log\lambda(x) = c+bx $$

or, on the original population scale:

$$ \lambda(P) \propto P^b $$

Compare this curve with posterior draws of the GP population function.

Ask:

- Is the GP approximately linear on the log-count scale?
- Is there evidence of a threshold or plateau?
- Is apparent curvature supported away from individual islands?
- How wide is uncertainty between sparse population values?
- Do conclusions depend on the spatial GP?

---

## 55. Comparison is not only predictive ranking

PSIS or WAIC may compare predictive adequacy, but the scientific comparison concerns assumptions.

The log-linear model assumes a global functional form with few parameters.

The GP assumes local smoothness and reversion to a mean outside support.

With ten observations, the flexible model may have wide uncertainty even if its posterior mean appears curved.

A narrow linear trend can be more extrapolative but only because it makes a stronger assumption.

- behavior of Hawaii and other influential societies;

- sensitivity to a gamma-Poisson likelihood.

Component plots are not substitutes for outcome-scale predictive checks.

## 58. Transition to phylogenetic distance

Geographic distance measures separation in space.

Phylogenetic distance measures separation through evolutionary history.

Closely related species may share:

- inherited traits;
- developmental constraints;
- unmeasured ecological adaptations;
- life-history features;
- common evolutionary shocks.

A phylogenetic covariance model uses tree distance to represent this expected dependence.

---

## 59. Primate causal question

The script studies associations among:

- brain size $B$;
- body mass $M$;
- group size $G$.

A proposed effect is:

$$ G\rightarrow B $$

Body mass may confound this relationship:

$$ G\leftarrow M\rightarrow B $$

Unobserved evolutionary variables may also affect all three.

Phylogenetic structure is used as a proxy for patterns among some unobserved causes, not as a magical causal adjustment.

---

## 60. Ordinary regression as a multivariate model

Standardized logged brain sizes form a vector:

$$ \mathbf B \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \boldsymbol\mu, \sigma^2\mathbf I \right) $$

with:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+\beta_MM_i+\beta_GG_i $$

The identity matrix asserts zero residual correlation among species.

Ordinary independent-error regression is therefore a special covariance model.

Phylogenetic regression replaces $\mathbf I$ with structured dependence.

---

## 61. Brownian-motion phylogenetic covariance

Under Brownian trait evolution, covariance depends on shared branch history.

The `ape` package supplies:

```r
Rbm <- corBrownian(phy=tree_trimmed)
V <- vcv(Rbm)
Dmat <- cophenetic(tree_trimmed)
```

The regression becomes:

$$ \mathbf B \sim \operatorname{MVNormal} \left( \boldsymbol\mu, \sigma^2\mathbf R_{BM} \right) $$

The covariance structure is fixed by the tree and Brownian model, while the overall residual scale is estimated.

---

## 62. Brownian assumptions

Brownian motion assumes trait differences accumulate through Gaussian random drift along branches.

This implies a rigid relationship between shared ancestry and covariance.

It may fail because:

- selection stabilizes traits;
- evolutionary rates differ among clades;
- traits jump;
- regimes change;
- the tree is uncertain;
- the relevant confounds do not follow Brownian dynamics.

Calling a model phylogenetically controlled does not make its evolutionary assumptions correct.

---

## 63. Ornstein–Uhlenbeck kernel

The lecture fits an L1 kernel:

$$ K_{ij} = \eta^2 \exp(-\rho D_{ij}) + \delta_{ij}\epsilon $$

This is associated with an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, often described as damped Brownian motion with tendency toward a mean.

Unlike the fixed Brownian covariance, it learns amplitude and decay from the data.

The resulting phylogenetic adjustment can therefore differ sharply from Brownian regression.

---

## 64. Three phylogenetic mean models

The script fits:

### Phylogeny only

$$ \mu_i=\alpha $$

### Phylogeny plus body mass

$$ \mu_i=\alpha+\beta_MM_i $$

### Phylogeny plus body mass and group size

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+\beta_MM_i+\beta_GG_i $$

Comparing their covariance functions shows how mean predictors and residual phylogenetic covariance divide the observed structure.

## 65. Row-order alignment

The phylogenetic matrix must be reordered to match the data:

```r
V <- V[spp_obs, spp_obs]
Dmat <- Dmat[spp_obs, spp_obs]
```

Without this step, the model assigns one species another species' covariance relationships.

The matrix remains symmetric and numerically valid, so sampling diagnostics may not reveal the error.

Name-based row and column assertions are essential.

---

## 66. Normalizing phylogenetic distance

The script uses:

$$ \widetilde D = \frac{D}{\max(D)} $$

This maps observed phylogenetic distances to $[0,1]$.

The decay prior then has an interpretable reference range.

Normalization does not remove phylogenetic uncertainty and does not make different trees equivalent.

---

## 67. Comparing phylogenetic models

The ordinary, Brownian, and OU models can give different posteriors for $\beta_G$.

This difference is not a nuisance to hide.

It reveals dependence of the scientific conclusion on assumptions about trait covariance through evolutionary distance.

Compare:

- posterior covariance functions;
- posterior predictive trait patterns on the tree;
- coefficient posteriors;
- prior sensitivity;
- residual phylogenetic structure;
- scientific plausibility of each evolutionary model.

---

## 68. Prediction under phylogenetic covariance

Prediction for a new species requires:

- its position in the phylogeny;
- distances or shared branches to observed species;
- predictor values for the mean function;
- covariance between the new and observed species;
- its marginal covariance.

The same conditional Gaussian equations used for one-dimensional functions apply.

A new species with no covariance to observed species reverts toward the mean model with population-level uncertainty.

Check:

- divergent transitions;
- maximum tree depth;
- rank-normalized $\widehat R$;
- bulk and tail effective sample sizes;
- energy diagnostics;
- trace and rank plots;
- condition of covariance matrices;
- amplitude–decay posterior dependence;
- centered versus non-centered performance.

GP models can produce apparently smooth plots even when hyperparameters mix poorly.

Never diagnose them from the plotted posterior mean function alone.

## 70. Common errors exposed by B06

### Error 1: Treating the GP as a black-box curve

It is a structured multivariate prior defined by a mean and kernel.

### Error 2: Using distance without scientific justification

The kernel only knows the similarity measure supplied to it.

### Error 3: Confusing amplitude and length scale

One controls vertical variation; the other controls covariance decay.

### Error 4: Forgetting that rho parameterizations reverse direction

Large $\rho$ may mean longer or shorter range depending on the formula.

### Error 5: Calling numerical jitter observation noise

Their roles and magnitudes differ.

### Error 6: Plotting marginal mean hyperparameters as one representative kernel

Posterior dependence can make that combination unrepresentative.

### Error 7: Confusing latent-function intervals with predictive intervals

Prediction adds observation variation.

### Error 8: Assuming an L2 kernel permits abrupt changes

It strongly favors smooth functions.

### Error 9: Treating spatial covariance as evidence of contact

Many common causes produce the same pattern.

### Error 10: Omitting row-order checks

A permuted distance matrix can silently invalidate inference.

### Error 11: Using normalized distances with unadjusted priors

Scaling changes decay-parameter meaning.

### Error 12: Adding population directly in the B06 homework model

The assignment requires population to enter only through its distance matrix.

### Error 13: Using one kernel's hyperparameters for both homework GPs

Geographic and population similarity need distinct scales.

### Error 14: Ignoring competition between additive GPs

Their component functions may be weakly identified.

### Error 15: Plotting observed latent effects as a continuous function without conditional prediction

A coherent curve requires the cross-covariance with new inputs.

### Error 16: Extrapolating GP curvature far beyond observed population values

The function reverts toward its mean away from support.

### Error 17: Calling phylogenetic regression automatic deconfounding

It only helps under an adequate model of evolutionary covariance.

### Error 18: Treating Brownian motion as the uniquely correct phylogenetic model

It is one restrictive generative assumption.

## 71. Recommended B06 workflow

### Step 1: define the estimand or prediction target

State whether the goal is interpolation, residual adjustment, functional discovery, or causal estimation.

### Step 2: define similarity scientifically

Construct and validate the distance matrix.

### Step 3: choose a mean function

Decide which scientific structure belongs outside the GP.

### Step 4: choose a kernel

Encode expected smoothness, periodicity, directionality, and range.

### Step 5: calibrate priors through functions

Simulate hyperparameters, kernels, latent functions, and observations.

### Step 6: fit with stable parameterization

Use Cholesky computation and consider non-centering.

### Step 7: diagnose hyperparameters and geometry

Inspect joint amplitude–decay behavior and HMC diagnostics.

### Step 8: validate relational summaries

Check residual patterns across distance, not only marginal outcomes.

### Step 9: predict jointly

Use cross-covariance matrices for new inputs.

### Step 10: report assumptions

Separate descriptive covariance, predictive interpolation, and causal interpretation.

## 75. Glossary

**Amplitude:** Marginal scale of latent function variation.

**Brownian motion:** Gaussian random-walk model used to derive one rigid form of phylogenetic covariance.

**Conditional GP:** Distribution of function values at new inputs after conditioning on observations.

**Covariance function:** Another name for a kernel when emphasizing the covariance it generates.

**Distance matrix:** Pairwise dissimilarities among observed inputs.

**Gaussian process:** A distribution over functions whose finite collections are multivariate Gaussian.

**Hyperparameter:** A kernel parameter such as amplitude or length scale.

**Inducing point:** A reduced representation used in scalable approximate GPs; not used in the official B06 examples.

**Isotropy:** Covariance depends only on scalar distance, not direction.

**Jitter:** A small diagonal value added for numerical stability.

**Kernel:** A function mapping pairs of inputs or their distance to covariance.

**L1 kernel:** Exponential covariance decay in unsquared distance.

**L2 kernel:** Exponential covariance decay in squared distance.

**Length scale:** Parameter controlling the distance over which latent effects remain similar.

**Mean function:** Prior expected function value at each input.

**Nugget:** Independent diagonal covariance, often representing measurement or micro-scale variation.

**Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process:** Mean-reverting process associated here with exponential phylogenetic covariance.

**Periodic kernel:** Kernel that restores similarity after a fixed cycle.

**Positive semidefinite:** Matrix property required of valid covariance matrices.

**Spatial autocorrelation:** Residual similarity associated with geographic proximity.

**Stationarity:** Covariance depends on separation rather than absolute input position.
