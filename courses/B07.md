## 1. Main thesis

An observed value is often not the scientific variable of interest.

Instead:

$$ \text{scientific process} \rightarrow \text{latent true state} \rightarrow \text{measurement process} \rightarrow \text{observed data} $$

The central lesson is:

> **Measurement is part of the generative model. If a reported value is uncertain, use the distribution of that uncertainty instead of collapsing it to a supposedly exact number.**

A measurement model allows information to flow in both directions:

$$ \text{measurement precision} \rightarrow \text{latent state} \rightarrow \text{scientific relationship} $$

and:

$$ \text{scientific relationship} \rightarrow \text{updated latent state} $$

This mutual updating produces principled shrinkage.

---

## 2. Relationship to earlier lectures

| Earlier concept | Measurement-model extension |
|---|---|
| Parameters are uncertain | Some data values are also uncertain |
| Multilevel partial pooling | Latent true measurements pool through a scientific model |
| Generative simulation | Simulate truth first, then its measurement |
| DAGs | Add latent variables and measurement-error nodes |
| Missing data | An uncertain observation lies between fully observed and fully missing |
| Posterior prediction | Simulate both latent states and reported observations |

The hierarchy is:

$$ \text{latent state model} + \text{observation model} = \text{measurement model} $$

---

## 3. Data as distributions

Suppose a reported estimate is:

$$ y_{obs}=10 $$

with known standard error:

$$ s=2 $$

Treating 10 as exact discards the supplied uncertainty.

A generative observation model is:

$$ y_{obs} \sim \mathrm{Normal}(y_{true},s) $$

The unknown center:

$$ y_{true} $$

becomes a latent parameter informed by both the observed value and the rest of the model.

---

## 4. Two linked submodels

A basic measurement-error regression contains:

### Scientific model

$$ y_{true,i} \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma) $$

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+\beta x_i $$

### Observation model

$$ y_{obs,i} \sim \mathrm{Normal}(y_{true,i},s_i) $$

The latent value appears in both submodels.

That shared role lets the scientific trend inform uncertain measurements and lets precise measurements inform the trend.

---

## 5. Measurement DAG

Let:

- $X$ be a true predictor;
- $Y$ be a true outcome;
- $X_{obs}$ and $Y_{obs}$ be measurements;
- $e_X$ and $e_Y$ be measurement processes.

A simple DAG contains:

$$ X\rightarrow Y $$

$$ X\rightarrow X_{obs}\leftarrow e_X $$

$$ Y\rightarrow Y_{obs}\leftarrow e_Y $$

The scientific and observation processes are distinct.

Using $X_{obs}$ and $Y_{obs}$ as though they were $X$ and $Y$ changes the model.

---

## 6. Known versus unknown measurement error

The simplest examples treat each measurement standard deviation:

$$ s_i $$

as known.

Often it is itself estimated.

Then possible models include:

$$ \widehat s_i \sim p(\widehat s_i\mid s_i,n_i) $$

with a prior on $s_i$.

The course models condition on reported standard errors or standard deviations.

This is appropriate only when their remaining uncertainty is negligible for the purpose at hand.

---

## 7. Heterogeneous precision

When measurement errors differ by case:

$$ y_{obs,i} \sim \mathrm{Normal}(y_{true,i},s_i) $$

precise cases have smaller $s_i$ and exert more influence.

Uncertain cases have larger $s_i$ and are updated more strongly by the scientific model.

This resembles inverse-variance weighting but goes further:

- latent values are estimated;
- regression uncertainty is propagated back to them;
- nonlinear models are supported;
- uncertainty enters posterior predictions coherently.

---

## 8. Shrinkage of measured values

For uncertain case $i$, the posterior estimate balances:

$$ y_{obs,i} $$

against the model expectation:

$$ \mu_i $$

Schematically:

$$ \mathbb E[y_{true,i}\mid D] \approx w_i y_{obs,i} + (1-w_i)\mu_i $$

where $w_i$ increases with measurement precision.

This is not arbitrary smoothing. It follows from the joint probability model.

---

## 9. Limiting cases

If:

$$ s_i\rightarrow0 $$

then:

$$ y_{true,i}\rightarrow y_{obs,i} $$

and the measurement model approaches ordinary regression.

If:

$$ s_i\rightarrow\infty $$

then the observation supplies little information and the latent value is estimated mainly from its scientific model and prior.

These limits provide useful implementation checks.

---

## 10. Outcome error is not always harmless

In a simple homoscedastic linear regression with independent classical outcome error, slopes may remain unbiased while residual variance increases.

But this comforting result does not generalize automatically.

Bias can arise when:

- error sizes vary with predictors;
- errors are correlated;
- selection depends on precision;
- the likelihood is nonlinear;
- latent outcomes are bounded or discrete;
- measurement error depends on the true state;
- errors share unobserved causes.

The measurement process must be modeled, not dismissed with a slogan.

---

## 11. Predictor error is not just extra residual noise

If a predictor is measured with error:

$$ x_{obs,i} \sim \mathrm{Normal}(x_{true,i},s_{x,i}) $$

then uncertainty is horizontal relative to a regression plot.

Ignoring it often attenuates a simple linear slope under classical assumptions.

But differential, heteroscedastic, Berkson, correlated, or outcome-dependent error can amplify, reverse, or create associations.

No universal attenuation correction exists without a generative measurement model.

---

## 12. Parent–child recall-bias simulation

The script simulates true parental income:

$$ P_i \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0,1) $$

and child income:

$$ C_i \sim \mathrm{Normal}(0.75P_i,1) $$

The reported parental income is:

$$ P_i^* \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( 0.8P_i+0.2C_i, 1 \right) $$

The measurement depends partly on the outcome.

This is recall bias, not classical independent noise.

---

## 13. Why recall bias distorts the slope

The proxy $P^*$ is caused by both:

$$ P $$

and:

$$ C $$

Regressing $C$ on $P^*$ therefore uses a predictor containing outcome information.

The observed association no longer isolates the causal or structural path from parental to child income.

Adding an ordinary residual term to the regression cannot repair the direction of the measurement process.

## 14. WaffleDivorce observation uncertainty

The lecture revisits the state-level variables:

- divorce rate $D$;
- marriage rate $M$;
- median age at marriage $A$.

Both divorce and marriage rates have reported standard errors that vary among states.

Smaller populations tend to produce less precise estimates.

The uncertainty is therefore associated with substantive case characteristics rather than constant across rows.

---

## 15. Outcome-error divorce model

The observation model is:

$$ D_{obs,i} \sim \mathrm{Normal}(D_{true,i},D_{sd,i}) $$

The scientific model is:

$$ D_{true,i} \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\mu_i,\sigma) $$

$$ \mu_i = \alpha + \beta_AA_i + \beta_MM_i $$

The posterior estimates all state-specific true divorce rates jointly with the regression.

---

## 16. `m15.1`

The official implementation is:

```r
m15.1 <- ulam(
  alist(
    D_obs ~ dnorm(D_true, D_sd),
    vector[N]:D_true ~ dnorm(mu, sigma),
    mu <- a + bA*A + bM*M,
    a ~ dnorm(0, 0.2),
    bA ~ dnorm(0, 0.5),
    bM ~ dnorm(0, 0.5),
    sigma ~ dexp(1)
  ), data=dlist, chains=4, cores=4
)
```

The first line is the measurement model.

The remaining lines model the latent scientific outcome.

---

## 17. Mutual updating in `m15.1`

For a state with large $D_{sd,i}$:

- its observation weakly anchors $D_{true,i}$;
- the regression trend influences $D_{true,i}$ strongly.

For a state with small $D_{sd,i}$:

- its observation tightly anchors $D_{true,i}$;
- it strongly influences the regression trend.

All latent values and regression parameters update simultaneously.

This is why post-hoc weighting is not equivalent to the full model.

---

## 18. Comparing with a no-error model

The no-error model substitutes:

$$ D_{obs,i} $$

directly for the scientific outcome.

Compare:

- coefficient posteriors;
- residual scale;
- posterior latent outcomes;
- regression lines;
- predictive distributions;
- case-specific shrinkage;
- calibration under simulation.

The book example demonstrates that incorporating error can weaken an association. It need not always do so.

---

## 19. Error on both outcome and predictor

Add:

$$ M_{obs,i} \sim \mathrm{Normal}(M_{true,i},M_{sd,i}) $$

and use:

$$ \mu_i = \alpha+\beta_AA_i+\beta_MM_{true,i} $$

The latent predictor also needs a population model:

$$ M_{true,i} \sim p(M_{true,i}\mid A_i,\boldsymbol\phi) $$

Without that model, noisy predictor values can be poorly identified.

---

## 20. `m15.2`

The script uses:

$$ M_{true,i} \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( \alpha_M+\beta_{AM}A_i, \tau \right) $$

This respects the causal model in which age at marriage helps generate marriage rate.

The joint structure is:

$$ A \rightarrow M_{true} \rightarrow D_{true} $$

and:

$$ A\rightarrow D_{true} $$

The predictor's generative model is not optional decoration; it contributes identification and scientific meaning.

---

## 21. Shrinkage in two dimensions

When both $M$ and $D$ are uncertain, each state has a posterior cloud over:

$$ (M_{true,i},D_{true,i}) $$

Posterior means move relative to observed points in both directions.

The movement depends on:

- horizontal measurement precision;
- vertical measurement precision;
- the regression relationship;
- the population model for $M$;
- priors and other predictors.

Plotting only the fitted line hides this joint update.

---

## 22. Latent-variable identification

The observation equation alone:

$$ x_{obs,i} \sim \mathrm{Normal}(x_{true,i},s_i) $$

does not provide a complete population model for $x_{true,i}$.

Identification improves through:

- known measurement precision;
- replicated measurements;
- validation subsamples;
- scientifically informed latent distributions;
- predictors of the latent state;
- multiple indicators;
- informative priors.

More latent parameters do not create information by themselves.

---

## 23. Correlated measurement errors

Suppose:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} e_{X,i}\\ e_{Y,i} \end{bmatrix} \sim \mathrm{MVNormal} \left( \mathbf0, \mathbf S_e \right) $$

with:

$$ \mathrm{Cov}(e_X,e_Y)\neq0 $$

Independent observation models are then misspecified.

A shared interviewer, instrument, batch, preservation condition, or reporting incentive can generate correlated error.

The error covariance must be modeled when relevant and identifiable.

---

## 24. Differential measurement error

Measurement quality can depend on:

- the true exposure;
- the true outcome;
- treatment assignment;
- group membership;
- sample size;
- preservation;
- disease status;
- observer expectations.

Then:

$$ p(y_{obs}\mid y_{true}) $$

must include those causes.

Assuming independent zero-mean error can be worse than omitting an error term if it creates false confidence in an incorrect observation process.

---

## 25. Classical and Berkson error

### Classical error

$$ X_{obs}=X_{true}+e $$

The observed value fluctuates around truth.

### Berkson error

$$ X_{true}=X_{assigned}+e $$

The true exposure fluctuates around an assigned or group value.

These models have different likelihoods and regression consequences.

The direction of the observation equation matters.

---

## 26. Systematic bias

An unbiased Gaussian observation model assumes:

$$ \mathbb E[y_{obs}\mid y_{true}]=y_{true} $$

With systematic calibration bias:

$$ y_{obs} \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( \gamma_0+\gamma_1y_{true}, s \right) $$

Estimating both $\gamma$ parameters and all latent truths requires anchors such as gold-standard measurements or informative calibration priors.

Otherwise scale and location may be unidentified.

---

## 27. Discrete misclassification

For a latent binary state:

$$ X\in\{0,1\} $$

the observed classification is:

$$ X^*\in\{0,1\} $$

Define sensitivity:

$$ Se = \Pr(X^*=1\mid X=1) $$

and specificity:

$$ Sp = \Pr(X^*=0\mid X=0) $$

The false-positive rate is:

$$ f=1-Sp $$

and false-negative rate is:

$$ q=1-Se $$

---

## 28. Marginal observed-positive probability

If:

$$ p_i = \Pr(X_i=1) $$

then:

$$ \Pr(X_i^*=1) = Se\,p_i + (1-Sp)(1-p_i) $$

With perfect sensitivity and known false-positive rate $f$:

$$ \Pr(X_i^*=1) = p_i+(1-p_i)f $$

This is the form used in the script's paternity-classification example.

---

## 29. Observed-negative probability

The general probability is:

$$ \Pr(X_i^*=0) = (1-Se)p_i + Sp(1-p_i) $$

With perfect sensitivity:

$$ \Pr(X_i^*=0) = (1-p_i)(1-f) $$

The likelihood averages over the unobserved true state rather than sampling a discrete latent parameter in Stan.

This marginalization is exact and computationally efficient.

---

## 30. Multilevel misclassification example

The latent probability model is:

$$ \mathrm{logit}(p_i) = \alpha + \sigma z_{mother[i]} + \tau x_{dyad[i]} $$

The hierarchy describes variation among mothers and dyads.

The observation model then converts $p_i$ to the probability of a reported positive under false positives.

Ignoring misclassification can inflate the inferred base rate, especially when the true event is rare.

---

## 31. Numerically stable mixture likelihoods

The positive observation probability is a sum:

$$ p+(1-p)f $$

On the log scale, calculate:

$$ \log \left[ \exp(\log p) + \exp(\log(1-p)+\log f) \right] $$

using `log_sum_exp`.

Use stable functions such as:

- `log1m(p)`;
- `log1m_exp(log_p)`;
- `log_inv_logit(eta)`.

Numerical stability is part of model correctness for extreme probabilities.

---

## 32. Repeated indicators

Multiple measurements of the same latent state provide leverage:

$$ y_{ir} \sim p(y_{ir}\mid\theta_i,\boldsymbol\phi_r) $$

where $r$ indexes instruments or observers.

Replicates can help separate:

- stable individual state;
- instrument bias;
- observer variation;
- occasion noise.

But repeated measurements with the same systematic bias do not automatically reveal truth.

---

## 33. Family-structure age-estimation idea

The age-estimation sketch simulates:

- maternal age at first birth;
- interbirth intervals;
- mother's current age;
- children's current ages;
- age-dependent observation error.

Family constraints relate latent ages:

$$ A_{mother} - A_{child,k} = \text{maternal age at birth }k $$

Information can flow across relatives to improve uncertain age estimates.

This is a structured measurement model rather than independent correction of each age.

---

## 34. Age-dependent error

The sketch simulates error proportional to age:

$$ A_{obs} \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( A_{true}, A_{true}/10 \right) $$

This is heteroscedastic measurement error.

Older individuals have more uncertain observed ages.

Using one constant error scale would discard that generative feature.

## 38. Homework outcome model

For specimen $i$:

$$ L_i \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(S_i,p_i) $$

where:

- $L_i$ is `num_amtl`;
- $S_i$ is `sockets`;
- $p_i$ is the probability that an observable socket corresponds to ante-mortem tooth loss.

The number of sockets is the binomial exposure or denominator.

Do not regress the raw fraction alone and discard denominator information.

---

## 39. Naive age model

The model without measurement error treats estimated age as exact:

$$ \mathrm{logit}(p_i) = \alpha+f(A_{obs,i}) $$

For a linear function:

$$ f(A_{obs,i}) = \beta_AA_{obs,i} $$

This is the comparison baseline requested by the homework.

It is not the preferred scientific model because it ignores `stdev_age`.

---

## 40. Measurement-error age model

Introduce one latent true age per specimen:

$$ A_{obs,i} \sim \mathrm{Normal} \left( A_{true,i}, s_{A,i} \right) $$

where:

$$ s_{A,i}=\texttt{stdev\_age}_i $$

Then use latent age in the outcome model:

$$ \mathrm{logit}(p_i) = \alpha+f(A_{true,i}) $$

The observed age never replaces the latent scientific predictor.

---

## 41. Population model for true ages

Latent ages need a plausible distribution:

$$ A_{true,i} \sim p(A_{true,i}\mid\boldsymbol\phi) $$

A simple truncated Gaussian is:

$$ A_{true,i} \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\mu_A,\sigma_A), \qquad A_{true,i}>0 $$

Alternatives include:

- log-normal age;
- gamma age;
- mixture distributions;
- site- or period-specific age populations;
- an empirically informed mortality distribution.

The latent-age prior can materially influence specimens with large age uncertainty.

---

## 42. Standardized homework model

Define:

$$ A_{obs,i}^{z} = \frac{A_{obs,i}-\bar A}{s_A^{sample}} $$

and transform measurement uncertainty consistently:

$$ s_{obs,i}^{z} = \frac{s_{obs,i}}{s_A^{sample}} $$

Then:

$$ A_{obs,i}^{z} \sim \mathrm{Normal}(A_{true,i}^{z},s_{obs,i}^{z}) $$

Forgetting to scale the standard deviations while scaling age changes the measurement model and can severely distort inference.

---

## 43. Homework `ulam` template

dlist <- list(

## 45. Is a linear age function adequate?

A linear log-odds model assumes:

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial A} \mathrm{logit}(p(A)) = \beta_A $$

at every age.

Dental loss may accelerate, plateau, or show thresholds.

Possible alternatives include:

- polynomial terms with regularizing priors;
- splines;
- monotonic effects;
- Gaussian processes;
- mechanistic cumulative-hazard models.

The measurement-error comparison should first hold the function class fixed, then assess functional-form sensitivity separately.

---

## 46. Monotonic age model

If scientific knowledge implies that cumulative tooth-loss probability should not decline with true age, impose monotonicity.

For ordered age intervals:

$$ f(A) = \sum_{k\le A}\delta_k $$

with:

$$ \delta_k\ge0 $$

This prevents implausible downward oscillations in sparse age regions.

But monotonicity concerns the population relationship, not each individual's exact dental history.

---

## 47. Binomial independence assumption

The binomial likelihood assumes exchangeability of sockets conditional on $p_i$.

Teeth within a specimen may be correlated because of:

- shared disease;
- diet;
- cultural practice;
- jaw region;
- preservation;
- individual frailty.

Extra-binomial variation can be modeled with:

- beta-binomial likelihood;
- specimen-level latent effects when lower-level data exist;
- tooth-position predictors;
- zero-inflated or mixture structures if scientifically justified.

---

## 48. Why sockets matter

Two specimens can have the same fraction lost but different information.

For example:

$$ 1/2 $$

and:

$$ 16/32 $$

have equal proportions but different binomial precision.

The likelihood retains this distinction through $S_i$.

Regressing proportions with constant Gaussian variance discards it.

---

## 49. Age uncertainty and outcome information

The posterior true age is informed by:

- the reported age estimate;
- `stdev_age`;
- the latent age population;
- the specimen's AMTL count;
- the learned age–AMTL relationship.

Thus a specimen with unexpectedly extensive tooth loss may shift toward an older latent age under the model.

That is valid only if the scientific model permits dental state to inform age and if circularity with the original age-estimation method is excluded.

---

## 50. Avoiding circular measurement

If the reported age estimate was itself derived partly from dental loss, then:

$$ A_{obs} $$

and:

$$ L $$

share measurement information.

The simple DAG assuming independent age measurement error is wrong.

One must determine which skeletal indicators generated `age` and `stdev_age`.

Otherwise the model can count the same dental evidence twice and exaggerate the age relationship.

---

## 51. Comparing the two homework models

Compare posterior distributions of:

- age slope or function;
- predicted AMTL probability by true age;
- latent ages;
- uncertainty bands;
- posterior predictive counts;
- residual overdispersion;
- influential specimens;
- calibration in simulation.

Do not compare only posterior means.

Measurement error may change the magnitude, shape, and uncertainty of the relationship.

## 52. Outcome-scale comparison

For posterior draw $s$ and true age $a$:

$$ p^{(s)}(a) = \mathrm{logit}^{-1} \left( \alpha^{(s)}+f^{(s)}(a) \right) $$

Plot:

- posterior median or mean curve;
- compatibility interval for latent socket-loss probability;
- predictive intervals for new specimen counts at specified socket totals.

A coefficient comparison alone is insufficient in a nonlinear model.

---

## 53. Prediction for uncertain age

For a new specimen with uncertain age distribution:

$$ A_{new,true}^{(s)} \sim p(A_{true}\mid A_{new,obs},s_{new},\boldsymbol\phi^{(s)}) $$

Then:

$$ p_{new}^{(s)} = \mathrm{logit}^{-1} \left( \alpha^{(s)}+f^{(s)}(A_{new,true}^{(s)}) \right) $$

and:

$$ L_{new}^{(s)} \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(S_{new},p_{new}^{(s)}) $$

This propagates age, parameter, and outcome uncertainty.

## 55. Posterior predictive checks for B07

Useful checks include:

### Zero counts

$$ T_0 = \sum_i\mathbb I(L_i=0) $$

### Near-complete loss

$$ T_{high} = \sum_i \mathbb I(L_i/S_i>c) $$

### Age-binned averages

Compare observed and replicated AMTL fractions across age intervals.

### Dispersion

Compare variance in counts or proportions conditional on sockets and age.

### Extreme specimens

Check whether the model reproduces young specimens with large losses and old specimens with little loss.

## 56. Sensitivity to reported age uncertainty

Refit under:

$$ s_{A,i}^{*}=c\,s_{A,i} $$

for plausible values of $c$.

Examples include:

$$ c\in\{0.5,1,2\} $$

This tests reliance on the reported uncertainty calibration.

It is not permission to select the multiplier that produces a preferred scientific conclusion.

---

## 57. Selection and preservation

The observed archaeological specimens are not a random sample of all past individuals.

Selection may depend on:

- survival to older age;
- burial practice;
- excavation;
- skeletal preservation;
- number of observable sockets;
- social status;
- site and period.

Measurement modeling of age does not repair selection bias.

The observation process may need a broader causal model.

---

## 58. Care-for-the-elderly interpretation

The homework notes a historical argument that advanced age in burials indicates care for elders.

An age–AMTL relationship alone does not identify care.

Possible alternative causes include:

- individual health and frailty;
- diet and dental disease;
- social inequality;
- preservation differences;
- mortality selection;
- age-estimation procedures;
- cultural treatment of the dead.

The measurement model improves the age association but does not transform it into a causal estimate of care.

---

## 59. Calibration data

The strongest measurement models use observations with known truth or a superior reference method.

Calibration data can identify:

- bias;
- heteroscedasticity;
- sensitivity and specificity;
- observer variation;
- age-dependent error;
- correlations among errors.

Without calibration, many error mechanisms are only weakly distinguishable from latent-state variation.

## 60. Simulation-based validation

Before trusting the B07 model:

1. simulate true ages from a plausible population;
2. simulate AMTL counts from a known function;
3. simulate age estimates using observed-like standard deviations;
4. fit naive and measurement-error models;
5. assess recovery of the age function;
6. repeat under biased or correlated errors;
7. quantify interval coverage and predictive calibration.

This reveals when the proposed model succeeds and when its assumptions fail.

---

## 61. MCMC diagnostics

The measurement-error model adds one latent age per specimen.

Check:

- divergent transitions;
- maximum tree depth;
- rank-normalized $\widehat R$;
- bulk and tail effective sample sizes;
- energy diagnostics;
- trace and rank plots;
- posterior correlation between age slope and latent-age scale;
- extreme latent ages;
- centered versus non-centered alternatives when applicable.

Good diagnostics do not validate the measurement assumptions, but poor diagnostics invalidate computation.

---

## 62. Identifiability diagnostics

Warning signs include:

- latent ages tracking their priors almost entirely;
- strong prior sensitivity;
- divergent amplitude or scale parameters;
- implausibly extreme corrected ages;
- near-perfect tradeoffs between slope and latent spread;
- very different component estimates with stable predictions;
- inability to recover simulated truth.

The solution is not automatically more iterations.

Additional information or a simpler model may be required.

## 63. Recommended B07 workflow

### Step 1: identify the latent scientific variable

Distinguish it from the reported measurement.

### Step 2: investigate how the measurement was produced

Ask about instruments, observers, calibration, censoring, and dependence.

### Step 3: draw the measurement DAG

Include causes of both truth and error.

### Step 4: write the scientific model

Define the relationship of substantive interest using latent variables.

### Step 5: write the observation model

Connect latent variables to observed data on the correct scale.

### Step 6: specify latent-state populations

Use scientific constraints and predictors.

### Step 7: simulate priors and measurements

Check both latent and observed scales.

### Step 8: fit and diagnose

Inspect computation and identification.

### Step 9: compare with the naive model

Hold unrelated assumptions constant.

### Step 10: report remaining uncertainty

Separate measurement correction from causal identification.

## 64. Common errors exposed by B07

### Error 1: Treating a reported estimate as an exact observation

Its standard error is part of the data.

### Error 2: Adding measurement variance to the wrong model layer

Observation error is distinct from scientific residual variation.

### Error 3: Correcting the outcome but not using latent values in the predictor

The scientific equation must reference truth, not its proxy.

### Error 4: Giving latent predictors no population model

Measurement equations alone may not identify them.

### Error 5: Assuming all measurement error attenuates slopes

That result requires restrictive classical conditions.

### Error 6: Ignoring outcome-dependent recall bias

It can create or amplify association.

### Error 7: Assuming errors are independent

Shared observers or instruments can correlate them.

### Error 8: Confusing classical and Berkson error

They reverse the generative direction.

### Error 9: Treating sensitivity and specificity as interchangeable

They govern different classification events.

### Error 10: Sampling discrete true states unnecessarily in Stan

Marginalize finite latent categories when possible.

### Error 11: Computing mixture probabilities naively on the probability scale

Extreme values can underflow; use stable log operations.

### Error 12: Standardizing estimates but not their standard deviations

This changes the implied precision.

### Error 13: Regressing AMTL proportions without socket counts

It discards denominator information.

### Error 14: Treating tooth sockets as conditionally independent without checking

Within-specimen dependence can cause overdispersion.

### Error 15: Using AMTL to update age when AMTL helped create the original age estimate

This double-counts evidence.

### Error 16: Comparing different age functions and calling the difference measurement error

Hold functional assumptions constant for the primary comparison.

### Error 17: Reporting only the slope

Compare probability-scale curves and predictions.

### Error 18: Claiming age correction identifies elder care

Selection, preservation, and alternative mechanisms remain.

### Error 19: Running the incomplete age-estimation Stan sketch as supplied

It is unfinished and references undefined objects.

### Error 20: Assuming a converged sampler validates the measurement process

Computation and scientific adequacy are separate.

## 68. Glossary

**Berkson error:** True exposure varies around an assigned or grouped exposure value.

**Calibration:** Use of reference observations to learn measurement bias and precision.

**Classical error:** Observed measurement varies around the latent true value.

**Differential error:** Measurement behavior depends on substantive variables or true states.

**False negative:** Observed negative when the latent state is positive.

**False positive:** Observed positive when the latent state is negative.

**Heteroscedastic error:** Measurement variance differs across cases or true values.

**Latent state:** Unobserved scientific variable inferred through observations and a population model.

**Measurement model:** Probability model connecting a latent state to its observed measurement.

**Misclassification:** Discrete measurement error assigning an observation to the wrong category.

**Observation model:** Model for how observed values arise from latent scientific states.

**Recall bias:** Reporting error affected by memory and potentially by current outcomes or beliefs.

**Sensitivity:** Probability of an observed positive given a true positive.

**Specificity:** Probability of an observed negative given a true negative.

**Standard error:** Estimated standard deviation of an estimator's sampling distribution.

**True value:** Latent value defined by the scientific and measurement model, not metaphysical error-free reality.
