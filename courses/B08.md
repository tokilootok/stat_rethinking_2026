## 1. Main thesis

Missingness and censoring are observations about the observation process.

A blank cell does not say that a scientific quantity does not exist. A right-censored duration does not say that the duration equals the censoring time. Both tell us that our access to the latent quantity is incomplete.

The central lesson is:

> **Do not replace incomplete observations with convenient numbers or silently discard them. Model what was observed, what was not observed, and why.**

For missing data, the generative sequence is often:

$$ X_i \longrightarrow Y_i, \qquad (X_i,Y_i,Z_i) \longrightarrow R_i, $$

where $R_i$ indicates whether a value is recorded.

For survival data:

$$ T_i \longrightarrow (t_i,\delta_i), $$

where $T_i$ is the latent event time, $t_i$ is the observed follow-up time, and $\delta_i$ reports whether the event occurred.

The likelihood must match the information in $(t_i,\delta_i)$.

---

## 2. Relationship to B07

B07 treated uncertain measurements as noisy observations of latent true states. B08 moves farther along the same continuum.

| Observation state | What is known | Appropriate model contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Exact | A value is treated as observed | Ordinary likelihood density or mass |
| Noisy | An estimate and uncertainty are observed | Measurement model |
| Missing continuous value | The value is unknown | Latent parameter plus predictive model |
| Missing discrete value | One of finitely many states is possible | Marginalize over possible states |
| Right-censored duration | Event time exceeds follow-up time | Survival probability beyond the boundary |
| Interval-censored duration | Event occurred inside an interval | Probability mass inside the interval |

Thus:

$$ \text{measurement error} \quad\text{and}\quad \text{missingness} $$

are not separate worlds. They are different observation models for latent scientific quantities.

---

## 3. Three quantities that must not be confused

Let $Y_i$ be a potentially missing variable.

Define:

$$ R_i= \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if }Y_i\text{ is observed},\\ 0 & \text{if }Y_i\text{ is missing}. \end{cases} $$

Then distinguish:

1. the latent scientific value $Y_i$;
2. the recording indicator $R_i$;
3. the observed data object $Y_i^{obs}$.

Conceptually:

$$ Y_i^{obs}= \begin{cases} Y_i & R_i=1,\\ \text{NA} & R_i=0. \end{cases} $$

`NA` is not a numerical value and should not be given a scientific interpretation.

---

## 4. Missingness has causes

Every missing value has a history:

- an animal removed the record;
- a participant skipped a sensitive question;
- a sensor failed in extreme weather;
- a fossil trait could not be reconstructed;
- a patient left the study;
- a database join failed;
- follow-up ended before an event.

Those causes determine whether the observed sample represents the target process.

The first analysis question is therefore not:

> Which imputation algorithm should I use?

It is:

> What process made this value unavailable, and which variables influenced that process?

---

## 5. The dog-and-homework thought experiment

The lecture uses a memorable data-removal mechanism.

Let:

- $S$ be a predictor, such as study effort;
- $H$ be homework quality;
- $D=1$ mean that the dog eats the homework.

The scientific model may be:

$$ H_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\alpha+\beta S_i,\sigma). $$

But $H_i$ is observed only when $D_i=0$.

Different causal structures for $D$ produce different consequences. The missing values may be harmless, inefficient, damaging, or fundamentally unidentified from the observed data alone.

---

## 6. Scenario A: random removal

Suppose:

$$ D_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(q) $$

independently of $S_i$ and $H_i$.

The observation mechanism contains no information about homework quality or study effort. A complete-case regression can remain unbiased under the correctly specified model, although it loses information and widens uncertainty.

This is the traditional **missing completely at random** case.

The important distinction is:

$$ \text{little bias} \neq \text{no cost}. $$

Discarding observations still reduces precision and may discard information in variables that remain observed.

---

## 7. Scenario B: removal depends on an observed predictor

Suppose the dog eats homework when study effort is high:

$$ S \longrightarrow D, \qquad S \longrightarrow H. $$

Conditional on $S$, missingness need not depend further on $H$. This resembles the traditional **missing at random** condition.

If the outcome model correctly conditions on $S$, the slope may be recoverable.

But this reassuring statement hides two requirements:

- the relevant cause $S$ is actually observed and included;
- enough outcomes remain observed across the relevant values of $S$.

Conditional independence alone does not guarantee useful information.

---

## 8. Positivity and support

Imagine every homework outcome with $S>0$ is missing.

Then the data contain no observed $H$ values in that region. A fitted straight line can extrapolate there, but this is a consequence of the assumed function, not direct evidence.

Formally, identification needs overlap such as:

$$ 0 < \Pr(R=1\mid S=s) < 1 $$

over the target region.

Without overlap:

- flexible nonlinear effects are weakly identified;
- posterior predictions depend heavily on priors and functional form;
- narrow intervals can be misleading if the model is overconfident about extrapolation.

Always plot the observed and missing cases against important predictors.

---

## 9. Scenario C: a latent common cause

Suppose an unobserved variable $X$ affects both homework and whether it is eaten:

$$ X \longrightarrow H, \qquad X \longrightarrow D. $$

Conditioning on the surviving homework selects cases through $D$. The distribution of observed $H$ can differ systematically from the target distribution.

This is not solved by assigning the missing values the observed mean. The missing cases may have a different distribution precisely because of $X$.

A DAG reveals the possible dependency, but it does not determine the sign or size of bias. Those depend on the functional relationships and parameter values.

---

## 10. Scenario D: removal depends on the missing value

Suppose the dog preferentially eats bad homework:

$$ H \longrightarrow D. $$

Then the probability of observing $H$ depends on $H$ itself:

$$ \Pr(R=1\mid H). $$

This is the classic **missing not at random** case.

The observed values alone usually cannot reveal the unseen tail. Identification requires additional information:

- a defensible model of selection;
- validation data;
- repeated measurements;
- an instrument or design feature;
- informative prior knowledge;
- sensitivity analysis.

Imputation software cannot manufacture the missing identifying information.

---

## 11. MCAR, MAR, and MNAR are shorthand

The traditional labels can be useful summaries:

| Label | Informal condition |
|---|---|
| MCAR | Missingness is unrelated to measured or unmeasured values |
| MAR | Missingness may depend on observed values, but not further on the missing value after conditioning |
| MNAR | Missingness still depends on unobserved values |

But the labels are not complete scientific explanations.

Prefer:

1. a causal graph;
2. an explicit model for the scientific variables;
3. an explicit statement of which variables are observed;
4. an overlap assessment;
5. sensitivity analyses for unverifiable assumptions.

The graph and generative story say more than the acronym.

---

## 12. Why complete-case analysis can fail

A complete-case analysis keeps only rows with all needed variables observed.

This may:

- waste outcome information when only a covariate is missing;
- waste covariate information when only the outcome is missing;
- change the composition of the analyzed sample;
- induce selection bias;
- reduce variation in predictors;
- amplify extrapolation;
- ignore phylogenetic, temporal, or group structure that predicts missing values.

Complete cases are not automatically a random subsample.

They are a sample selected by:

$$ R_1=R_2=\cdots=1. $$

That selection event must be scientifically considered.

---

## 13. Why deterministic imputation can fail

Common shortcuts include replacing missing values with:

- zero;
- the mean;
- a group mean;
- the last observation;
- a single regression prediction.

These create artificial certainty.

If all missing values are set to the mean, their imputed variance is zero. Relationships involving the imputed variable are attenuated or distorted, and conventional standard errors ignore uncertainty about the replacements.

Bayesian imputation instead represents each missing value with a posterior distribution.

---

## 14. Imputation is a model, not a preprocessing step

Suppose predictor $X_i$ is missing for some rows and the scientific model is:

$$ Y_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\alpha+\beta X_i,\sigma_Y). $$

Add a model for $X$:

$$ X_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu_X,\sigma_X). $$

For a missing $X_i$, the posterior is informed by:

- the population model for $X$;
- observed group or phylogenetic structure;
- the corresponding outcome $Y_i$;
- the scientific regression linking $X_i$ to $Y_i$;
- prior information.

The missing value is estimated jointly with every other unknown.

---

## 15. Posterior imputation is mutual updating

For an observed outcome and missing predictor:

$$ p(X_i^{mis}\mid Y_i,\theta) \propto p(Y_i\mid X_i^{mis},\theta_Y) p(X_i^{mis}\mid\theta_X). $$

The outcome informs the plausible predictor value, and the plausible predictor value informs the regression.

This is not circular reasoning. It is ordinary conditioning in a joint probability model.

The joint model is:

$$ p(Y,X\mid\theta) = p(Y\mid X,\theta_Y) p(X\mid\theta_X). $$

---

## 16. A continuous-imputation template

In `ulam`, continuous missing values can often be represented automatically when an input vector contains `NA` values and the variable also has a model.

Conceptually:

```r
m <- ulam(
  alist(
    Y ~ normal(mu, sigma),
    mu <- a + b * X,
    X ~ normal(mu_X, sigma_X),
    a ~ normal(0, 1),
    b ~ normal(0, 0.5),
    sigma ~ exponential(1),
    mu_X ~ normal(0, 1),
    sigma_X ~ exponential(1)
  ),
  data = dat,
  chains = 4,
  cores = 4,
  sample = TRUE
)
```

The crucial line is the model for `X`. An `NA` does not acquire a meaningful posterior merely because it was declared as a parameter; it needs a generative distribution and connections to observed data.

---

## 17. What the imputation model assumes

The preceding template assumes:

- a Normal marginal distribution for $X$;
- exchangeability unless more structure is included;
- a linear relationship between $X$ and $Y$;
- an observation mechanism that can be ignored conditional on modeled information.

If $X$ is skewed, bounded, multimodal, or group-structured, use a suitable model.

Examples include:

$$ \log X_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\mu,sigma), $$

$$ X_i \sim \operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,eta), $$

or:

$$ X_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\alpha_{g[i]},\sigma_X). $$

An imputation model should be at least as rich as the analysis relationships it must preserve.

---

## 18. Include predictive auxiliary variables

An auxiliary variable may not be part of the final scientific estimand but may predict:

- the missing value;
- the probability that it is missing;
- both.

Including such a variable in the joint model can make the conditional ignorability assumption more credible and improve precision.

But do not condition mechanically on post-treatment variables or colliders. Auxiliary-variable selection is still a causal modeling decision.

---

## 19. Model the missingness indicator when necessary

When missingness plausibly depends on latent values, introduce a selection model:

$$ R_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(\pi_i), $$

$$ \operatorname{logit}(\pi_i) = \gamma_0+\gamma_X X_i+\gamma_Y Y_i. $$

If $Y_i$ is missing, $\gamma_Y$ is often weakly identified because the relevant $Y_i$ values are unseen.

The purpose of such a model is not to pretend the problem is solved. It creates a transparent location for substantive assumptions and sensitivity priors.

Fit several plausible values or priors for the unidentified selection effect and report how the scientific conclusion moves.

---

## 20. Pattern-mixture perspective

An alternative factorization models the outcome distribution by missingness pattern:

$$ p(Y,R)=p(Y\mid R)p(R). $$

One can posit that missing outcomes differ from observed outcomes by a sensitivity parameter $\Delta$:

$$ E(Y\mid R=0,X) = E(Y\mid R=1,X)+\Delta. $$

Observed data may not identify $\Delta$, but domain knowledge can bound it.

Selection models and pattern-mixture models emphasize the same truth: MNAR conclusions require assumptions about values that were not observed.

## 21. The primate example

The lecture analyzes `Primates301`, with variables such as:

- brain size $B$;
- body mass $M$;
- group size $G$;
- species identity and phylogenetic distance.

After log transformation and standardization, the scientific question is represented by:

$$ B_i \sim \operatorname{MVNormal}(\mu,K_B), $$

$$ \mu_i=\alpha+\beta_M M_i+\beta_G G_i. $$

Some values of $B$, $M$, and $G$ are absent. A complete-case analysis uses only species for which all three are recorded.

---

## 22. Why phylogeny helps imputation

Species are not exchangeable independent rows. Closely related species may resemble one another.

The script constructs a phylogenetic distance matrix $D$ and an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck-style covariance:

$$ K_{ij} = \eta^2\exp(-\rho D_{ij}) + \delta_{ij}\epsilon. $$

Here:

- $\eta^2$ controls covariance magnitude;
- $\rho$ controls decay with phylogenetic distance;
- $\epsilon$ is a small diagonal stabilizer.

The resulting imputation draws information from scientifically relevant neighbors rather than only from a global mean.

---

## 23. First primate imputation model

The initial joint model contains:

$$ B \sim \operatorname{MVNormal}(\alpha+\beta_M M+\beta_G G,K_B), $$

$$ M_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,1), $$

$$ G_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(0,1). $$

Rows with an observed brain value can contribute to the brain regression even if $M$ or $G$ is missing, because those predictors become latent parameters.

This model is better than fixed mean imputation, but its independent standard-Normal models for $M$ and $G$ omit known relationships that can improve prediction.

---

## 24. Model relationships among predictors

The next extension models group size as related to body mass:

$$ G_i \sim \operatorname{Normal}(\nu_i,\sigma_G), $$

$$ \nu_i=\alpha_G+\beta_{MG}M_i. $$

Now a missing group size is informed by:

- the observed or imputed body mass;
- the brain-size equation;
- the population distribution of group size.

This preserves the joint associations among variables rather than imputing each predictor independently.

---

## 25. Add phylogenetic structure to predictors

The lecture then gives $G$ its own phylogenetic covariance:

$$ G \sim \operatorname{MVNormal}(\nu,K_G). $$

In the most complete Stan version, body mass also receives a phylogenetic model:

$$ M \sim \operatorname{MVNormal}(0,K_M). $$

The joint model is therefore:

$$ M \rightarrow G \rightarrow B, \qquad M \rightarrow B, $$

with separate phylogenetic covariance structures for $M$, $G$, and $B$.

This is not merely a device for filling cells. It is a generative model of comparative biology.

## 26. The full Stan strategy

`18_BMG_OU.stan` explicitly declares parameter vectors for missing values:

```stan
vector[N_B_miss] B_impute;
vector[N_M_miss] M_impute;
vector[N_G_miss] G_impute;
```

The data contain:

- vectors with sentinel values at missing positions;
- counts of missing values;
- integer arrays locating those positions;
- the phylogenetic distance matrix.

A helper function merges observed and imputed elements into complete latent vectors. These merged vectors then enter all likelihood components.

The sentinel, such as `-99`, is only a transport device. It is never interpreted as a biological observation.

---

## 27. Missing outcomes can also be imputed

When $B_i$ is missing, its posterior prediction follows from:

- $M_i$ and $G_i$;
- regression coefficients;
- phylogenetic covariance;
- observations from other species.

But missing outcomes do not directly provide new evidence about the regression coefficients. They are posterior predictions conditional on the model and observed data.

This distinction prevents a common misunderstanding:

> Imputing an absent outcome does not create an additional observed outcome.

It propagates uncertainty and permits coherent joint prediction.

---

## 28. Ordering is part of the data contract

The rows of $B$, $M$, $G$, and the rows and columns of the phylogenetic distance matrix must refer to the same species in the same order.

The lecture code explicitly reorders:

```r
Dmat <- Dmat[spp_all, spp_all]
```

A misaligned covariance matrix may still have valid dimensions and allow sampling, while representing the wrong biological model.

Always verify:

```r
identical(rownames(Dmat), spp_all)
identical(colnames(Dmat), spp_all)
```

Structural data checks belong before inference.

## 29. Compare complete-case and joint models

The script compares posterior distributions for:

- $\beta_G$, the association of group size with brain size;
- $\beta_M$, the association of body mass with brain size;
- $\beta_{MG}$, the association of body mass with group size;
- covariance-kernel parameters for all three traits.

A difference between models is not automatically proof that one is correct.

Ask:

- Which rows re-entered the analysis?
- Which submodel predicts their absent values?
- How much is learned from phylogeny?
- How sensitive are coefficients to priors and covariance structure?
- Does the imputation model reproduce observed distributions and relationships?

## 30. Diagnose continuous imputations

For each imputed variable:

1. plot posterior means and intervals at missing positions;
2. compare their distribution with observed values;
3. stratify by relevant groups or missingness patterns;
4. inspect relationships with predictors;
5. simulate replicated missing and observed values;
6. check whether estimates are driven by extrapolation;
7. conduct prior predictive checks.

Very narrow imputation intervals can be suspicious when data support is weak. Very wide intervals may be the honest result.

Do not judge an imputation by whether its posterior mean “looks reasonable” alone.

---

## 31. Missing discrete values require marginalization

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo samples continuous parameters. A missing binary or categorical value cannot generally be declared as a discrete Stan parameter.

Suppose $X_i\in\{0,1\}$ is missing. Its likelihood contribution is:

$$ p(y_i\mid\theta) = \sum_{x\in\{0,1\}} p(y_i\mid x,\theta) p(x\mid\theta). $$

For $K$ categories:

$$ p(y_i\mid\theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} p(y_i\mid X_i=k,\theta) p(X_i=k\mid\theta). $$

This integrates the missing discrete state out of the posterior.

---

## 32. Stable log-scale marginalization

On the log scale:

$$ \log p(y_i\mid\theta) = \log\sum_k \exp\left[ \log p(y_i\mid X_i=k,\theta) + \log p(X_i=k\mid\theta) \right]. $$

Use `log_sum_exp` rather than exponentiating small log probabilities manually.

For two alternatives:

```stan
target += log_sum_exp(
  log_p_x0 + log_lik_x0,
  log_p_x1 + log_lik_x1
);
```

Marginalization often improves sampling because the sampler explores one smooth continuous posterior rather than an imagined discrete parameter space.

---

## 33. Censoring is not ordinary missingness

If a duration is right-censored at $c_i$, the event time is not wholly unknown. We know:

$$ T_i>c_i. $$

That inequality is information.

| Situation | Observation |
|---|---|
| Missing event time | $T_i$ is unknown |
| Right censoring | $T_i>c_i$ |
| Left censoring | $T_i\le c_i$ |
| Interval censoring | $L_i<T_i\le U_i$ |

Replacing a right-censored time with $c_i$ falsely declares that the event occurred at the censoring boundary.

Dropping it discards the evidence that the individual survived event-free until $c_i$.

---

## 34. Event and censoring notation

Let:

- $T_i$ be the latent event time;
- $C_i$ be the censoring time;
- $t_i=\min(T_i,C_i)$ be observed time;
- $\delta_i=I(T_i\le C_i)$ be the event indicator.

For a continuous event-time density $f(t)$ and survival function:

$$ S(t)=\Pr(T>t), $$

the likelihood contribution is:

$$ L_i = f(t_i)^{\delta_i} S(t_i)^{1-\delta_i}. $$

The log likelihood is:

$$ \ell_i = \delta_i\log f(t_i) + (1-\delta_i)\log S(t_i). $$

---

## 35. Density, survival, and hazard

The hazard is the instantaneous event rate among individuals still at risk:

$$ h(t)=\frac{f(t)}{S(t)}. $$

The cumulative hazard is:

$$ H(t)=\int_0^t h(u)\,du, $$

and:

$$ S(t)=\exp[-H(t)]. $$

These are different summaries:

- $f(t)$ describes event-time density;
- $S(t)$ describes remaining event-free;
- $h(t)$ describes current event intensity among those still at risk.

An effect on hazard is not automatically the same as an effect on cumulative event probability at a chosen horizon.

---

## 36. Exponential waiting-time example

The lecture simulates:

$$ T_i \sim \operatorname{Exponential}(\lambda) $$

and right-censors at time 5.

For an observed event:

$$ f(t_i\mid\lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t_i}. $$

For a censored observation:

$$ S(5\mid\lambda) = e^{-5\lambda}. $$

The censored subject contributes evidence favoring event-time distributions that commonly extend beyond 5.

---

## 37. Why dropping censored observations biases the rate

If only event times below 5 are retained, long waits are systematically removed.

The observed-event subset is drawn from:

$$ p(T\mid T\le5), $$

not from the original exponential distribution $p(T)$.

The retained waits are shorter, so a naive model tends to infer an event rate that is too high.

This is selection by the outcome:

$$ T \longrightarrow I(T\le5). $$

The censor-aware likelihood repairs the observation model, provided the censoring assumptions are credible.

---

## 38. Marginalized exponential model

The lecture uses:

```r
m2 <- ulam(
  alist(
    y | k == 0 ~ exponential(lambda),
    y | k == 1 ~ custom(exponential_lccdf(y | lambda)),
    lambda ~ exponential(0.5)
  ),
  data = dat,
  chains = 4,
  cores = 4
)
```

Here `k == 0` identifies observed events and `k == 1` identifies censored times.

`exponential_lccdf(y | lambda)` is:

$$ \log\Pr(T>y\mid\lambda). $$

Using a log complementary CDF is more stable than computing `log(1 - CDF)`.

---

## 39. Latent-time imputation for censoring

An alternative declares a latent event time for every censored case, constrained by:

$$ T_i^{mis}>c_i. $$

Then:

$$ T_i^{mis}\sim\operatorname{Exponential}(\lambda) $$

under the lower-bound constraint.

This yields posterior draws of individual event times, which can be useful for prediction. But it introduces many parameters.

If the scientific target is only $\lambda$ or regression coefficients, marginalization is usually simpler and more efficient.

The lecture verifies that the marginalized and correctly constrained imputation approaches should agree about shared parameters.

---

## 40. Marginalization and imputation are two views

For a censored case:

$$ p(T>c\mid\theta) = \int_c^\infty p(T\mid\theta)\,dT. $$

The marginalized model evaluates this integral analytically.

The latent-time model samples from the corresponding truncated posterior.

Use marginalization when:

- only population parameters are needed;
- the survival function is available;
- computational efficiency matters.

Use latent times when:

- individual event-time predictions matter;
- downstream latent processes depend on event time;
- the integral is unavailable but augmentation is practical.

## 42. Discrete-time adoption model

Suppose a cat has a constant daily adoption probability $p$ while it remains available.

The waiting time follows a geometric distribution.

For adoption on day $d$:

$$ \Pr(T=d\mid p) = (1-p)^{d-1}p. $$

Interpretation:

- the cat was not adopted on days $1,\ldots,d-1$;
- the cat was adopted on day $d$.

The corresponding survival probability after $d$ days is:

$$ \Pr(T>d\mid p) = (1-p)^d. $$

---

## 43. Event likelihood for a cat

For cat $i$ adopted on day $d_i$:

$$ L_i = (1-p_i)^{d_i-1}p_i. $$

The stable log contribution is:

$$ \ell_i = (d_i-1)\log(1-p_i)+\log p_i. $$

In Stan-like code:

```stan
target += (days[i] - 1) * log1m(p[i]) + log(p[i]);
```

`log1m(p)` accurately computes $\log(1-p)$ when $p$ is close to zero.

---

## 44. Censored likelihood for a cat

For a cat observed for $d_i$ days without an adoption event:

$$ L_i = (1-p_i)^{d_i}. $$

The log contribution is:

$$ \ell_i=d_i\log(1-p_i). $$

In code:

```stan
target += days[i] * log1m(p[i]);
```

The censored record says the cat survived $d_i$ daily adoption opportunities without the focal event.

It does not say the cat's event time equals $d_i$.

---

## 45. Unified geometric likelihood

With event indicator $\delta_i$:

$$ \ell_i = \delta_i \left[(d_i-1)\log(1-p_i)+\log p_i\right] + (1-\delta_i)d_i\log(1-p_i). $$

This can also be simplified algebraically, but the two-branch form makes the observation logic explicit.

The complete posterior is:

$$ p(\theta\mid d,\delta,X) \propto p(\theta) \prod_i L_i(\theta). $$

The predictors $X_i$ determine $p_i$.

---

## 46. The lecture color model

The lecture gives each color category its own daily probability:

$$ p_i=P_{color[i]}, $$

with:

$$ P_k\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1,10). $$

This prior places more mass on small daily probabilities while retaining substantial uncertainty.

Its meaning should be checked on waiting-time and survival scales, not only on the probability scale.

A daily probability that seems small can imply rapid cumulative adoption across many days.

---

## 47. Prior predictive survival curves

For each prior draw $p^{(s)}$, simulate cat waiting times and compute the implied survival curve:

$$ S^{(s)}(d)=(1-p^{(s)})^d. $$

Ask:

- Does the prior imply almost all cats are adopted immediately?
- Does it imply most remain indefinitely?
- Are plausible median waiting times represented?
- Does the prior permit meaningful contrasts among groups?

Prior predictive checks translate abstract priors into observables.

## 48. Kaplan–Meier curves

The Kaplan–Meier estimator provides a nonparametric description of observed survival under standard censoring assumptions.

In this setting, “survival” means remaining unadopted with respect to the focal event.

Use it to:

- visualize time to adoption by group;
- show censored records;
- compare model-based posterior predictive curves with empirical patterns;
- detect obvious nonconstant hazards.

It is a descriptive benchmark, not a substitute for a generative causal model.

---

## 49. Why the adopted-only model differs

The lecture fits one model using only adopted cats and another including censored contributions.

The adopted-only model conditions on eventual observed adoption within the observation process. It tends to overrepresent short waits and groups with more observed adoptions.

The censor-aware model learns from both:

- when observed adoptions occurred;
- how long other cats remained without observed adoption.

Agreement between the two is not guaranteed and should not be expected when censoring is substantial.

---

## 50. Posterior predictive survival checks

For posterior draw $s$:

1. compute or simulate event times from $p_i^{(s)}$;
2. apply a censoring process matching the data collection;
3. calculate replicated survival curves and summaries;
4. compare them with observed curves.

Useful discrepancies include:

- early adoption fraction;
- median observed time;
- survival at 7, 30, 60, or 90 days;
- group differences through time;
- tail behavior;
- number censored.

Simulating only uncensored event times can miss defects in the observation model.

---

## 51. Independent censoring assumption

The usual likelihood assumes that, conditional on modeled covariates, censoring does not convey additional information about the latent adoption time.

Informally:

$$ T_i \perp C_i \mid X_i. $$

Administrative end-of-study censoring may approximate this condition.

But removal from risk for health, transfer, return to owner, or euthanasia may be associated with characteristics that also predict adoption. Calling every non-adoption outcome “censored” does not make the assumption true.

The censoring mechanism needs the same causal scrutiny as any missing-data mechanism.

---

## 52. Competing risks

In the script, every `out_event` other than `Adoption` is coded as censored.

Some alternatives may be competing events that prevent adoption in the shelter. If event type matters, define cause-specific daily hazards:

$$ p_{A,i}(t)=\Pr(\text{adoption at }t\mid\text{still at risk}), $$

$$ p_{K,i}(t)=\Pr(\text{competing event }K\text{ at }t\mid\text{still at risk}). $$

The probability of no event that day is:

$$ 1-p_{A,i}(t)-\sum_K p_{K,i}(t). $$

Competing-risk modeling separates adoption incidence from removal by other processes.

There is an important estimand distinction. Treating other event types as censored can be used to estimate a **cause-specific adoption hazard** among cats still at risk. But the survival curve obtained by pretending competing events cannot occur is not the real-world cumulative probability of adoption. To estimate cumulative adoption incidence, the model must also represent the competing hazards, because those events remove cats from the future adoption risk set.

For the homework, follow the lecture coding first, then name this limitation and, if feasible, perform a sensitivity analysis.

## 55. Choose an age scale

Raw weeks may be strongly right-skewed and make the intercept hard to interpret.

One useful transform is:

$$ A_i = \frac{\log(1+age_i)-\overline{\log(1+age)}}{s_{\log(1+age)}}. $$

In R:

```r
age_log <- log1p(d$intake_age)
A <- as.numeric(scale(age_log))
```

Benefits:

- distinguishes small differences among kittens;
- compresses the long adult-age tail;
- makes weakly regularizing slope priors easier to specify.

Always retain the transformation constants so predictions can be made at ages in weeks.

## 56. Continuous-age hazard model

Let the daily adoption probability vary by age:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p_i) = \alpha+\beta_A A_i. $$

Then:

$$ p_i = \operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\alpha+\beta_A A_i). $$

If $A$ increases with age:

- $\beta_A<0$ means older cats have a lower daily adoption probability;
- $\beta_A>0$ means older cats have a higher daily adoption probability.

But $\beta_A$ is a change in log odds of the daily hazard, not a direct difference in eventual adoption probability.
