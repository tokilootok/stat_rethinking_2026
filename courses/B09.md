## 1. Main thesis

The central argument is:

> **Start from a generative scientific theory when one is available, and build the statistical model around that theory rather than forcing the theory into an off-the-shelf regression template.**

A conventional generalized linear model often has the form:

$$
Y_i
\sim
p
\left(
Y_i
\mid
\mu_i,
\phi
\right)
$$

$$
g(\mu_i)
=
\alpha
+
\sum_k
\beta_k X_{ik}
$$

This is extremely useful.

But it presumes that the systematic part of the model is an additive linear predictor, perhaps transformed by a link.

Many scientific processes are not naturally additive.

A bespoke scientific model may instead be:

$$
\text{mechanism}
\rightarrow
\text{latent process}
\rightarrow
\text{measurement process}
\rightarrow
\text{observed data}
$$

The modeling workflow becomes:

$$
\text{causal theory}
\rightarrow
\text{generative equations}
\rightarrow
\text{parameter constraints}
\rightarrow
\text{observation model}
\rightarrow
\text{prior predictive simulation}
\rightarrow
\text{Bayesian inversion}
\rightarrow
\text{posterior process simulation}
$$

This is the mature form of the workflow introduced in A01.

## 3. What “Generalized Linear Madness” means

The title is not an argument that generalized linear models are bad.

It criticizes the habit:

$$
\text{scientific question}
\rightarrow
\text{choose familiar GLM}
\rightarrow
\text{interpret coefficients as mechanism}
$$

without asking whether the additive predictor actually represents the process.

The problem is **model substitution**:

$$
\text{statistical convenience}
\neq
\text{scientific mechanism}
$$

A GLM can be:

- an excellent descriptive approximation;
- a good estimator for a causally identified contrast;
- a practical forecasting device.

But it may still be a poor model of how the world generates the observations.

---

## 4. Geocentric versus mechanistic models

Earlier lectures used the geocentric metaphor.

A geocentric model can predict extremely well while representing the wrong mechanism.

A flexible regression can do the same.

Suppose:

$$
Y
=
f(X)
+
\epsilon
$$

is approximated with:

$$
f(X)
=
\alpha+
\beta_1X+
\beta_2X^2+
\beta_3X^3+
\cdots
$$

With enough terms, the approximation may fit almost any smooth pattern.

But the coefficients need not correspond to causal quantities.

A mechanistic model instead asks:

> Which physical, biological, behavioral, or institutional process would generate the observed relationship?

When that model fails, the failure can point toward a missing mechanism.

---

## 5. Scientific model versus statistical model

It is useful to separate two layers.

### Scientific process

A theory determines latent quantities:

$$
Z
=
f(X,\theta)
$$

### Observation process

Measurements are generated from those quantities:

$$
Y
\sim
p
\left(
Y
\mid
Z,
\psi
\right)
$$

Together:

$$
p
\left(
Y
\mid
X,\theta,\psi
\right)
$$

is the statistical model.

This distinction is crucial in Chapter 16.

For lynx and hares:

- the ODEs describe the population process;
- the pelt likelihood describes measurement.

For the Boxes experiment:

- latent strategies describe the behavioral process;
- observed choices are emissions from those strategies.

# Part I — Geometric people

## 6. From correlation to geometry

The `Howell1` data contain human height and weight.

A standard regression might write:

$$
W_i
=
\alpha+
\beta H_i+
\epsilon_i
$$

or add powers of height.

But scientific knowledge suggests a geometric relationship.

Approximate a body as a cylinder.

Cylinder volume is:

$$
V
=
\pi r^2h
$$

Assume radius is proportional to height:

$$
r
=
ph
$$

where $p$ is a body-width-to-height parameter.

Then:

$$
V
=
\pi p^2h^3
$$

If mass or weight is proportional to volume:

$$
W
=
kV
$$

then:

$$
W
=
k\pi p^2h^3
$$

The exponent on height is supplied by geometry:

$$
3
$$

It is not estimated from the data.

---

## 7. Why fixing the exponent matters

A generic power-law regression might estimate:

$$
W
=
\theta H^b
$$

with unknown exponent $b$.

The cylinder model instead asserts:

$$
b=3
$$

before seeing the data.

This is a scientific restriction.

It reduces flexibility but increases interpretability.

If the cubic relationship fails:

$$
W
\not\propto
H^3
$$

then the failure points toward incorrect assumptions such as:

- body proportions change with age;
- radius is not a constant proportion of height;
- body composition changes;
- density changes;
- the cylinder approximation breaks down.

A free polynomial would fit the pattern but provide less diagnostic information.

---

## 8. Scaling away arbitrary measurement units

The 2026 script scales:

$$
w_i
=
\frac{
W_i
}{
\overline{W}
}
$$

and:

$$
h_i
=
\frac{
H_i
}{
\overline{H}
}
$$

This produces dimensionless variables.

At the sample means:

$$
w=1
$$

and:

$$
h=1
$$

Scaling can make scientifically meaningful priors easier to construct because:

- kilograms disappear;
- centimeters disappear;
- parameters describe proportions or relative scales.

The model should nevertheless remember the transformation for interpretation and deployment.

---

## 9. Observation model for weight

The book chooses a Log-Normal likelihood:

$$
W_i
\sim
\operatorname{LogNormal}
\left(
\mu_i,
\sigma
\right)
$$

with:

$$
\exp(\mu_i)
=
k\pi p^2H_i^3
$$

The reason is that weight is:

- positive;
- continuous;
- plausibly multiplicative in its deviations.

For a Log-Normal distribution:

$$
\operatorname{median}(W_i)
=
\exp(\mu_i)
$$

while:

$$
\mathbb{E}[W_i]
=
\exp
\left(
\mu_i+
\frac{\sigma^2}{2}
\right)
$$

The 2026 prior-predictive plotting code correctly accounts for this distinction when plotting expected weight.

---

## 10. Scientifically meaningful parameters

The model contains:

$$
p
=
\frac{r}{h}
$$

and:

$$
k
=
\frac{
\text{weight}
}{
\text{volume}
}
$$

These have physical interpretations.

This changes prior construction.

Instead of asking:

> What is a weak prior for a generic regression slope?

we ask:

> Which body-width ratios are biologically plausible?

and:

> Which mass-per-volume relationships are plausible after scaling?

Scientific meaning makes prior information easier to articulate.

---

## 11. Book prior versus 2026 script prior

The book motivates a prior such as:

$$
p
\sim
\operatorname{Beta}(2,18)
$$

because the radius-to-height ratio must be positive and is plausibly much smaller than $0.5$.

The 2026 script uses a different scaled-model prior:

$$
p
\sim
\operatorname{Beta}(25,50)
$$

and:

$$
k
\sim
\operatorname{Exponential}(0.5)
$$

with:

$$
\sigma
\sim
\operatorname{Exponential}(1)
$$

The difference should not be silently reconciled.

The script's priors belong to its scaled formulation and its 2026 teaching demonstration.

The book's priors illustrate the domain-reasoning process.

In either case, the correct test is prior predictive behavior.

---

## 12. Prior predictive curves

For prior draw $s$:

$$
p^{(s)}
\sim
p(p)
$$

$$
k^{(s)}
\sim
p(k)
$$

$$
\sigma^{(s)}
\sim
p(\sigma)
$$

the latent median curve is:

$$
\widetilde{W}^{(s)}(h)
=
\pi
k^{(s)}
\left(
p^{(s)}
\right)^2
h^3
$$

The Log-Normal expected curve is:

$$
\mathbb{E}
\left[
W
\mid
h,
\theta^{(s)}
\right]
=
\widetilde{W}^{(s)}(h)
\exp
\left(
\frac{
(\sigma^{(s)})^2
}{2}
\right)
$$

Prior predictive simulation asks whether the complete implied body-weight curves are plausible.

This is more informative than inspecting parameter priors independently.

---

## 13. Structural non-identifiability

The mean function contains:

$$
kp^2
$$

but not $k$ and $p$ separately.

Therefore the likelihood identifies only:

$$
\theta
=
kp^2
$$

Any pair satisfying:

$$
kp^2
=
c
$$

produces the same expected curve.

The data cannot distinguish them.

This is **structural non-identifiability**.

The posterior therefore contains a ridge:

$$
k
\propto
\frac{1}{p^2}
$$

The 2026 script explicitly plots:

$$
p^2
$$

against:

$$
k
$$

and overlays the inverse relationship.

---

## 14. Prediction can be identified when parameters are not

Even though $k$ and $p$ cannot be learned separately from height and weight alone, the product:

$$
\theta
=
kp^2
$$

can be estimated.

A reduced model is:

$$
\exp(\mu_i)
=
\pi\theta H_i^3
$$

with:

$$
\theta>0
$$

The posterior predictive curve may therefore be precise even when individual scientific parameters are not.

This is a fundamental distinction:

$$
\text{parameter identifiability}
\neq
\text{predictive identifiability}
$$

The intended scientific question determines whether the non-identifiability is acceptable.

---

## 15. Priors do not create data identification

Informative priors can make the posterior proper and numerically estimable.

But they do not change the fact that the likelihood contains only:

$$
kp^2
$$

The posterior separation of $k$ and $p$ therefore comes from:

- prior information;
- not independent information in the observed height–weight pairs.

A mature report should distinguish:

$$
\text{likelihood information}
$$

from:

$$
\text{prior information}
$$

This is especially important in mechanistic models, which often contain more scientifically meaningful parameters than the data can identify directly.

---

## 16. The parameter-free cubic comparison

The 2026 script includes a strong restricted model:

$$
\exp(\mu_i)
=
H_i^3
$$

for scaled height and weight.

It effectively asks:

> How far can the pure cubic scaling relationship go without fitting an additional geometric multiplier?

This is a useful scientific baseline.

The comparison is not merely about predictive score.

It tests the consequences of a strong mechanistic claim.

A model with fewer free parameters can be more scientifically informative because its failures are more interpretable.

---

## 17. Where the cylinder model fails

The book notes poor fit among the shortest individuals.

This suggests the constant-proportion assumption:

$$
r=ph
$$

may fail over development.

Children do not have the same body proportions as adults.

Likewise, density parameter $k$ may vary with:

- fat;
- muscle;
- bone;
- age;
- sex;
- development.

The model's misfit therefore suggests explicit extensions:

$$
p=p(h)
$$

or:

$$
k=k(h)
$$

rather than an arbitrary polynomial.

---

## 18. Mechanistic extension in the 2026 script

The script explores a height-dependent form in which geometric components themselves can change with height.

Schematically:

$$
k(h)
=
\exp
\left(
k_0+k_1h
\right)
$$

and:

$$
p(h)
=
\exp
\left(
p_0+p_1h
\right)
$$

leading to:

$$
\exp(\mu)
=
\pi
k(h)
p(h)^2
h^3
$$

The important conceptual move is:

> Add flexibility by modifying scientifically meaningful sub-processes.

This is different from simply adding:

$$
h^2,h^3,h^4
$$

to a regression because fit is poor.

---

## 19. The scientific model can reduce to a GLM

Take logs:

$$
W
=
k\pi p^2H^3
$$

Then:

$$
\log W
=
\log k
+
\log \pi
+
2\log p
+
3\log H
$$

If $k$ and $p$ are constant:

$$
\log W
=
\alpha
+
3\log H
$$

where:

$$
\alpha
=
\log k+
\log \pi+
2\log p
$$

This is a linear model on the log scale with slope fixed at:

$$
3
$$

So the critique is not that linear models are mathematically inappropriate.

The point is that theory tells us:

- which transformation to use;
- which slope to fix;
- which parameters have scientific meaning.

# Part II — Hidden minds and observed behavior

## 20. The Boxes experiment

The second official case study uses `Boxes`.

Children observe demonstrations involving three colored options.

The observed choice is coded:

$$
Y_i
\in
\{1,2,3\}
$$

where the categories represent:

1. previously unchosen color;
2. majority-demonstrated color;
3. minority-demonstrated color.

A simple categorical regression could estimate choice frequencies.

But the scientific question is different:

> Which latent strategy generated each child's choice?

Behavior is observed.

Strategy is hidden.

---

## 21. Behavior is not strategy

Suppose half the children:

- choose randomly;

and half:

- follow the majority.

Random choice selects the majority option one-third of the time.

Therefore the observed fraction choosing the majority is greater than the fraction using the majority strategy.

In general:

$$
\Pr
\left(
Y=\mathrm{majority}
\right)
\neq
\Pr
\left(
S=\mathrm{majority\ strategy}
\right)
$$

because:

$$
Y
$$

is generated by a mixture of strategies.

This is the central inferential problem.

---

## 22. Five candidate strategies

The book and script use five latent strategies.

Let:

$$
S_i
\in
\{1,2,3,4,5\}
$$

represent:

1. follow majority;
2. follow minority;
3. maverick / choose the unchosen color;
4. random color choice;
5. follow the first demonstrated color.

Each strategy has a different emission distribution:

$$
\Pr
\left(
Y_i=y
\mid
S_i=s,
X_i
\right)
$$

where $X_i$ includes whether the majority color was shown first.

The strategies are theory objects.

They are not discovered automatically from a generic classifier.

---

## 23. Strategy frequencies form a simplex

Let:

$$
\boldsymbol{p}
=
\left(
p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4,p_5
\right)
$$

where:

$$
p_s
=
\Pr(S=s)
$$

The probabilities satisfy:

$$
p_s\geq0
$$

and:

$$
\sum_{s=1}^{5}p_s=1
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boldsymbol{p}
\in
\Delta^4
$$

the five-category probability simplex.

The book's Stan model uses a Dirichlet prior:

$$
\boldsymbol{p}
\sim
\operatorname{Dirichlet}(4,4,4,4,4)
$$

This mildly regularizes the population strategy mixture away from extreme corners.

---

## 24. Marginalizing the hidden strategy

The strategy itself is not observed.

For one observation:

$$
\Pr(Y_i=y_i)
=
\sum_{s=1}^{5}
\Pr(S_i=s)
\Pr
\left(
Y_i=y_i
\mid
S_i=s
\right)
$$

or:

$$
\Pr(Y_i=y_i)
=
\sum_{s=1}^{5}
p_s
q_{is}
$$

where:

$$
q_{is}
=
\Pr
\left(
Y_i=y_i
\mid
S_i=s
\right)
$$

This is a finite mixture model.

The latent discrete state is integrated out rather than sampled directly.

---

## 25. Why Stan marginalizes discrete parameters

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo requires differentiable continuous parameter spaces.

A discrete latent strategy:

$$
S_i
\in
\{1,\ldots,5\}
$$

cannot be sampled directly with HMC.

Instead, compute:

$$
\Pr(Y_i)
=
\sum_s
p_s q_{is}
$$

and add:

$$
\log
\left[
\sum_s
p_s q_{is}
\right]
$$

to the target log density.

This preserves exact uncertainty about the hidden state while keeping the sampled parameters continuous.

---

## 26. `log_sum_exp`

Direct computation of tiny probabilities can underflow numerically.

Stan works on the log scale.

Define:

$$
\phi_s
=
\log p_s
+
\log q_{is}
$$

Then:

$$
\log
\Pr(Y_i)
=
\log
\left(
\sum_s
\exp(\phi_s)
\right)
$$

Stan computes this stably with:

```stan
log_sum_exp(phi)
```

The official model uses precisely this pattern.

This is one of the standard computational tools for:

- mixture models;
- hidden states;
- latent classes;
- HMMs;
- finite-strategy models.

---

## 27. Deterministic and probabilistic strategies

Some strategies produce a deterministic choice.

For the majority strategy:

$$
q_{is}
=
\begin{cases}
1 & Y_i=\mathrm{majority} \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

The random strategy produces:

$$
q_{i,\mathrm{random}}
=
\frac{1}{3}
$$

for every choice.

The follow-first strategy depends on trial structure:

$$
q_{i,\mathrm{first}}
=
\Pr
\left(
Y_i
\mid
\mathrm{majority\_first}_i
\right)
$$

The likelihood therefore encodes behavioral theory directly.

---

## 28. Mixture identifiability

Two different strategies can sometimes produce the same observation.

Therefore a single trial rarely identifies an individual's strategy.

Identification comes from:

- different emission patterns;
- variation in trial design;
- many observations;
- priors;
- experimental counterbalancing.

If two strategies generate exactly the same distribution for every possible experimental condition, they are observationally equivalent.

No amount of data can distinguish them without changing the design.

This is a design problem, not an MCMC problem.

---

## 29. Experimental design identifies latent strategies

The variable:

$$
\mathrm{majority\_first}
$$

changes the predictions of the follow-first strategy.

When the majority color appears first:

$$
\mathrm{follow\ first}
$$

and:

$$
\mathrm{follow\ majority}
$$

predict the same choice.

When the minority appears first, they predict different choices.

Counterbalancing therefore creates observations that separate otherwise overlapping strategies.

This is a fundamental design principle:

> To identify hidden mechanisms, create interventions or conditions under which they make different predictions.

---

## 30. Posterior strategy probabilities

The model estimates a posterior distribution over:

$$
\boldsymbol{p}
$$

not one fixed strategy composition.

For posterior draw $m$:

$$
\boldsymbol{p}^{(m)}
=
\left(
p_1^{(m)},
\ldots,
p_5^{(m)}
\right)
$$

All components sum to one.

An increase in one strategy necessarily reduces available probability for others.

Therefore marginal posterior summaries are dependent.

The official script animates draws of the complete strategy vector to emphasize this joint structure.

---

## 31. Posterior probability of an individual's strategy

Although the model estimates population strategy frequencies, Bayes' theorem can also infer an individual's latent strategy.

For child $i$:

$$
\Pr
\left(
S_i=s
\mid
Y_i,X_i,\boldsymbol{p}
\right)
=
\frac{
p_s
\Pr
\left(
Y_i
\mid
S_i=s,X_i
\right)
}{
\sum_r
p_r
\Pr
\left(
Y_i
\mid
S_i=r,X_i
\right)
}
$$

Posterior averaging then integrates uncertainty in $\boldsymbol{p}$.

The result is generally not a hard classification.

It is a probability distribution over strategies.

---

## 32. Predictor-dependent strategy mixtures

The book notes that the strategy simplex could vary across categories.

For group $g$:

$$
\boldsymbol{p}_g
=
\left(
p_{g1},\ldots,p_{g5}
\right)
$$

Then:

$$
\Pr(Y_i)
=
\sum_s
p_{G_i,s}
q_{is}
$$

This can compare strategy compositions across:

- cultures;
- age groups;
- conditions;
- interventions.

For continuous predictors, there is no unique default.

A geocentric approach could place logits on strategy probabilities.

A scientific approach should first ask why the strategy distribution should change.

---

## 33. Mixture models are not ordinary categorical regressions

A categorical GLM models:

$$
\Pr(Y_i=y)
$$

directly.

The Boxes model instead models:

$$
\Pr(S_i=s)
$$

and derives:

$$
\Pr(Y_i=y)
$$

through the strategy-specific emission process.

This difference matters because the scientific estimand is latent.

A high observed frequency of category 2 does not imply a high frequency of strategy 1.

The observation model mediates between strategy and behavior.

---

## 34. State-space interpretation

The Boxes model has:

- a hidden state $S_i$;
- an observed emission $Y_i$.

This is the anatomy of a state-space model.

A general state-space model contains:

### State model

$$
Z_t
\sim
p
\left(
Z_t
\mid
Z_{t-1},\theta
\right)
$$

### Observation model

$$
Y_t
\sim
p
\left(
Y_t
\mid
Z_t,\psi
\right)
$$

In the Boxes example, the latent strategy need not evolve over time.

B10 will extend this logic to dynamic discrete states:

$$
\text{Hidden Markov Models}
$$

# Part III — Book-only bridge: ordinary differential nut cracking

## 35. Scope note

Chapter 16 contains a chimpanzee nut-cracking model.

The official 2026 `19_gen_lin_madness.r` script does **not** implement this section.

It is included here because B09 officially assigns Chapter 16 and because the example illustrates another important route beyond GLMs:

$$
\text{derive a nonlinear statistical model from a differential equation}
$$

Treat the following sections as **book correspondence**, not direct 2026 script transcription.

---

## 36. Scientific model of growth

Let:

$$
M_t
$$

be body mass at age $t$.

Assume terminal growth:

$$
\frac{dM}{dt}
=
k
\left(
M_{\max}-M_t
\right)
$$

The farther the individual is from adult mass, the faster growth occurs.

The solution is:

$$
M_t
=
M_{\max}
\left(
1-
\exp(-kt)
\right)
$$

This produces:

- rapid early growth;
- deceleration;
- an asymptote at adult mass.

The functional form is derived from a process assumption rather than selected from a curve library.

---

## 37. From body mass to strength

Suppose strength is proportional to mass:

$$
S_t
=
\beta M_t
$$

The model then links strength to nut-opening efficiency.

A convenient rate form becomes:

$$
r(t)
=
\phi
\left[
1-
\exp(-kt)
\right]^{\theta}
$$

where:

- $\phi$ controls maximum nut-opening rate;
- $k$ controls developmental speed;
- $\theta$ controls how growth translates into performance.

The shape has a mechanistic interpretation.

---

## 38. Poisson observation process

Let:

- $n_i$ be nuts opened;
- $d_i$ be bout duration;
- $t_i$ be age.

The model is:

$$
n_i
\sim
\operatorname{Poisson}
\left(
\lambda_i
\right)
$$

with:

$$
\lambda_i
=
d_i
\phi
\left[
1-
\exp(-kt_i)
\right]^{\theta}
$$

Duration acts as exposure directly.

Unlike a standard Poisson GLM, there is no requirement to write:

$$
\log \lambda_i
=
\log d_i
+
X_i\beta
$$

because the mechanistic rate is modeled directly.

---

## 39. Mechanistic exposure versus GLM offset

In a standard log-linear Poisson model:

$$
\log \lambda_i
=
\log d_i
+
\eta_i
$$

The exposure term is an offset.

In the nut model:

$$
\lambda_i
=
d_i r(t_i)
$$

This is the same basic accounting identity:

$$
\text{expected count}
=
\text{time exposed}
\times
\text{rate}
$$

but the rate:

$$
r(t)
$$

comes from a nonlinear developmental model.

The exposure principle survives.

The generic linear predictor does not.

---

## 40. Biological priors

The book uses positive priors such as:

$$
\phi
\sim
\operatorname{LogNormal}
\left(
\log 1,
0.1
\right)
$$

$$
k
\sim
\operatorname{LogNormal}
\left(
\log 2,
0.25
\right)
$$

$$
\theta
\sim
\operatorname{LogNormal}
\left(
\log 5,
0.25
\right)
$$

The priors encode biological constraints:

- rates must be positive;
- chimpanzees approach adult mass over a known developmental time scale;
- maximum nut-opening rates should be plausible.

Prior predictive simulation should examine the entire developmental curve.

---

## 41. Model failure as scientific evidence

Suppose the strength-only model fails systematically.

That suggests omitted mechanisms such as:

- learning;
- experience;
- technique;
- dexterity;
- social transmission;
- sex-specific growth;
- individual heterogeneity.

The next model should modify the scientific process.

A generic spline may improve fit without revealing which mechanism was missing.

This is one of Chapter 16's deepest lessons:

> A wrong mechanistic model can be more scientifically productive than a highly accurate uninterpretable approximation.

# Part IV — Population dynamics

## 42. Observed pelts are not populations

The official 2026 script loads `Lynx_Hare`.

The observations are historical pelt counts.

Let:

- $H_t$ be true hare population;
- $L_t$ be true lynx population;
- $h_t$ be observed hare pelts;
- $\ell_t$ be observed lynx pelts.

The causal structure is:

$$
H_t,L_t
\rightarrow
h_t,\ell_t
$$

The observed series are measurements of latent populations.

They are not the states themselves.

This immediately suggests a state-space model.

---

## 43. Why an autoregression can be scientifically misleading

A generic autoregression might write:

$$
H_t
=
\alpha_H
+
\beta_{HH}H_{t-1}
+
\beta_{HL}L_{t-1}
+
\epsilon_t
$$

and:

$$
L_t
=
\alpha_L
+
\beta_{LL}L_{t-1}
+
\beta_{LH}H_{t-1}
+
\epsilon_t
$$

A still more flexible model might add:

$$
H_{t-2},
L_{t-2},
H_{t-3},
\ldots
$$

But in a mechanistic view, the distant past does not directly cause the present.

It acts through intervening states.

If measurements contain error, using observed lagged values as if they were true states also propagates measurement noise.

A state-space process separates:

$$
\text{latent dynamics}
$$

from:

$$
\text{measurement}
$$

---

## 44. The Lotka–Volterra process

The hare population obeys:

$$
\frac{dH}{dt}
=
H
\left(
b_H-m_HL
\right)
$$

where:

- $b_H$ is hare per-capita birth rate;
- $m_HL$ is predator-induced mortality.

The lynx population obeys:

$$
\frac{dL}{dt}
=
L
\left(
b_LH-m_L
\right)
$$

where:

- $b_LH$ governs reproduction supported by hare abundance;
- $m_L$ is lynx mortality.

These are coupled ODEs.

Each species changes the growth rate of the other.

---

## 45. Mechanistic interpretation of parameters

In:

$$
\frac{dH}{dt}
=
H
\left(
b_H-m_HL
\right)
$$

$H$ multiplies the entire rate because births and deaths are per-capita processes.

If:

$$
H=0
$$

then:

$$
\frac{dH}{dt}=0
$$

There are no spontaneous hares.

Likewise, if:

$$
L=0
$$

then:

$$
\frac{dL}{dt}=0
$$

under this simple model.

The equations obey biological constraints automatically.

A generic autoregression with a positive intercept may imply population appearing from nothing.

---

## 46. Predator–prey feedback

When hares are abundant:

$$
H
\uparrow
$$

lynx growth becomes positive:

$$
b_LH-m_L>0
$$

so:

$$
L
\uparrow
$$

As lynx increase:

$$
m_HL
$$

increases, reducing hare growth:

$$
H
\downarrow
$$

As hares become scarce:

$$
b_LH-m_L
$$

falls, so lynx decline:

$$
L
\downarrow
$$

This releases predation pressure, allowing hares to recover.

The feedback produces oscillations.

The cycle is an emergent property of the coupled equations.

---

## 47. Numerical integration

The ODEs generally cannot be rearranged into a simple explicit formula for:

$$
H_t
$$

and:

$$
L_t
$$

at arbitrary $t$.

The 2026 script uses a small time increment:

$$
dt=0.002
$$

and updates:

$$
H_{t+dt}
=
H_t
+
dt
H_t
\left(
b_H-m_HL_t
\right)
$$

$$
L_{t+dt}
=
L_t
+
dt
L_t
\left(
b_LH_t-m_L
\right)
$$

Repeated updates approximate the continuous trajectory.

This is Euler integration for simulation.

Stan uses a more sophisticated ODE solver for inference.

---

## 48. Prior predictive dynamics

The 2026 script samples candidate parameter vectors around:

$$
\left(
0.5,
0.05,
0.025,
0.5
\right)
$$

and simulates the resulting population dynamics.

This is crucial.

For nonlinear dynamic models, individual parameter priors can look reasonable while jointly generating:

- explosive populations;
- immediate extinction;
- unrealistic cycle periods;
- impossible amplitudes.

The scientifically meaningful prior object is often the trajectory:

$$
\left\{
H_t,L_t
\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
$$

not the marginal prior density of each rate constant.

---

## 49. Measurement model for pelts

True populations are not observed.

The book uses an approximate Log-Normal observation model:

$$
h_t
\sim
\operatorname{LogNormal}
\left(
\log(p_HH_t),
\sigma_H
\right)
$$

$$
\ell_t
\sim
\operatorname{LogNormal}
\left(
\log(p_LL_t),
\sigma_L
\right)
$$

where:

- $p_H$ is an observation or trapping fraction for hares;
- $p_L$ is the corresponding fraction for lynx;
- $\sigma_H$ and $\sigma_L$ represent measurement dispersion.

The measurement model is distinct from population dynamics.

---

## 50. Why the observation fraction matters

Suppose:

$$
h_t
\approx
p_HH_t
$$

The observed pelt count identifies only the product of:

- true population;
- trapping probability.

Without information about $p_H$, absolute population scale is difficult to identify.

The same is true for lynx.

Again:

$$
\text{mechanistic modeling exposes non-identifiability}
$$

rather than hiding it.

The model may still identify:

- relative cycles;
- phase relations;
- biological rate combinations.

---

## 51. Initial states are parameters

The ODE trajectory requires starting values:

$$
H_1
$$

and:

$$
L_1
$$

The book treats them as unknown latent parameters with priors such as:

$$
H_1
\sim
\operatorname{LogNormal}
\left(
\log 10,
1
\right)
$$

$$
L_1
\sim
\operatorname{LogNormal}
\left(
\log 10,
1
\right)
$$

Once the initial states and rate parameters are specified, the deterministic ODE defines later latent states.

Thus:

$$
H_{2:T},L_{2:T}
$$

are derived quantities rather than free independent parameters.

---

## 52. Full latent-process model

The process component is:

$$
\frac{dH}{dt}
=
H
\left(
b_H-m_HL
\right)
$$

$$
\frac{dL}{dt}
=
L
\left(
b_LH-m_L
\right)
$$

The initial-state priors are:

$$
p(H_1,L_1)
$$

The measurement model is:

$$
p
\left(
h_t,\ell_t
\mid
H_t,L_t,p_H,p_L,\sigma_H,\sigma_L
\right)
$$

The posterior is:

$$
p
\left(
b_H,m_H,b_L,m_L,
H_1,L_1,
p_H,p_L,
\sigma_H,\sigma_L
\mid
h_{1:T},\ell_{1:T}
\right)
$$

The latent population trajectories are deterministic functions of the process parameters in this model.

---

## 53. ODE inference in Stan

During MCMC, every proposed parameter vector implies a different ODE trajectory.

For posterior candidate:

$$
\theta^{(s)}
=
\left(
b_H,m_H,b_L,m_L,H_1,L_1,\ldots
\right)
$$

Stan must:

1. solve the ODE;
2. obtain $H_t^{(s)}$ and $L_t^{(s)}$;
3. evaluate the pelt likelihood;
4. compute gradients;
5. advance HMC.

This is computationally much more expensive than evaluating a linear predictor.

But it directly estimates the scientific process.

The 2026 script loads `Lynx_Hare_model` and fits it through Stan.

---

## 54. Deterministic state, stochastic measurement

In the official model:

$$
\theta,H_1,L_1
$$

determine the latent trajectory exactly.

Therefore, conditional on the parameters:

$$
\operatorname{Var}
\left(
H_t
\mid
\theta,H_1,L_1
\right)
=
0
$$

The stochasticity occurs in the measurement model.

This is a **deterministic process / stochastic observation** state-space model.

A richer ecological model could add process noise:

$$
Z_{t+1}
\sim
p
\left(
Z_{t+1}
\mid
Z_t,\theta
\right)
$$

The distinction should be explicit.

---

## 55. Posterior pelt predictions

For posterior draw $s$:

$$
h_t^{\mathrm{rep},(s)}
\sim
\operatorname{LogNormal}
\left(
\log
\left[
p_H^{(s)}
H_t^{(s)}
\right],
\sigma_H^{(s)}
\right)
$$

and similarly for lynx.

These posterior predictive series include:

- posterior parameter uncertainty;
- latent population uncertainty induced by parameters;
- measurement error.

They should therefore appear noisy, as the observations do.

---

## 56. Posterior population predictions

The latent population trajectories:

$$
H_t^{(s)},L_t^{(s)}
$$

exclude observation noise.

They are smooth because the ODE is deterministic.

Comparing:

$$
\text{observed-level predictions}
$$

with:

$$
\text{latent-state predictions}
$$

is one of the most important visualizations in the chapter.

The observed jagged series should not be mistaken for jagged underlying population dynamics.

---

## 57. State-space causal direction

A naïve time-series interpretation might imply:

$$
h_{t-1}
\rightarrow
h_t
$$

But the scientific model says:

$$
H_{t-1}
\rightarrow
H_t
$$

and:

$$
H_{t-1}
\rightarrow
h_{t-1}
$$

$$
H_t
\rightarrow
h_t
$$

The measurements do not cause future measurements.

The hidden state causes both its measurement and the next state.

This distinction is essential whenever measurements are noisy.

---

## 58. Why deeper lags are epicycles

A lag model may improve prediction by including:

$$
Y_{t-1},
Y_{t-2},
Y_{t-3},
\ldots
$$

But a mechanistic interpretation generally requires intermediate states or delayed causal processes.

The statement:

$$
Y_{t-3}
\rightarrow
Y_t
$$

without causal mediation through $t-2$ and $t-1$ is usually not plausible.

If a process has delayed effects, model the delay mechanism.

Do not automatically add more lagged observations as direct causes.

---

## 59. Mechanistic models can forecast better outside the training regime

A flexible autoregression can interpolate historical patterns.

But when conditions change, it may fail because it lacks structural constraints.

A mechanistic model can sometimes extrapolate more credibly under:

- altered initial populations;
- changed mortality;
- changed birth rates;
- interventions on predator abundance.

This is the same reason causal models matter for policy.

Prediction under intervention requires a model of the process that remains valid after the intervention.

---

## 60. Mechanistic extrapolation is still conditional

A mechanistic model does not automatically extrapolate correctly.

The Lotka–Volterra model omits:

- alternative predators;
- food limitation;
- seasonality;
- disease;
- climate;
- migration;
- stochastic ecological shocks.

The book explicitly notes reasons to doubt the model as a literal explanation of the lynx–hare system.

The advantage is that its assumptions are visible and criticizable.

---

# Part V — Unifying principles

## 61. Four kinds of bespoke structure in Chapter 16

Chapter 16 demonstrates four distinct modeling moves.

### Scientific parameter restriction

Geometry fixes:

$$
W
\propto
H^3
$$

### Latent mixture

Observed behavior arises from hidden strategies:

$$
\Pr(Y)
=
\sum_s
\Pr(S=s)
\Pr(Y\mid S=s)
$$

### Mechanistic nonlinear curve

Developmental dynamics imply:

$$
r(t)
=
\phi
\left[
1-\exp(-kt)
\right]^\theta
$$

### Dynamic latent state

Population states obey coupled ODEs:

$$
\frac{dZ}{dt}
=
f(Z,\theta)
$$

These are all beyond the default “linear predictor plus link” mindset.

---

## 62. Bespoke does not mean complicated for its own sake

The geometric model is arguably simpler than a flexible polynomial.

The Lotka–Volterra equations use only a few process parameters.

A bespoke model can be parsimonious because scientific theory imposes structure.

Complexity should be measured in possible behavior, not only in the number of coefficients.

A highly constrained nonlinear model may have fewer effective degrees of freedom than a large GLM.

---

## 63. Structural parameters and nuisance parameters

A bespoke model often distinguishes:

### Structural parameters

These define the process:

$$
b_H,m_H,b_L,m_L
$$

### Measurement parameters

These describe observations:

$$
p_H,p_L,\sigma_H,\sigma_L
$$

### Initial conditions

These initialize the state:

$$
H_1,L_1
$$

This separation improves interpretation.

It also makes clear which interventions act on which part of the system.

Changing measurement error is not the same as changing predator mortality.

---

## 64. Model failure is localized

Suppose pelt predictions fail but latent population dynamics look scientifically plausible.

The problem may be in:

$$
p(Y\mid Z)
$$

the measurement model.

Suppose both observed and latent cycles have the wrong period.

The problem may be in:

$$
f(Z,\theta)
$$

the process model.

Suppose the posterior runs to implausible rate values.

The issue may be:

- non-identifiability;
- poor priors;
- wrong scaling;
- omitted process structure.

Modular scientific models make criticism more localized.

---

## 65. Prior predictive simulation becomes more important

With a GLM, a bad prior may produce implausible regression lines.

With a nonlinear system, bad priors can produce:

- explosions;
- extinctions;
- impossible latent states;
- numerical solver failure;
- wildly oscillatory trajectories;
- degenerate mixtures.

Prior predictive simulation is therefore part of model engineering.

The relevant unit of inspection is often a complete trajectory or behavioral distribution.

---

## 66. Identifiability should be tested before fitting

Before MCMC, ask whether different parameter values can produce the same observables.

Examples:

### Geometric people

$$
kp^2
$$

is identified, but $k$ and $p$ are not separately.

### Pelt model

Absolute population and trapping fraction can trade off.

### Hidden strategies

Strategies with identical emission distributions cannot be distinguished.

### Dynamic systems

Several rate combinations can produce similar trajectories over a short time window.

Simulation and algebra can expose these problems before expensive inference.

---

## 67. Scientific identifiability versus statistical identifiability

A parameter can be statistically estimable but scientifically ambiguous.

For example, a flexible regression coefficient may be tightly estimated while not corresponding to a meaningful mechanism.

Conversely, a scientifically meaningful parameter may be only weakly identified.

The desirable target is:

$$
\text{scientific meaning}
+
\text{identification}
+
\text{computational stability}
$$

All three are separate requirements.

---

## 68. DAGs versus full structural models

A DAG can state:

$$
X
\rightarrow
Y
$$

but it does not specify:

$$
Y=f(X,U)
$$

A structural model adds:

- functional form;
- time;
- nonlinear feedback;
- state transitions;
- measurement.

For predator–prey dynamics, a DAG is insufficient because:

$$
H_t
\leftrightarrow
L_t
$$

through time creates feedback.

The dynamic system requires time-indexed structural equations or differential equations.

DAG reasoning remains useful, but it is not the final model.

---

## 69. Feedback is not representable in a static DAG

A DAG must be acyclic.

Predator–prey feedback seems cyclic:

$$
H
\rightarrow
L
\rightarrow
H
$$

The resolution is time.

Write:

$$
H_t
\rightarrow
L_{t+dt}
$$

and:

$$
L_t
\rightarrow
H_{t+dt}
$$

Now the graph is acyclic when expanded across time.

ODEs provide a compact continuous-time representation of this dynamic causal structure.

---

## 70. Interventions in mechanistic models

A mechanistic model naturally supports interventions on structural parameters.

Examples include:

### Geometry

Change body proportion parameter:

$$
p
$$

### Behavioral strategy mixture

Change experimental information and recompute emissions.

### Nut development

Change developmental rate:

$$
k
$$

or an experience parameter in an expanded model.

### Predator–prey system

Change lynx mortality:

$$
m_L
$$

and simulate future trajectories.

The intervention acts on a process component and the consequences propagate through the generative model.

---

## 71. Counterfactual trajectories

For intervention $a$, define process parameters:

$$
\theta(a)
$$

Then simulate:

$$
Z^{(a)}(t)
=
F
\left(
Z_0,
\theta(a),
t
\right)
$$

The causal estimand may be:

$$
\tau(t)
=
\mathbb{E}
\left[
Z^{(1)}(t)
-
Z^{(0)}(t)
\right]
$$

or an integrated quantity:

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{AUC}}
=
\int_0^T
\left[
Z^{(1)}(t)
-
Z^{(0)}(t)
\right]
dt
$$

Mechanistic models make dynamic causal estimands natural.

# Part VI — CRM and CausalOps

## 72. Why Chapter 16 matters for CRM

Many CRM problems are forced into:

$$
\operatorname{logit}
\Pr
\left(
Y=1
\right)
=
\alpha+
X\beta
$$

even when the real system involves:

- customer states;
- exposure histories;
- repeated interventions;
- stock-and-flow dynamics;
- latent strategies;
- delayed effects;
- measurement error;
- feedback.

B09 suggests replacing some of these regressions with explicit generative customer-system models.

---

## 73. CRM example: latent promotion strategies

Suppose customers react to promotions using latent behavioral types:

$$
S_i
\in
\{
\text{never-buy},
\text{organic},
\text{small-discount},
\text{large-discount},
\text{deal-seeker}
\}
$$

Offer response is generated from:

$$
\Pr
\left(
Y_i
\mid
S_i,
A_i
\right)
$$

Population strategy frequencies are:

$$
\boldsymbol{p}
$$

Then:

$$
\Pr
\left(
Y_i
\mid
A_i
\right)
=
\sum_s
p_s
\Pr
\left(
Y_i
\mid
S_i=s,A_i
\right)
$$

This is analogous to the Boxes model.

It differs conceptually from labeling customers by observed discount response.

---

## 74. CRM experimental design for latent strategies

To distinguish:

- organic buyers;
- 5-euro-sensitive buyers;
- 10-euro-sensitive buyers;
- high-discount-only buyers,

the offer design must create conditions under which these strategies predict different behavior.

If every historically targeted customer receives 20 euros, then several latent strategies are observationally indistinguishable.

Identification requires variation such as:

$$
A
\in
\{
0,5,10,20
\}
$$

preferably randomized or quasi-randomized.

This is the CRM analogue of counterbalancing `majority_first`.

---

## 75. Static mixture versus dynamic customer state

A latent strategy mixture assumes the hidden state is stable during the modeled observation.

But customer state may evolve:

$$
S_t
\rightarrow
S_{t+1}
$$

For example:

- dormant;
- browsing;
- price-sensitive;
- subscriber;
- at-risk;
- churned.

Then the proper model becomes:

$$
\Pr
\left(
S_{t+1}
\mid
S_t,A_t,X_t
\right)
$$

plus:

$$
\Pr
\left(
Y_t
\mid
S_t
\right)
$$

This is the bridge from B09 to B10's Hidden Markov Models.

---

## 76. CRM example: subscription stock-and-flow dynamics

Let:

- $C(t)$ be active customers;
- $A(t)$ be acquisition flow;
- $r(t)$ be churn hazard.

A simple continuous-time model is:

$$
\frac{dC}{dt}
=
A(t)
-
r(t)C(t)
$$

An intervention may affect:

$$
A(t)
$$

or:

$$
r(t)
$$

rather than directly affecting monthly active customers.

Observed CRM metrics are measurements of this latent process.

This model can be extended to:

- acquisition;
- activation;
- retained customers;
- churn;
- reactivation.

## 77. CRM example: coupled funnel compartments

Let:

- $P(t)$ be prospects;
- $E(t)$ engaged customers;
- $S(t)$ subscribers;
- $C(t)$ churned customers.

A mechanistic system might be:

$$
\frac{dP}{dt}
=
\lambda
-
k_{PE}P
$$

$$
\frac{dE}{dt}
=
k_{PE}P
-
k_{ES}E
-
k_{EC}E
$$

$$
\frac{dS}{dt}
=
k_{ES}E
-
k_{SC}S
$$

$$
\frac{dC}{dt}
=
k_{EC}E
+
k_{SC}S
$$

Campaigns can modify transition rates.

This is a much richer causal representation than regressing churn on campaign counts.

## 78. CRM measurement model

True customer state is rarely observed perfectly.

Suppose:

$$
S_t
$$

is true engagement.

Observed events include:

- web visits;
- app opens;
- purchases;
- support contacts;
- email clicks.

The emission model is:

$$
Y_t
\sim
p
\left(
Y_t
\mid
S_t
\right)
$$

- genuine state deterioration;
- missing events;

- channel migration.

Separating state from measurement can prevent operational noise from being interpreted as customer behavior.

## 79. CRM dynamic intervention model

Suppose retention offer $A_t$ changes churn intensity.

A process model might define:

$$
r_t
=
r_0
\exp
\left(

\right)
$$

and:

$$
\frac{dC}{dt}
=
A_{\mathrm{new}}(t)

$$

It is a trajectory:

$$

-
C^{(0)}(t)
$$

Business outcomes can integrate the difference:

$$
\Delta \mathrm{CLV}
=
\int_0^T
m(t)
\left[
C^{(1)}(t)
-
C^{(0)}(t)
\right]
dt
$$

where $m(t)$ is expected margin.

```yaml
  inference:
    discrete_states: marginalized
```

The system should preserve the distinction between:

- posterior strategy probability;
- hard customer label.

For ODE models, numerical choices are part of the computational workflow.

```yaml
solver:
  type: rk45

  absolute_tolerance: 1e-8

  active_customers:
```

## 87. Validation hierarchy

A bespoke model should be validated at several levels.

### Equation-level validation

Do the equations implement the intended mechanism?

### Prior predictive validation

Do plausible parameters generate plausible systems?

### Simulation recovery

Can the inference recover known parameters from synthetic data?

### Posterior predictive validation

Does the model reproduce observed data patterns?

### Latent-state validation

When external measurements exist, do inferred hidden states agree?

### Intervention validation

When randomized interventions exist, does the structural model predict their effects?

These validations are more informative than a single predictive score.

---

## 88. Simulation-based model development

Before fitting real data:

1. choose plausible parameters;
2. simulate latent process;
3. simulate measurements;
4. fit the model to the synthetic data;
5. inspect parameter and estimand recovery.

For a dynamic CRM model:

$$
\theta_{\mathrm{true}}
\rightarrow
Z_{\mathrm{true}}(t)
\rightarrow
Y_{\mathrm{sim}}(t)
\rightarrow
p(\theta\mid Y_{\mathrm{sim}})
$$

If the model cannot recover its own simulated parameters, real-data interpretation is unsafe.

---

## 89. Causal interventions require structural invariance

A model estimated observationally is useful for intervention only if the structural equations remain valid under intervention.

For example:

$$
r_t
=
r_0
\exp(-\beta A_t)
$$

assumes the campaign changes churn through this mechanism.

If the campaign also changes:

- acquisition;
- support contacts;
- measurement;
- future campaign eligibility,

the intervention model must include these pathways.

Mechanistic modeling makes invariance assumptions explicit.

---

## 90. When a GLM is still the right tool

A GLM remains attractive when:

- the estimand is local and well identified;
- the observation support is naturally modeled by a standard likelihood;
- scientific mechanism is weak or unavailable;
- flexible prediction is sufficient;
- intervention does not require long-range extrapolation;
- the GLM approximates the relevant structural relation well.

The point is:

$$
\text{GLM by choice}
$$

not:

$$
\text{GLM by reflex}
$$

# Part VII — Common errors

## 91. Common errors exposed by B09

### Error 1: Treating a regression equation as a causal mechanism

An additive predictor is often a descriptive approximation.

### Error 2: Adding flexible terms before asking why the model fails

A mechanistic failure can identify missing science.

### Error 3: Estimating parameters that theory already fixes

The cylinder model predicts the exponent $3$.

### Error 4: Ignoring structural non-identifiability

$k$ and $p$ cannot be separately learned from $kp^2$ alone.

### Error 5: Claiming informative priors make the likelihood identify parameters

They add information; they do not alter likelihood structure.

### Error 6: Equating observed behavior with latent strategy

Different strategies can generate the same action.

### Error 7: Hard-classifying latent states when the posterior is uncertain

Mixture inference should preserve probabilities.

### Error 8: Sampling discrete latent states directly with HMC

They should generally be marginalized.

### Error 9: Ignoring experimental conditions that distinguish strategies

Identifiability is often a design property.

### Error 10: Treating noisy observations as the dynamic state itself

Pelts are measurements of populations.

### Error 11: Letting observed lagged values cause future observations

Latent states usually carry the causal dynamics.

### Error 12: Adding deeper lags instead of modeling delayed mechanisms

Predictive epicycles are not mechanistic explanations.

### Error 13: Ignoring the observation process

Measurement parameters can be as important as state equations.

### Error 14: Reading nonlinear dynamic parameters one at a time

Their scientific implications often emerge only jointly through simulation.

### Error 15: Skipping prior trajectory simulation

Nonlinear systems can imply pathological behavior under apparently mild priors.

### Error 16: Assuming a mechanistic model must be literally true

Useful models are deliberate simplifications.

### Error 17: Assuming a mechanistic model is causal merely because parameters have names

Identification and invariance still require justification.

### Error 18: Comparing only observed-level fit

Latent process behavior must also be inspected.

### Error 19: Ignoring numerical solver sensitivity

Computational approximation is part of the fitted model workflow.

### Error 20: Treating one successful case study as a universal template

Bespoke modeling means adapting structure to the scientific problem.

# Part VIII — Lecture takeaways

## 92. Core takeaways

1. Generalized linear models are powerful approximations, not universal scientific laws.
2. A causal theory can often provide stronger structure than an additive linear predictor.
3. Scientific models should generate the observables before being inverted with Bayes.
4. The observation process should be separated from the latent scientific process.
5. Geometric reasoning implies $W\propto H^3$ in the cylinder model.
6. Theory can fix parameters that a generic regression would estimate.
7. Mechanistic parameters often make prior information easier to articulate.
8. Mechanistic models can contain structural non-identifiability.
9. Prediction can remain identified when individual parameters are not.
10. Priors can resolve posterior computation without creating likelihood identification.
11. Model misfit is valuable when parameters and equations have scientific meanings.
12. Hidden behavior requires latent-state or mixture models when actions are compatible with several strategies.
13. A strategy mixture lives on a simplex.
14. Discrete latent states can be marginalized with `log_sum_exp`.
15. Experimental design is central to distinguishing hidden strategies.
16. State-space models separate hidden state from observed emission.
17. The Panda-nut example derives a nonlinear count rate from developmental theory.
18. Exposure remains meaningful even outside a standard log-linear Poisson GLM.
19. Predator–prey dynamics are naturally represented by coupled ODEs.
20. Feedback becomes acyclic when represented through time.
21. Numerical integration allows direct Bayesian estimation of dynamic scientific models.
22. Pelt counts are noisy measurements of latent populations.
23. Autoregressive lags can be predictive while lacking a sensible causal interpretation.
24. Posterior process trajectories and posterior predictive observations answer different questions.
25. Prior predictive simulation should inspect complete curves and trajectories.
26. Structural causal models naturally support counterfactual trajectory simulation.
27. CRM systems often contain latent states, mixtures, flows, measurement processes, and feedback that ordinary GLMs obscure.
28. CausalOps should represent process equations, measurement models, identifiability, solvers, and interventions as first-class artifacts.
29. A GLM should be used because its approximation is appropriate, not because it is the only available template.
30. Chapter 16's broader message is to make scientific expertise operational inside the model.

## 98. `19_gen_lin_madness.r`: Boxes strategy model

- loads raw Stan code `Boxes_model`;

- animates posterior draws of the complete simplex.

$$

# Part XII — Practical CausalOps checklist

## 102. Before choosing a GLM

Ask:

1. Is there a known conservation law, stock-flow identity, geometric constraint, or state transition?
2. Does the scientific theory imply a fixed exponent or coefficient?
3. Are important states latent?
4. Is the observed variable a noisy measurement of another quantity?
5. Does feedback operate over time?
6. Are there latent behavioral strategies?
7. Does the intervention change a process rate rather than the outcome directly?
8. Is extrapolation outside the observed support required?

A “yes” answer to several of these questions should trigger consideration of a bespoke structural model.

---

## 103. Before fitting a bespoke model

Verify:

- dimensional consistency;
- parameter domains;
- initial-state definitions;
- observation process;
- identifiability;
- prior predictive trajectories;
- numerical stability;
- intervention semantics;
- target estimands;
- synthetic-data recovery.

Only then run full MCMC.

---

## 104. After fitting

Check:

- chain diagnostics;
- posterior parameter correlations;
- posterior process trajectories;
- posterior predictive observations;
- latent-state plausibility;
- prior-to-posterior learning;
- sensitivity to priors;
- sensitivity to solver settings;
- sensitivity to alternative mechanistic structures.

The final report should distinguish:

$$
\text{what the data learned}
$$

from:

$$
\text{what the mechanism assumed}
$$

and:

$$
\text{what the priors supplied}
$$
