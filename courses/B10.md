## 1. Main thesis

The central idea is:

> **Observed absence is not the same thing as latent absence. A Hidden Markov Model separates the hidden process from the imperfect observations and uses temporal structure to infer the hidden state.**

The basic causal-generative structure is:

$$ Z_t \rightarrow Y_t $$

and:

$$ Z_t \rightarrow Z_{t+1} $$

where:

- $Z_t$ is the hidden state;
- $Y_t$ is the observed emission.

Across time:

$$ Z_1 \rightarrow Z_2 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow Z_T $$

with one observation layer at each time:

$$ Z_t \rightarrow Y_t $$

The inferential target is not just a regression coefficient. It is a posterior over:

$$ p \left( Z_{1:T},\theta \mid Y_{1:T} \right) $$

where $\theta$ contains transition and emission parameters.

# Part I — HMM anatomy

## 3. Three probability components

A basic HMM contains three components.

### Initial-state distribution

$$ p(Z_1) $$

### Transition model

$$ p \left( Z_t \mid Z_{t-1} \right) $$

### Emission model

$$ p \left( Y_t \mid Z_t \right) $$

These three pieces define the complete generative story.

---

## 4. Markov assumption

The first-order Markov assumption is:

$$ p \left( Z_t \mid Z_{1:t-1} \right) = p \left( Z_t \mid Z_{t-1} \right) $$

Once the previous state is known, earlier states do not directly alter the distribution of the next state.

This does **not** imply that observations are independent through time. Dependence among observations is transmitted through the hidden state.

---

## 5. Conditional independence of emissions

The standard HMM assumes:

$$ p \left( Y_t \mid Z_{1:T},Y_{1:t-1} \right) = p \left( Y_t \mid Z_t \right) $$

Once current hidden state $Z_t$ is known, the current observation does not depend directly on earlier observations or earlier hidden states.

This is an assumption about the measurement process.

---

## 6. Joint distribution

The full joint model is:

$$ p \left( Z_{1:T},Y_{1:T} \right) = p(Z_1) p(Y_1\mid Z_1) \prod_{t=2}^{T} p(Z_t\mid Z_{t-1}) p(Y_t\mid Z_t) $$

This factorization is the key to efficient inference.

---

## 7. HMM versus mixture model

B09's Boxes model has a hidden strategy:

$$ S_i \rightarrow Y_i $$

and marginal likelihood:

$$ p(Y_i) = \sum_s p(S_i=s)p(Y_i\mid S_i=s) $$

An HMM adds:

$$ S_t \rightarrow S_{t+1} $$

so the hidden states are temporally dependent.

A useful conceptual identity is:

$$ \text{finite mixture} + \text{Markov transitions} = \text{HMM} $$

---

## 8. HMM versus Markov chain

A Markov chain models transitions among states that are themselves observed or conceptually available:

$$ Z_t \rightarrow Z_{t+1} $$

An HMM adds an imperfect observation process:

$$ Z_t \rightarrow Y_t $$

The state must therefore be inferred indirectly.

---

## 9. HMM versus general state-space model

A state-space model contains:

$$ \text{latent process} + \text{observation process} $$

The latent state may be continuous or discrete.

An HMM is the discrete finite-state special case:

$$ \text{HMM} \subset \text{state-space models} $$

The lynx–hare model in B09 is another state-space model, but its latent population state is continuous rather than categorical.

# Part II — Official alive/dead example

## 10. Hidden states

The official script sets:

$$ K=2 $$

with:

$$ Z_t=1 \quad \text{alive} $$

and:

$$ Z_t=2 \quad \text{dead} $$

The data contain only a binary observation:

$$ Y_t\in\{0,1\} $$

where $Y_t=1$ means the individual was seen publicly that day.

---

## 11. Mortality parameter

The model estimates:

$$ \phi = \Pr \left( Z_{t+1}= \text{dead} \mid Z_t= \text{alive} \right) $$

Thus:

$$ 1-\phi $$

is one-interval survival probability.

The time unit is part of the parameter definition. In the first example, $\phi$ is daily mortality. In the capture–recapture extension, it becomes annual mortality.

---

## 12. Transition matrix

The official transition matrix is:

$$ \mathbf{\Gamma} = \begin{bmatrix} 1-\phi & \phi \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Rows represent the current state and columns the next state.

Therefore:

$$ \Gamma_{11}=1-\phi $$

is survival,

$$ \Gamma_{12}=\phi $$

is death,

$$ \Gamma_{21}=0 $$

forbids resurrection, and:

$$ \Gamma_{22}=1 $$

makes death absorbing.

---

## 13. Absorbing state

An absorbing state satisfies:

$$ \Pr \left( Z_{t+1}=k \mid Z_t=k \right)=1 $$

Death is structurally absorbing in the official model.

This is not estimated. It is scientific structure imposed before seeing the data.

Structural zeros like:

$$ \Gamma_{21}=0 $$

can be extremely informative because they rule out impossible latent paths.

---

## 14. Initial state

The script fixes:

$$ \boldsymbol{\rho} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Therefore:

$$ \Pr(Z_1=\text{alive})=1 $$

The individual is known alive at the start.

In capture–recapture language, the analysis is conditioned on a known-live initial occasion.

---

## 15. Emission model

The observation equation is:

$$ Y_t \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli} \left( \theta_{Z_t} \right) $$

where:

$$ \theta_1 = \Pr(Y_t=1\mid Z_t=\text{alive}) $$

is detection probability, while:

$$ \theta_2 = \Pr(Y_t=1\mid Z_t=\text{dead}) $$

is a false-positive detection probability.

---

## 16. The key ambiguity

A zero observation:

$$ Y_t=0 $$

is compatible with:

- alive but not seen;
- dead.

Therefore:

$$ Y_t=0 \not\Rightarrow Z_t=\text{dead} $$

This is the central statistical reason for the HMM.

A run of absences increases the probability of death, but does not deterministically prove it.

---

## 17. Detection priors

The script uses:

$$ \theta_1 \sim \operatorname{Beta}(1,4) $$

and:

$$ \theta_2 \sim \operatorname{Beta}(1,100) $$

The second prior strongly favors a false-positive probability near zero.

The script does not set $\theta_2$ exactly to zero. Therefore a positive observation after a candidate death state is extremely unlikely but not mathematically impossible.

This distinction matters when observation error or false reports are scientifically plausible.

---

## 18. Mortality prior

For the daily example:

$$ \phi \sim \operatorname{Beta}(1,50) $$

with prior mean:

$$ \mathbb{E}[\phi] = \frac{1}{51} $$

The prior favors small daily mortality.

The correct interpretation is not merely “small probability.” It is a prior over plausible survival durations.

---

# Part III — Forward algorithm

## 19. State-specific observation likelihoods

The script computes:

$$ \log \omega_{k,t} = \log p(Y_t\mid Z_t=k) $$

For Bernoulli emissions:

$$ \log \omega_{k,t} = \log \operatorname{Bernoulli} \left( Y_t \mid \theta_k \right) $$

This creates a $K\times T$ matrix describing how compatible each observation is with each possible hidden state.

---

## 20. Why brute-force enumeration fails

With $K$ states across $T$ times, there are:

$$ K^T $$

possible hidden-state paths.

For two states and sixty observations:

$$ 2^{60} $$

paths are possible before exploiting structural constraints.

A direct sum over every path is computationally impractical.

The Markov structure allows dynamic programming.

---

## 21. Forward probability

Define:

$$ \alpha_t(k) = p(Y_{1:t},Z_t=k) $$

At the first observation:

$$ \alpha_1(k) = \rho_k p(Y_1\mid Z_1=k) $$

This combines the initial-state probability with the first emission likelihood.

---

## 22. Forward recursion

For $t\geq2$:

$$ \alpha_t(k) = p(Y_t\mid Z_t=k) \sum_{j=1}^{K} \alpha_{t-1}(j) \Gamma_{jk} $$

Interpretation:

1. start with probability mass in previous states;
2. propagate that mass through the transition matrix;
3. weight each destination state by its compatibility with the new observation.

The complete data likelihood is then:

$$ p(Y_{1:T}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_T(k) $$

---

## 23. Computational gain

The forward algorithm requires approximately:

$$ O(TK^2) $$

operations rather than enumerating:

$$ K^T $$

state paths.

This is why HMMs with long sequences remain computationally manageable.

---

## 24. Stan's `hmm_marginal`

The official code uses:

```stan
target += hmm_marginal(log_omega, gamma, rho);
```

Conceptually this adds:

$$ \log p \left( Y_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{\Gamma}, \boldsymbol{\rho}, \boldsymbol{\theta} \right) $$

to the target density.

The entire hidden-state sequence has been marginalized out.

---

## 25. Why discrete states are marginalized

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo requires differentiable continuous parameter spaces.

The hidden variables:

$$ Z_t\in\{1,2, ldots,K\} $$

are discrete.

Stan therefore samples continuous parameters such as:

$$ \phi, \theta_1, \theta_2 $$

while integrating over $Z_{1:T}$ analytically through the forward algorithm.

This is exactly the general discrete-marginalization principle introduced in Chapter 15.

---

# Part IV — Reconstructing hidden states

## 26. Filtering

Filtering asks:

$$ p(Z_t\mid Y_{1:t}) $$

Only observations available up to time $t$ are used.

This is the relevant quantity for a live operational system.

Example:

> Based only on evidence observed up to today, what is the probability the individual is dead today?

---

## 27. Prediction

One-step state prediction is:

$$ p(Z_{t+1}\mid Y_{1:t}) $$

and follows from:

$$ p(Z_{t+1}=k\mid Y_{1:t}) = \sum_j p(Z_t=j\mid Y_{1:t}) \Gamma_{jk} $$

The state posterior is propagated through the transition matrix.

---

## 28. Smoothing

Smoothing asks:

$$ p(Z_t\mid Y_{1:T}) $$

using the complete sequence, including observations after time $t$.

A future reappearance can therefore revise our belief about an earlier run of zeros.

This backward information flow is one of the most useful properties of latent-state models.

---

## 29. `hmm_hidden_state_prob`

The generated-quantities block uses:

```stan
hidden_probs =
    hmm_hidden_state_prob(log_omega, gamma, rho);
```

The R code extracts the posterior dead-state probability over days and plots posterior means and intervals.

The result is a probabilistic reconstruction:

$$ p(Z_t=\text{dead}\mid Y_{1:T}) $$

rather than a single hard death date.

---

## 30. `hmm_latent_rng`

The script also uses:

```stan
latent_states =
    hmm_latent_rng(log_omega, gamma, rho);
```

This produces a complete plausible latent-state path conditional on the observation sequence and the current posterior parameter draw.

Across posterior draws, these paths approximate uncertainty over complete hidden histories.

One sampled path should never be mistaken for “the” true path.

---

## 31. Reappearance after zeros

Suppose observations are:

$$ 1,1,0,0,0,1 $$

The final sighting provides strong evidence that the individual was alive during the earlier zero run, because the model forbids dead-to-alive transition:

$$ \Gamma_{21}=0 $$

Thus future evidence changes posterior beliefs about previous states.

A simple row-by-row classifier cannot exploit this logic correctly.

---

## 32. Long zero runs

A run of $m$ zero observations while alive has probability roughly:

$$ (1-\theta_1)^m $$

conditional on survival throughout the run.

Death followed by continued zeros becomes more plausible as the run grows.

Therefore inference balances:

- mortality probability $\phi$;
- detection probability $\theta_1$;
- false positive probability $\theta_2$;
- run length;
- later reappearance.

---

# Part V — Survival interpretation

## 33. Constant discrete hazard

The basic model assumes constant interval mortality:

$$ \phi_t=\phi $$

If initially alive, survival across $m$ transitions is:

$$ S(m) = (1-\phi)^m $$

This is a discrete constant-hazard model.

---

## 34. Duration distribution

Under constant $\phi$, time to death has a geometric structure:

$$ \Pr(D=d) = (1-\phi)^{d-1}\phi $$

Thus a prior on $\phi$ is also a prior on how long the alive state persists.

This suggests a better prior predictive check:

- simulate lifetimes;
- simulate disappearance histories;
- inspect the implied duration distribution.

---

## 35. Time unit matters

If the same numerical $\phi$ is used for daily and annual observations, it represents radically different survival behavior.

Transition parameters must therefore store their interval definition explicitly:

$$ \text{probability per day} \neq \text{probability per year} $$

This is one reason the capture–recapture script changes the mortality prior.

---

# Part VI — Capture–recapture extension

## 36. Many individuals

The second official model contains:

$$ M $$

individuals observed across:

$$ N $$

years.

The data are:

$$ Y_{m,t} \in \{0,1\} $$

Each individual has an unobserved alive/dead history:

$$ Z_{m,1:N} $$

while mortality and detection parameters are shared.

---

## 37. Shared transition and emission parameters

The basic model assumes:

$$ \Pr \left( Z_{m,t+1}=\text{dead} \mid Z_{m,t}=\text{alive} \right) =\phi $$

for all individuals and years.

Detection is also shared:

$$ \Pr(Y_{m,t}=1\mid Z_{m,t}=k) =\theta_k $$

Many incomplete histories jointly inform the population parameters.

---

## 38. Capture–recapture logic

If an individual disappears for one year, that may indicate:

- mortality;
- missed detection.

If it reappears later, the earlier absence was almost certainly non-detection under the absorbing-death model.

If it never reappears, death becomes increasingly plausible.

The HMM formalizes this uncertainty instead of assigning death at last sighting.

---

## 39. Annual mortality prior

The multi-individual model uses:

$$ \phi \sim \operatorname{Beta}(1,10) $$

instead of the daily model's:

$$ \operatorname{Beta}(1,50) $$

This reflects the change in time scale.

The detection priors retain the same general structure.

---

## 40. Official simulation recovery

The script simulates:

$$ N=20 $$

years and:

$$ M=100 $$

individuals.

The data generator uses true annual mortality:

$$ \phi=0.05 $$

and alive-state detection probability:

$$ 0.5 $$

The posterior for $\phi$ is then compared with:

- the prior;
- the simulation truth.

This is a direct simulation-recovery test.

---

## 41. Individual likelihoods

Conditional on shared parameters, individual sequences are independent:

$$ p(Y_{1:M}\mid\theta) = \prod_{m=1}^{M} p(Y_m\mid\theta) $$

Therefore:

$$ \log p(Y_{1:M}\mid\theta) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \log p(Y_m\mid\theta) $$

The official Stan code calls `hmm_marginal` separately for each individual.

---

## 42. Detection–mortality ambiguity

If many individuals disappear, the data may support:

- higher mortality;
- lower detection;
- a mixture of both.

Thus posterior dependence between:

$$ \phi $$

and:

$$ \theta_1 $$

is scientifically expected.

More repeated histories and better detection help separate the processes.

---

# Part VII — General HMM extensions

## 43. More states

For $K>2$:

$$ Z_t \in \{1, ldots,K\} $$

and:

$$ \mathbf{\Gamma} = \left[ \gamma_{jk} \right] $$

with:

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{K} \gamma_{jk}=1 $$

for each current state $j$.

Each row of the transition matrix is a simplex.

---

## 44. Structural zeros

Scientific theory may forbid transitions.

For example:

$$ \gamma_{ mathrm{dead}, mathrm{alive}}=0 $$

In CRM, a “closed account” state might be absorbing only if reactivation is impossible under the business definition.

Structural constraints should be encoded in the transition graph, not patched after fitting.

---

## 45. Covariate-dependent transitions

Transition probabilities can depend on predictors.

For a binary transition:

$$ \operatorname{logit} \left( \phi_{it} \right) = \alpha + X_{it}^{\mathsf T}\beta $$

For multiple destination states, a multinomial-logit transition model can be used.

This embeds regression inside the state-transition mechanism rather than using regression as the entire scientific model.

---

## 46. State-dependent emissions

Emissions can be Bernoulli, Gaussian, Poisson, or more complex.

Examples:

$$ Y_t \sim \operatorname{Poisson} \left( \lambda_{Z_t} \right) $$

or:

$$ Y_t \sim \operatorname{Normal} \left( \mu_{Z_t},\sigma_{Z_t} \right) $$

Multiple observed channels can also be generated conditionally on the same hidden state.

---

## 47. Missing emissions

If $Y_t$ is missing but the missingness process is ignorable, the state transition still occurs.

That time point contributes no state-specific observation evidence, while probability is propagated through:

$$ \mathbf{\Gamma} $$

If missingness depends on state, missingness itself belongs in the observation model.

---

## 48. Irregular intervals

The basic script assumes equal observation intervals.

For varying interval $\Delta t$, constant per-step mortality is inappropriate.

A continuous-time hazard $\lambda$ gives:

$$ \phi(\Delta t) = 1- \exp(-\lambda\Delta t) $$

so the transition matrix changes with elapsed time.

This is an extension beyond the basic B10 script but follows directly from its time-scale assumptions.

---

## 49. Semi-Markov warning

Standard HMM persistence implies a geometric-like state-duration distribution.

If leaving a state depends on how long the system has already occupied it, the Markov assumption may fail.

Possible remedies include:

- richer states;
- explicit duration models;
- semi-Markov models.

This can matter for dormancy, disease stages, or equipment degradation.

---

# Part VIII — Identification

## 50. Label switching

If state labels are symmetric, exchanging labels can leave the likelihood unchanged.

The alive/dead model avoids this because:

- initial state is alive;
- dead is absorbing;
- resurrection is forbidden;
- dead-state detection is near zero.

The state labels therefore have scientific anchors.

---

## 51. Emissions must distinguish states

If:

$$ \theta_1=\theta_2 $$

then observations provide no direct information distinguishing alive and dead.

More generally, two states with identical:

- transition behavior;
- emission distributions

are observationally redundant.

Adding labels does not create information.

---

## 52. Transitions can identify states

Two states with similar emissions may still be identifiable if their persistence differs.

For example:

- state A is short-lived;
- state B is persistent.

Sequence structure can distinguish them even when one-time observations cannot.

This is why HMMs are fundamentally different from static clustering.

---

## 53. Simulation before real data

Before interpreting a custom HMM:

1. choose known transition parameters;
2. simulate hidden state paths;
3. simulate emissions;
4. fit the model;
5. assess transition recovery;
6. assess emission recovery;
7. assess state-probability calibration.

If the model cannot recover its own generated system, interpretation on real data is unsafe.

---

# Part IX — Model checking

## 54. Posterior predictive sequences

For posterior draw $s$:

1. simulate hidden states:

$$ Z_{1:T}^{ mathrm{rep},(s)} $$

2. simulate emissions:

$$ Y_t^{ mathrm{rep},(s)} \sim p \left( Y \mid Z_t^{ mathrm{rep},(s)},\theta^{(s)} \right) $$

Compare complete replicated sequences with the observed one.

---

## 55. Sequence-level checks

Useful summaries include:

- total number of detections;
- longest zero run;
- number of zero runs;
- time of last sighting;
- gap between sightings;
- inferred state occupancy;
- number of transitions;
- time to absorbing state.

Matching the marginal frequency of $Y=1$ is not sufficient.

Temporal structure is the central object.

---

## 56. Filtering versus smoothing in validation

A real-time system must be evaluated using information available at decision time:

$$ p(Z_t\mid Y_{1:t}) $$

A retrospective analysis can use:

$$ p(Z_t\mid Y_{1:T}) $$

Using smoothed probabilities to claim production performance leaks future information.

---

# Part X — Causal interpretation

## 57. An HMM is not automatically causal

Suppose treatment $A_t$ predicts transition:

$$ p \left( Z_{t+1} \mid Z_t,A_t \right) $$

This association is not automatically causal.

Treatment assignment may depend on:

- observed history;
- latent state;
- risk scores;
- clinician judgment;
- previous treatment.

The HMM solves hidden-state inference, not treatment confounding by itself.

---

## 58. Treatment on transitions

A treatment model might be:

$$ \operatorname{logit} \left[ \Pr \left( Z_{t+1}=\text{good} \mid Z_t=\text{bad},A_t \right) \right] = \alpha+\beta A_t $$

A dynamic estimand could be:

$$ \Pr \left( Z_t^{\pi_1}=\text{good} \right) - \Pr \left( Z_t^{\pi_0}=\text{good} \right) $$

under two intervention policies.

---

## 59. Treatment on emissions

An intervention can change observation without changing state.

For example, a reminder may increase app logging or survey response.

Then:

$$ A_t \rightarrow Y_t $$

through:

$$ \theta_{k,t} $$

while leaving state transitions unchanged.

If this pathway is omitted, better measurement can be misinterpreted as better latent state.

---

## 60. Hidden-state confounding

A latent state may influence both treatment assignment and future outcome:

$$ Z_t \rightarrow A_t $$

and:

$$ Z_t \rightarrow Y_{t+1} $$

Then $Z_t$ is itself a confounder.

Inferring $Z_t$ probabilistically can help, but causal identification still requires a defensible treatment-assignment model or randomized design.

---

# Part XI — CRM applications

## 61. Customer-state HMM

A useful CRM state space might be:

$$ Z_t \in \{ \text{engaged}, \text{dormant}, \text{at-risk}, \text{churned} \} $$

Observed emissions could include:

- purchase;
- login;
- click;
- support contact;
- payment;
- subscription event.

The hidden state summarizes persistent customer condition while observations are noisy manifestations of it.

---

## 62. “No activity” is not “churn”

A week with no recorded events may correspond to:

- active but unobserved;
- temporarily dormant;
- at risk;
- churned.

Therefore:

$$ \text{no event} \not\Rightarrow \text{churn} $$

This is exactly analogous to:

$$ \text{not seen} \not\Rightarrow \text{dead} $$

in the official example.

---

## 63. Churn need not be absorbing

If business rules allow reactivation:

$$ \Pr \left( \text{churned} \rightarrow \text{engaged} \right) >0 $$

Then churn should not be modeled as absorbing.

An absorbing churn state is appropriate only when it matches the estimand and business definition.

---

## 64. CRM transition matrix

For four states:

$$ \mathbf{\Gamma} = \begin{bmatrix} \gamma_{EE} & \gamma_{ED} & \gamma_{ER} & \gamma_{EC}\\ \gamma_{DE} & \gamma_{DD} & \gamma_{DR} & \gamma_{DC}\\ \gamma_{RE} & \gamma_{RD} & \gamma_{RR} & \gamma_{RC}\\ \gamma_{CE} & \gamma_{CD} & \gamma_{CR} & \gamma_{CC} \end{bmatrix} $$

Each row sums to one.

Scientific or operational constraints can remove implausible transitions.

---

## 65. Multiple emissions

For hidden state $k$:

$$ \Pr \left( \text{purchase}_t=1 \mid Z_t=k \right) =\theta^P_k $$

$$ \Pr \left( \text{click}_t=1 \mid Z_t=k \right) =\theta^E_k $$

and:

$$ \text{support count}_t \sim \operatorname{Poisson} \left( \lambda^S_k \right) $$

Several signals can distinguish states better than one binary emission.

---

## 66. Filtering for operational CRM

At decision time:

$$ p \left( Z_{i,t}=\text{at-risk} \mid Y_{i,1:t} \right) $$

can drive targeting.

This should remain a probability rather than being prematurely converted into a hard customer label.

A business rule may use:

$$ \Pr \left( Z_{i,t}=\text{at-risk} \right)>c $$

while preserving uncertainty downstream.

---

## 67. Smoothing for retrospective CRM

After a full observation period:

$$ p \left( Z_{i,t} \mid Y_{i,1:T} \right) $$

can reconstruct likely histories.

This supports questions such as:

- when did deterioration probably begin?
- how long was a customer dormant before churn?
- did support events precede state decline?
- which campaigns occurred during inferred at-risk periods?

Smoothing is retrospective; filtering is deployable in real time.

---

## 68. Campaign effects on transitions

For at-risk customer $i$:

$$ \operatorname{logit} \left[ \Pr \left( Z_{i,t+1}=\text{engaged} \mid Z_{i,t}=\text{at-risk},A_{i,t} \right) \right] = \alpha+\beta A_{i,t} $$

The campaign effect becomes a change in transition probability rather than a direct one-period conversion coefficient.

This is often a more natural causal representation of retention.

---

## 69. Offer levels

For offers:

$$ A_t \in \{0,5,10,20\} $$

transition dynamics can depend on offer amount:

$$ \eta_{jk,t} = \alpha_{jk} + f_{jk}(A_t) $$

Different hidden states can respond differently.

For example, dormant customers and deal-seeking customers need not have the same transition response to a €10 offer.

---

## 70. Observation-system changes

A tracking failure can reduce observed app activity without changing customer state.

The emission model may need to depend on:

- SDK version;
- consent status;
- device class;
- tracking outages.

Without this measurement layer, the HMM may infer artificial changes in customer state.

This is the direct connection to B07 measurement models.

# Part XIII — Validation strategy

## 77. Simulation-recovery loop

Generate:

$$ \mathbf{\Gamma}_{ mathrm{true}}, \boldsymbol{\theta}_{ mathrm{true}} $$

then:

$$ Z_{1:T}^{ mathrm{true}} $$

then:

$$ Y_{1:T}^{ mathrm{sim}} $$

Fit the model and test recovery of:

- transition probabilities;
- emission probabilities;
- state durations;
- posterior state probabilities;
- intervention estimands.

---

## 78. State probability scoring

Hard accuracy is useful but insufficient.

A probabilistic score can evaluate:

$$ \log p \left( Z_t^{ mathrm{true}} \mid Y \right) $$

averaged across time.

This rewards calibrated uncertainty rather than forcing a binary state decision.

---

## 79. Holdout designs

### Hold out future time

Tests forecasting for known individuals.

### Hold out individuals

Tests transport to new individuals.

### Hide selected emissions

Tests latent-state reconstruction.

The validation design must match the intended deployment task.

# Part XIV — Common errors

## 80. Common errors exposed by B10

1. Equating non-detection with absence of the hidden state.
2. Ignoring temporal transition structure.
3. Trying to sample discrete hidden states directly with HMC.
4. Enumerating all $K^T$ state sequences instead of using the forward algorithm.
5. Treating a single decoded path as certain.
6. Confusing filtering with smoothing.
7. Using future information in a real-time state score.
8. Adding hidden states without distinct emissions or transitions.
9. Ignoring label switching.
10. Ignoring the time scale of transition probabilities.
11. Fixing false detection to zero without scientific justification.
12. Allowing impossible transitions because software defaults to a full matrix.
13. Interpreting detection failure as mortality.
14. Inspecting only marginal observation frequencies instead of sequence structure.
15. Ignoring the duration distribution implied by self-transition probabilities.
16. Applying a fixed discrete-time transition matrix to irregular intervals.
17. Assuming HMM transition regressions are automatically causal.
18. Letting treatment-induced measurement changes masquerade as state changes.
19. Making churn absorbing when reactivation exists.
20. Defining states only after seeing fitted clusters rather than from a scientific or operational story.

# Part XV — Lecture takeaways

## 81. Core takeaways

1. An HMM is a discrete-state state-space model.
2. Hidden states evolve through a Markov transition mechanism.
3. Observations are imperfect emissions from hidden states.
4. The alive/dead example separates mortality from detection.
5. Death is an absorbing state in the official model.
6. The individual is known alive at the initial time.
7. A zero observation can mean either missed detection or death.
8. The emission model is therefore essential, not secondary.
9. HMC cannot directly sample unknown discrete state sequences.
10. The forward algorithm marginalizes them efficiently.
11. Stan's `hmm_marginal` implements the marginalized sequence likelihood.
12. Hidden-state probabilities preserve uncertainty over state at each time.
13. `hmm_latent_rng` produces plausible complete latent histories after fitting.
14. Filtering uses only information available through the current time.
15. Smoothing uses the complete sequence and can revise beliefs about the past.
16. Constant transition probabilities imply specific state-duration distributions.
17. Priors should be checked by simulating entire histories.
18. The capture–recapture extension shares mortality and detection information across individuals.
19. Many individual histories help separate non-detection from mortality.
20. Transition probabilities are inseparable from their time unit.
21. Structural zeros encode scientific knowledge directly.
22. More states do not automatically mean better inference.
23. HMMs can be extended with time-varying transitions and multiple emission channels.
24. An HMM solves hidden-state inference but not causal treatment assignment automatically.
25. A treatment may affect transitions, emissions, or both.
26. CRM customer states are a natural HMM application when event data imperfectly reveal latent engagement.
27. Production systems need filtered states, while retrospective analyses can use smoothed states.
28. The 2026 B10 material is substantially more developed than the brief HMM treatment in the second edition.

# Part XVIII — Official code guide

## 85. One-individual Stan model

The script declares:

```stan
int N;
int K;
array[N] int Y;
```

with continuous parameters:

```stan
real<lower=0,upper=1> phi;
vector<lower=0,upper=1>[K] theta;
```

It constructs:

```stan
gamma[1,1] = 1-phi;
gamma[1,2] = phi;
gamma[2,1] = 0;
gamma[2,2] = 1;
```

and fixes:

```stan
vector[K] rho = [1.0,0.0]';
```

The emission log likelihood is:

```stan
log_omega[k,n] =
  bernoulli_lpmf(Y[n] | theta[k]);
```

The sequence likelihood is:

```stan
target += hmm_marginal(log_omega, gamma, rho);
```

Generated quantities include posterior latent paths and hidden-state probabilities.

---

## 86. One-individual simulation

The script uses:

$$ N=60 $$

days.

The demonstration fixes a death around day 50 and simulates public appearances while alive with probability:

$$ 0.5 $$

The fitted model receives only the observed appearance sequence.

The true hidden state is retained only for simulation validation and plotting.

---

## 87. Capture–recapture Stan model

The second model changes the data structure to:

```stan
int N;
int M;
int K;
array[M,N] int Y;
```

For each individual $m$ it computes an emission matrix and adds:

```stan
target +=
  hmm_marginal(log_omega[m], gamma, rho);
```

The mortality probability and detection probabilities are shared across individuals.

---

## 88. Capture–recapture simulation

The official simulation uses:

$$ N=20, \qquad M=100 $$

with true annual mortality:

$$ \phi=0.05 $$

and simulated detection while alive of:

$$ 0.5 $$

The posterior mortality density is then compared with both the prior and the known simulation truth.

# Part XIX — CausalOps checklist

## 89. Before fitting an HMM

Confirm:

1. hidden states have scientific or operational definitions;
2. transition graph is explicit;
3. observation interval is defined;
4. initial state assumptions are explicit;
5. emissions are defined for every state;
6. false positives and false negatives are considered;
7. structural zeros are justified;
8. priors are checked through state durations and simulated sequences;
9. simulated data permit parameter recovery;
10. deployment uses filtering or smoothing appropriately.

---

## 90. After fitting

Inspect:

- transition probabilities;
- emission parameters;
- implied state durations;
- posterior state probabilities;
- sampled complete paths;
- sequence-level posterior predictions;
- state identifiability;
- prior sensitivity;
- transition-constraint sensitivity;
- treatment assignment assumptions if causal claims are made.

Do not stop at a coefficient table.

The primary scientific output is the inferred dynamic process.

# Part XX — Final synthesis

## 91. Progression from B09

B09 introduced:

$$ S_i \rightarrow Y_i $$

for hidden strategy and observed behavior.

B10 adds temporal dynamics:

$$ S_t \rightarrow S_{t+1} $$

The complete architecture becomes:

$$ \boxed{ \text{hidden dynamic state} + \text{transition mechanism} + \text{measurement model} + \text{Bayesian marginalization} } $$

This is the discrete-state counterpart of the continuous latent-process models developed in B09.

---

## 92. One-sentence summary

> **A Hidden Markov Model separates what the system actually is from what we happen to observe, then uses temporal transition structure and the complete pattern of observations to infer the hidden state without pretending that missing evidence is evidence of absence.**
